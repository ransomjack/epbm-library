This half covers the other four shortcuts the class discovered through its own experiments (availability, representativeness, the decoy and mental accounting) and then the price cues participants asked about and the lessons of the first case discussion.

## Availability: what comes to mind feels likely

Third experiment. In four pages of a novel (about 2,000 words) do you expect to find more than 20 seven-letter words of the form <strong>\_ \_ \_ \_ \_ n \_</strong> (n in the sixth position)? That was Questionnaire A. Questionnaire B asked about seven-letter words of the form <strong>\_ \_ \_ \_ i n g</strong> (ending in -ing). Group A said mostly no; Group B said mostly yes.

Mathematically, A must be at least as likely as B. Every seven-letter word ending in -ing has n in the sixth position, so the -ing words are a <strong>subset</strong> of the n-words: think of the Venn diagrams from school. Group A should have said yes more often, not less. Why the reverse? Because -ing words come to mind instantly (reading, talking, walking) while n-in-sixth-place words do not, even though every -ing word is one of them, along with others like "present".

<div class="callout">
<p class="callout-title">Availability (definition on the slide)</p>

Examples that are more easily available to us, easier to recall, carry a higher weight in our decisions and in how probable we judge an event to be.

</div>

### Availability in buyers' minds

<strong>One bad experience spreads across categories.</strong> Suppose you had trouble with an LG smartphone. Next time you shop for a washing machine, would you buy LG? Most people say no, though a washing machine and a smartphone share almost no technology. The bad memory is easily available, so it gets a high weight and colours unrelated decisions.

<strong>Recent, vivid events feel likely.</strong> A participant raised plane crashes. The professor recalled that the Air India crash on a London-bound flight happened a day before she was to fly to Dubai; suddenly the odds felt like 50:50, and relatives called to wish her luck. Another participant, then in Prague, said colleagues there were still talking about it. Driving is statistically far riskier, but the crash was vivid and recent.

### What brands must do when something goes wrong

Because failures are so easy to recall, how a brand handles a crisis decides how long the memory hurts it. The professor gave four examples.

| Situation | What happened | Lesson |
|---|---|---|
| Taj Mahal Palace, Mumbai, after the 26/11 terror attack (2008) | The hotel had to reopen and say "we are open" without sounding cold, and without replaying images of the hotel in flames. It used home as a metaphor: come back, come home, feel safe. | Address the fear people can easily recall, with care. |
| Malaysia Airlines MH370 (2014), which went missing | The airline went silent at first, then spoke under media pressure, with its messages contradicting officials'. Trust collapsed. Months later a second Malaysia Airlines plane, MH17, was shot down over Ukraine (not the airline's fault) and the airline was pushed towards bankruptcy. | Silence lets customers write their own story. |
| AirAsia crash (late 2014) | CEO Tony Fernandes became the face of the response, met families, arranged their stay at the site at the airline's cost and was available around the clock. The business was far less damaged. | Communicate early, personally and consistently. |
| A product failure (the LG example) | Research suggests a brand rated 3 out of 5 that drops to 1 after a failure can recover to 4, higher than before, if the failure is handled well. | Good after-sales service can turn a failure into loyalty. |

> Even if you feel you are not at fault, start communicating. Otherwise customers will come up with their own narratives.
>
> <small>The professor on crisis communication, paraphrased</small>

### Availability and advertising

The everyday application is recall. In <strong>impulse categories</strong> (chocolates, soft drinks, chips) customers do not deliberate at the counter; they pick what comes to mind. Jingles, taglines and repeated advertising exist to make the brand the most available option at the point of purchase.

<div class="callout beyond">
<p class="callout-title">Good to know: two dates checked</p>

The professor recalled the Taj attack as 2006; it took place on 26 November 2008 ("26/11"). The 2014 AirAsia flight, QZ8501, was flying from Surabaya in Indonesia to Singapore when it crashed into the Java Sea; it was not bound for Beijing (MH370 was the Beijing-bound flight).

</div>

<strong>On a manager's desk.</strong> Assume customers remember your worst moment best. A product company should treat after-sales service as brand-building, because one failure handled well can lift the brand above where it was. A service provider facing an incident (an outage, a missed alert) should communicate first, openly and through a senior face, before the client's own narrative sets in.

## Representativeness: judging by the stereotype

Fourth experiment, open to everyone. <strong>Linda</strong> is 31, single, outspoken and very bright. She majored in philosophy. As a student she was deeply concerned with discrimination and social justice, and took part in anti-nuclear demonstrations. Rank six descriptions by how likely they are: (a) elementary school teacher; (b) active in a feminist movement; (c) psychiatric social worker; (d) bank teller; (e) insurance salesperson; (f) bank teller who is active in a feminist movement.

The professor asked the class to focus on (d) and (f). Of about 25 responses, only 5 ranked (d) above (f). Yet (f) can never be more likely than (d): every feminist bank teller is a bank teller, so (f) is a subset of (d). Linda must meet two conditions to be (f) and only one to be (d). People ranked (f) higher because it <strong>fits the description</strong> (the stereotype of Linda) and fit overrode probability.

<div class="callout">
<p class="callout-title">Representativeness (definition on the slide)</p>

Assigning a high probability when categorising a situation or item based on how well it fits our pre-existing prototypes, often stereotypes.

</div>

Everyday versions: you see someone in a white coat and assume a doctor. The professor said a new fast-food outlet was opening near the campus and asked what food the class imagined; the answers were rolls and chowmein: fried, unhealthy. It might serve healthy food, but "fast food" fits a prototype.

### Marketing implications

| Application | How it works | Example |
|---|---|---|
| Celebrity endorsers | An endorser stands for certain attributes; you want them to rub off on the brand. | Choosing a star whose image matches what the brand wants to say. |
| Category cues | A strong brand can come to represent a category's attributes; others can borrow its visual cues. | Pears made a translucent soap stand for gentle, soft, pure. Any brand wanting to signal gentleness can use a translucent look and be read instantly. |
| Breaking a prototype | If a category leader defines what a product of that type looks like, a challenger that does not fit the prototype must spend heavily to be seen differently. | A soap that wants to be seen as mild and caring but does not look like the category's gentle prototype has to work much harder: discussed in the session's case. |

<div class="callout trap">
<p class="callout-title">Heuristics are not positioning</p>

A participant asked which heuristic brand positioning falls under. The answer: none. Heuristics describe how customers make decisions; positioning is a strategic choice the firm makes (Session 0, STP). Marketers can use their knowledge of heuristics to support a positioning (for example, by borrowing a category cue) but the two are different things.

</div>

<strong>On a manager's desk.</strong> Customers judge you by what you resemble. A product company entering a category should decide whether to look like the prototype (instant understanding) or deliberately unlike it (harder, costlier, but differentiating). A service firm is read the same way: a security provider whose reports, people and website look like an engineering firm's will be assumed to understand plants.

## The decoy: adding a dominated alternative

Fifth experiment: choosing a detergent. Questionnaire A offered two brands: <strong>X</strong> at $3.00 with a quality rating of 5, and <strong>Y</strong> at $3.80 with a quality rating of 6. Questionnaire B offered the same X and Y plus a third brand, <strong>Z</strong>, priced above X with the same quality rating of 5. Which would you buy?

Rationally, Z should change nothing: nobody should want it. One participant even argued, with good logic, that X should do better in A. But the share choosing X was <strong>higher in B</strong>. Why? Z is clearly worse than X (dearer for the same quality) but it cannot be directly compared with Y. Its presence gives X an easy win. Shoppers who were torn between X and Y, the <strong>fence-sitters</strong>, now have a reason to settle on X.

<div class="callout">
<p class="callout-title">Adding a dominated alternative (the slide)</p>

The tendency to prefer X over Y can be increased by adding a third alternative that is clearly inferior to X but not to Y. The third option is a decoy, which is why this is also called the <strong>decoy effect</strong>.

</div>

A marketer does not want to sell the decoy. To push X, create something clearly inferior to X. To push Y instead, create a decoy dominated by Y: for example, the same price as Y with lower quality, or a higher price with the same quality.

### Popcorn pricing

Earlier, during anchoring, a participant had raised multiplex popcorn. The professor came back to it here. A small tub might cost around Rs. 600, a medium around Rs. 750 and a large around Rs. 800. The medium is the decoy: it costs almost as much as the large but gives much less. The raw material costs almost nothing, so every extra rupee is profit, and the cinema wants you to buy the large tub. The same logic applied to the professor's own experience of a medium Coke priced close to an unlimited one. There is some anchoring here too, but the sharper label is the decoy: one option exists only to make another look good.

### The Economist's subscription

The Economist magazine once offered a web subscription for $59 and web-plus-print for $125. The company earned more when readers chose web-plus-print, but about 80% chose web only. How would you use a decoy? Participants proposed changing the prices; one proposed adding a third option: a print-only subscription. That is what the magazine did: <strong>print only, at $125</strong>, the same price as web-plus-print. Nobody wants print alone at that price, but it makes web-plus-print look like a free gift of the web edition. In the class's telling, the split shifted to about 70% choosing web-plus-print.

| Option | Before | After adding the decoy |
|---|---|---|
| Web only: $59 | About 80% chose this | About 30% |
| Print only: $125 | Not offered | The decoy: almost nobody |
| Web + print: $125 | About 20% | About 70% |

The professor gave two more examples. An optician's chain lays out lens options so that one is clearly better value than its neighbour. And when the iPhone 14 range launched in India, in the professor's account, the Pro at about Rs. 1.3 lakh made the Pro Max at about Rs. 1.4 lakh look like a small step up, and the Pro Max sold strongly.

Does the decoy ever fail? Companies would not announce it if it did, and nothing works every time. Two participants chose Z in the experiment. One simply went for the middle option: the <strong>Goldilocks effect</strong>: not the cheapest, not the dearest, the one in between. Others ignore the stated quality and infer that the dearer product must be better, because price is such a strong cue to quality.

<strong>On a manager's desk.</strong> Design your range, not just your products. A product company with two variants should ask which one it really wants to sell and whether a third, slightly worse option would make that one obvious. A service firm can do the same with tiers: a Standard tier, a Premium tier, and a middle tier priced close to Premium with clearly less, so that Premium reads as the sensible choice.

## Mental accounting

Last experiment, open to all. <strong>Situation A:</strong> you bought a concert ticket for Rs. 2,000. At the hall you find you have lost the ticket. Would you buy another? <strong>Situation B:</strong> you are going to the concert to buy a ticket at the door. At the hall you find you have lost a Rs. 2,000 note. Would you still buy the ticket? (Assume you have travelled to the venue in both cases and have enough money in your wallet.)

Many said no in A; almost everyone said yes in B. Yet the money is identical: in both cases, watching the concert now costs you Rs. 4,000 in total. One participant explained the difference exactly: in A the ticket was already paid for, so buying another feels like paying twice for the concert; in B the lost money doesn't feel connected to the concert at all.

<div class="callout">
<p class="callout-title">Mental accounting</p>

People keep separate mental accounts for different purposes. Losing the ticket drains the "concert" account, so a second ticket feels like spending Rs. 4,000 on one concert: over budget. Losing cash drains a general account, so the concert budget is untouched. Same money, different accounts, different decision. As the slide notes, people typically say yes more in Situation B.

</div>

The professor played a short clip in which a friend of the actor Dustin Hoffman recalls lending him money in their struggling days. At Hoffman's flat he found a row of jars labelled food, entertainment, books and so on. All had money except the one marked food, and Hoffman would not take money from the other jars to buy food. That is mental accounting in its purest form.

### Where marketers use it: managing waiting time

The slide asked how you would reduce the waiting time at an airport baggage claim. The professor's example was Houston airport. Passengers complained about waiting around 8 minutes for their bags at the belt. First the airport added baggage handlers and improved service at the belt; the complaints did not fall. Then it moved the arrival gates farther from the belt. Passengers now walked for about 6 minutes and waited about 2. The total time was the same, but complaints dropped sharply.

Time, like money, is booked into accounts: occupied time is not felt as waiting time. Retailers use the same idea (TV screens, magazines, impulse products near the queue, mirrors in clothing stores) anything that keeps the customer occupied.

<strong>On a manager's desk.</strong> Customers judge the account, not the total. A product company can bundle (one price for the phone, case and warranty feels smaller than three separate payments) or separate charges deliberately to match how buyers budget. A service firm should fill waits: an onboarding portal with progress updates during a three-week deployment makes the same three weeks feel shorter than silence.

## More price cues the class asked about

Participants kept pulling the discussion towards pricing, and the professor answered several questions that go beyond the five heuristics on the slides. They are worth knowing as separate effects, and she promised a pricing session later if time allowed.

| Effect | What it says | Example from class |
|---|---|---|
| Left-digit effect | The leftmost digit has the most influence on how a price is read. | Rs. 999 reads as "nine hundred-something", Rs. 1,000 as "a thousand". |
| Prices ending in 9 | A 9-ending signals a deal or discount. | An apparel experiment: cutting a price from $39 to $34 <strong>reduced</strong> sales (by about 25%, as the professor recalled) because $39 looked like an offer. |
| Prices ending in 0 | A round price signals quality. | A premium brand that does not want a discount image may prefer Rs. 5,000 to Rs. 4,999. |
| Veblen (snob, prestige) effect | For some luxury goods, demand rises as price rises, because exclusivity is the point. | Called the Veblen effect after the economist who described it, and sometimes the Chivas Regal effect. |
| Price as a quality cue | When unsure, buyers infer quality from price. | Some US universities raised tuition as their rankings slipped, to signal quality. A participant asked if EPBM fees worked the same way; the professor smiled that it was a great question. |
| Sensitivity to price vs quantity | Buyers react more to a price change than to a quantity change. | Shrinkflation: firms hold the price and shrink the pack instead. |
| Price thresholds | Demand can drop sharply when a price crosses a familiar point. | Rs. 10 and Rs. 20 packs: moving from Rs. 10 to Rs. 11 can lose far more sales than the rupee suggests. This is why FMCG firms prefer to adjust grams rather than cross the threshold, and why they resist fixed standard pack sizes. |
| Scarcity | Limited time, limited stock or limited editions create urgency (FOMO). | "Last chance", "only 2 left". |

<div class="callout">
<p class="callout-title">A note on luxury brands</p>

A participant argued that brands like Louis Vuitton never use anchors, discounts or bonus packs. Fair: these tactics make a price look good, which is not what a luxury brand wants. Luxury works on the snob or Veblen effect instead. Even that holds only within a range of prices (beyond a point, demand will fall) so firms should test their own sweet spot rather than assume.

</div>

> These findings come from experiments. You may want to run your own to see what works in your category.
>
> <small>The professor, paraphrased</small>

<strong>On a manager's desk.</strong> Every price ending sends a signal: choose it on purpose. A mass-market product can use Rs. 99 and bonus packs; a premium product should consider round numbers and no visible discounting. A professional-services firm quoting Rs. 9,99,000 instead of Rs. 10,00,000 looks like a discounter; a round fee says "this is what our expertise costs".

## Lessons from the first case discussion

The last part of the class opened the module's first case: a premium soap, launched in a new region, that was falling far short of its market-share target. The discussion continued into the next session. The case itself is not retold here. What matters are the lessons the professor drew about analysing any case: lessons that apply in every subject.

### The method, again

Asked how to approach a case, participants were reminded of the method from Session 0 ([How to analyse a case](/marketing-management/the-marketing-plan/#how-to-analyse-a-case)). Find the dilemma: read the opening and closing paragraphs closely, since case writers state the issue at the start and close the loop at the end. Break the main problem into sub-problems. Identify the options. Develop the pros and cons of each. Recommend, and support the recommendation with the case's data. In this case the options were clearly laid out (broadly: withdraw the brand, make small changes to the strategy, or make major changes including to the product). Often they are not, and you have to find them.

<div class="callout">
<p class="callout-title">What the evaluator is looking for</p>

"We are least interested in the answer." The professor was explicit: you are judged on whether you developed the pros and cons of each option, not on which option you picked. Any option can score well if it is well argued. What cannot score well is a recommendation that goes against the data in the case.

</div>

### Five lessons from the discussion

| Lesson | What happened in class | The principle |
|---|---|---|
| Research where you sell | The market research had been done in the company's head-office city, not in the region where the product was launched. Why? A participant guessed it right: convenience. | Firms often choose what is easy to implement; that convenience introduces bias. Research must represent the market you are entering. |
| A number needs a reference point | A participant called one-third of respondents willing to buy "a bad number". The professor asked: bad compared to what? | Judge a number against a benchmark (a target, a competitor, a category norm) never in isolation. |
| Read the exhibit correctly | One participant read a row in a research exhibit as the weakest result, when its percentage jump between non-users and users was actually the largest. | Know what each column means (total sample vs triers), and distinguish absolute levels from changes. |
| Check the unit | A participant treated a figure for households as if it were population. | Households, people, units and rupees are not interchangeable. Check the unit before you multiply. |
| "Do more research" is not a recommendation | One proposal was to start over with fresh research. | Use the research you have. A recommendation must say what to change: across all 4Ps. |

The professor's homework for the next session was to go back through every exhibit and test the case against all four Ps: is the price right, is the product right, is the advertising right, is the distribution right? If you recommend small changes, name them; if major, name those.

<strong>On a manager's desk.</strong> These are the same disciplines a manager needs with any research report. Before acting on a product survey, ask where it was run and whether that matches the market. Before acting on a service satisfaction score, ask what it should be compared with. And when a team recommends "more research", ask what decision the existing research already supports.

## The session on a manager's desk

| Heuristic or effect | In a product business | In a service business |
|---|---|---|
| Anchoring | Struck-through MRP; premium models at the store entrance | Table your fully scoped price first; state a turnaround you can beat |

<div class="callout">
<p class="callout-title">The session in one breath</p>


Economists assume buyers are rational and predictable. Psychologists say they are subjective and unpredictable. Research shows something in between: buyers are boundedly rational, limited in ability, time and information. So they use heuristics (mental shortcuts) and settle for good-enough, or satisficing, choices. That creates predictable biases, which behavioural economics studies. Kahneman's 2002 Nobel prize put the field on the map.

Marketers can put six of these shortcuts to work:

- **Anchoring.** The first number sets the scale: strike-through prices, store placement, Disney's queues, the $1,000 sundae.
- **Framing.** A gain beats a loss: "survive" rather than "die". "33% more" beats "33% off", even though 33% more is only 25% off.
- **Availability.** What is easy to recall feels likely. So handle a crisis openly, and build recall for impulse buys.
- **Representativeness.** Fitting a stereotype beats probability: Linda, the white coat, the translucent soap.
- **The decoy.** An option clearly worse than X, but not clearly worse than Y, pushes buyers to X: the popcorn tubs, The Economist's subscriptions.
- **Mental accounting.** Money and time sit in separate mental accounts: the lost ticket, the Houston baggage walk.

As a marketer, use them. As a consumer, beware of them. Session 2 asks the step before influence: how do you understand customers in the first place?
