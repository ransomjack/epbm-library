A position, once taken, is hard to move. The second half of the session looked at brands that tried: why they did it, how, and what went wrong. It closed with a choice every launch faces: position for everyone, or for a few.

## Repositioning: what it is and why

<strong>Repositioning</strong> means trying to create a new image of the brand in customers' minds. A participant gave Tropicana's redesigned packaging as an example; the professor distinguished it: that was <strong>rebranding</strong> (changing the look, logo or name) not repositioning. Axe's move from seduction to "Find Your Magic" (Session 0) is repositioning.

Fair & Lovely's change of name to Glow & Lovely sits on the line. The brand may intend a new image, but it came in response to a backlash, and in many customers' minds the perception has not changed. One thing is what the brand is trying to do; another is how customers see it.

| Why reposition (from the slide and class) | Examples |
|---|---|
| Customers' needs have changed | <strong>Big Bazaar</strong> (value retail as shoppers grew more aspirational); <strong>Maggi</strong> (from two-minute convenience to health as tastes evolved); <strong>Fair & Lovely</strong> |
| To target a different segment or expand the customer base | <strong>Saffola</strong> (from heart patients to everyday families); <strong>Cadbury Dairy Milk</strong> (from children to adults and occasions) |
| The positioning went wrong | <strong>Tata Nano</strong> |
| Competition has crowded the position, or sales are falling because of the positioning | Axe, after many brands copied its theme (a participant's point) |

### Is it repositioning, or just growth?

Participants offered many candidates; the professor sorted them. Entering a new segment or adding products is not automatically repositioning. Ask what the brand stood for before, and whether its communication has changed.

| Candidate | The professor's view |
|---|---|
| Starbucks adding tea, sandwiches (and alcohol in some markets) | Growth, not repositioning. It still wants to be seen as a premium coffee outlet: otherwise its competitive set changes. It even chooses cold sandwiches that will not mask the smell of coffee. |
| Titan Eye+, Tanishq's Mia, Taj's Vivanta | Brand extensions or new brands to grow: the parent brand is not repositioning. |
| Maruti's Nexa | A strategy to grow into a new segment that also helps a slow repositioning towards premium: building on its past image, not breaking from it. |
| ITC moving from tobacco into FMCG and hotels | Repositioning at the <strong>company</strong> level, away from being seen as a tobacco company; Sunfeast and Aashirvaad themselves are new brands. |
| Food-delivery or listing apps adding services; a clothing brand adding kidswear | Not necessarily: if what the brand stands for applies across the new segments, it is expansion. |
| Jaguar's dramatic 2024 rebrand ahead of an all-electric line (a participant's example) | An attempt at a complete change of target and image. |

<strong>On a manager's desk.</strong> Before calling a move repositioning, ask whether you want customers to think something different about you. A product company adding a line may only need an extension. A service firm moving from "cheapest outsourced monitoring" to "trusted security partner" is repositioning, and needs the budget, patience and proof that implies.

## Repositioning: how, and what can go wrong

<Fig title="How to reposition" caption="The three questions on the class slide.">
  <Steps items={[
    { title: "Where are we?", body: "Find out what customers think of the brand now, relative to competitors." },
    { title: "Where to?", body: "Decide what you want customers to think of the brand." },
    { title: "How?", body: "Change the communication only (Cadbury Dairy Milk, Saffola), or change the product and communicate (Tata Nano, Fair & Lovely)." },
  ]} />
</Fig>

| Route | Example | What happened |
|---|---|---|
| Change the communication only | <strong>Cadbury Dairy Milk</strong> | Long positioned around children's celebrations, it changed its frame of reference to traditional Indian sweets ("kuch meetha ho jaaye") for adults and occasions, through messaging alone. |
| Change the communication only | <strong>Saffola</strong> | Its early ads targeted people vulnerable to heart disease: an elderly man in an ambulance, his wife waiting outside the operating theatre. Having built awareness, it widened to everyday healthy cooking for families. |
| Change the product and communicate | <strong>Tata Nano</strong> | Later ads showed young people and college students and stressed new features, but the image had stuck. |
| Change the product and communicate | <strong>Fair & Lovely</strong> (an earlier episode) | Research said customers wanted a multipurpose cream. The brand relaunched as multipurpose with new packaging and a higher price, and lost about 20% market share within two months. Over half its sales came from rural markets, where customers had not seen the new ads; seeing a changed pack, they assumed it was a fake. |
| Change the tagline and store | <strong>Big Bazaar</strong> | From a value promise ("isse sasta aur achha kahin nahin") to "Naye India ka Bazaar", with trendier brands and new layouts, then to "Making India Beautiful". Large investments; neither change worked. |
| Stretch with a new product | <strong>Kellogg's Special K</strong> | A brand for women trying to lose weight launched a healthy breakfast sandwich (sausage, egg and cheese). Customers could not connect it with weight loss, and it did not work. |

<div class="callout">
<p class="callout-title">What the examples teach</p>

<strong>Communication is a must.</strong> Unless customers are told, nobody knows, and every customer the brand relies on must hear it, including rural ones.

<strong>Usually communication alone is not enough.</strong> Most repositioning needs supporting changes to the product, channels or strategy (Maruti's Nexa).

<strong>It is expensive and slow</strong>, and moving far from what the brand stands for usually fails, as with the Phaeton and Special K.

</div>

<div class="callout trap">
<p class="callout-title">A new segment is not a new position</p>

Launching a product for a new group, or renaming a product line, is not repositioning unless you are changing what customers think the brand stands for. Brand extension (using the same name for a new product, like Titan Eye+) is a separate decision, covered in Session 5.

</div>

<strong>On a manager's desk.</strong> Plan a repositioning like a product launch: research where you are, decide where you want to be, change what has to change, and make sure every customer group hears about it. A product company that changes the pack without telling its rural buyers risks being mistaken for a counterfeit. A service firm that rebrands without changing its delivery will find clients judge it by the delivery.

## Lessons from the case: mass or niche?

The session's case was a new beverage from an Indian bottled-water company: a caffeine-free drink with vitamins, sitting somewhere between a soft drink and an energy drink. The case is not retold here; its lessons are about a choice every launch faces: position for the <strong>mass</strong> market, or for a <strong>narrower</strong> segment?

|   | Mass positioning | Niche positioning |
|---|---|---|
| Volume | High | Lower |
| Margin | Lower: mass prices are low | Higher: a narrow segment will pay more |
| Appeal | Weak: something for everyone becomes very appealing to no one | Strong: very appealing to a few |
| What the 4Ps must look like | Low price, wide variety and flavours, mass distribution, broad taste appeal | Price, product and channels built for that segment |

Applied to the case, the class found that the brand's intended mass position ("anyone, anytime, anywhere") was contradicted by almost everything else:

| Element | Mass needs... | What the brand did |
|---|---|---|
| Price | A low price | About Rs. 50: high for a mass drink |
| Variants | Many flavours and options | One variant |
| Name | A neutral, broad name | A name meaning energy, which signals a narrow energy-drink role |
| Communication | Clear reasons and occasions to drink | A launch ad that never made clear who should drink it, when or why |
| Distribution | Everywhere, and, for an energy drink, pubs, bars and gyms | Its existing water distribution, missing the channels where energy drinks sell |

Why not simply position it as an energy drink? Because the brand feared the limited volume of a small category, and because of <strong>representativeness</strong> (Session 1): one dominant brand defines what an energy drink is: caffeine, sugar, a particular taste. A caffeine-free drink does not fit that prototype, so customers would ask where the energy comes from. If the main source was sugar, the brand could not easily say so either.

<div class="callout">
<p class="callout-title">The professor's verdict</p>

The product concept was not bad. The positioning was confused, and the strategy was not aligned with any position. Whatever you choose, mass or niche, the price, variants, taste, name, communication and channels must follow it.

A narrower energy-drink position, stressing its real differences (vitamins, no caffeine), might have limited the segment but made the brand far more appealing within it.

Do more research on why buyers choose the category leader. If part of its appeal is symbolic (being seen with the can) a challenger could even price close to it and spend the margin on advertising. A small company with limited marketing money has less room to make errors and relaunch than a giant with deep pockets.

</div>

<strong>On a manager's desk.</strong> Choose mass or niche deliberately, then make every P agree. A product company that names its drink "energy" but prices and distributes it as a soft drink has positioned it nowhere. A service firm that claims to serve everyone, with one package and one price, should ask whether a sharper niche would win more of the clients it actually wants.

## The session on a manager's desk

| Idea | In a product business | In a service business |
|---|---|---|
| Value-based price differences | Two names, two packs, two shelves, one test | Tiers must carry real extra value |
| Positioning = target + difference | Rolls-Royce luxury, BMW driving pleasure | What clients think of first |
| Perceptual maps and MDS | Subway's healthy-and-tasty gap | Map rivals on expertise and speed |
| POP vs POD | Efficacy is parity; fewer side effects is difference | 24x7 is parity; domain depth is difference |
| Question old segments | Kwid: aspirational features at a low price | Small clients may pay for premium care |
| Intended vs actual | The Nano: media set the image | A low-cost tier will be talked about as cheap |
| Stretching up is hard | Phaeton fails; Lexus and Nexa succeed | Premium advisory needs its own name and people |
| Positioning statement | Snickers: hunger, peanuts | One statement for sales, delivery and marketing |
| Repositioning | Cadbury and Saffola by communication | Change delivery, not just the logo |
| Mass or niche | Align price, variants, name and channels | One package for all appeals to no one |

### The manager's checklist from this session

| # | Discipline | What it looks like on Monday morning |
|---|---|---|
| 1 | Define the image you want | Positioning is the end; advertising is the means. |
| 2 | Map before you message | Plot brands on the attributes customers use; look for desirable gaps. |
| 3 | Check perception | Ask customers where you actually sit, and whether your innovation registered. |
| 4 | Separate POP and POD | Meet parity; lead with a difference customers value. |
| 5 | Challenge industry assumptions | Ask whether "low price" really means "basic". |
| 6 | Align the 4Ps with the position | Channels, price and selling method must reach the target. |
| 7 | Move up with a new name | Use a separate brand or channel when stretching upmarket. |
| 8 | Write the positioning statement | Who, frame, POD, reason to believe: shared internally. |
| 9 | Reposition only with full commitment | Research, decide, change what must change, tell every customer. |
| 10 | Choose mass or niche on purpose | Then make price, variants, name and distribution agree. |

<div class="callout">
<p class="callout-title">The session in one breath</p>

Positioning is the marketer's effort to occupy a clear, distinctive and attractive place in target customers' minds relative to competitors (target segment plus differentiation) and it is found with perceptual maps on two known attributes (Subway's healthy-and-tasty gap) or with multidimensional scaling from simple similarity ratings (the car map, axes named by the analyst), then expressed through the three-circle framework as points of parity that get you considered and points of difference that get you chosen. Kwid showed a fresh psychographic lens can beat an industry assumption; the Nano showed intended and actual positioning can diverge and that the 4Ps must follow the position; the Phaeton and Kizashi showed brands struggle to move up, which is why Toyota created Lexus and Maruti created Nexa. A positioning statement (who, frame of reference, point of difference, reason to believe: Snickers, Maggi Oats, Mountain Dew, Gatorade) keeps the organisation aligned. Repositioning (creating a new image, not just rebranding) is driven by changing needs, a new target or a positioning gone wrong; it can be done through communication (Cadbury, Saffola) or product and communication, but it is costly, must reach every customer (Fair & Lovely's rural slump) and rarely works far from the brand's core. Finally, mass versus niche is a trade-off of volume against margin and appeal, and whichever is chosen, every P must agree.

</div>
