Once a brand is strong, the company is tempted to put its name on other things. The second half of the session asked when that works, how prominent the parent's name should be, and how to prune a portfolio that has grown too large.

## Brand extension: line and category

Every time a company launches a new product, it faces a choice: use a new name, or an existing one. Using an existing brand name for a new product is <strong>brand extension</strong>: Godrej, Maggi and Nike were the slide's examples.

| Type | Definition (from the slide) | Examples from class |
|---|---|---|
| Line extension | Existing brand name, existing product category: new flavours, pack sizes, forms, ingredients, colours | Diet Coke; Oreo's strawberry and other fillings; Lay's flavours; a liquid version of a powder; a perfume line within the same fragrance category |
| Category (brand) extension | Existing brand name, new product category | Dove from soap to deodorant and lotions; Apple from computers to the iPod and iPhone |

Some textbooks use "brand extension" only for category extensions; the class used it as the umbrella term for both. The boundary can blur: Bajaj moving from scooters into motorcycles could be called a category extension, or a line extension within two-wheelers.

<strong>On a manager's desk.</strong> Most growth starts as a line extension, because it is cheaper and safer. A product company adds flavours and sizes to keep customers who want variety. A service firm adds a tier or a module: the risk rises sharply only when it carries its name into a genuinely new category.

## Extend or create a new brand?

| Why extend (from the slide and class) | The disadvantages of extending |
|---|---|
| Saves time and money: you leverage a brand that took decades to build | May cannibalise the parent brand |
| Responds to neglected customer needs and to more competition | May dilute the parent brand's image |
| Aids awareness, associations and evaluation: quicker acceptance | Loses the chance to reach a different segment with a new brand and new associations |
| Induces trial and reduces the customer's risk | Reputational risk: if the new product fails, the parent suffers |
| Lets customers switch without leaving the brand (Lay's flavours keep a bored customer from moving to Kurkure) | A blurred, less sharp positioning than a clean new brand could have |
| Reduces dependence on one or a few products |   |

The advantages of a new brand are simply the disadvantages of extension turned round: a clean slate, a sharper positioning, freedom to price differently and to reach a different segment without touching the parent's image. A participant gave the example of an ethnic-wear brand creating a separate, lower-priced brand rather than stretching its own name down, and Toyota's Lexus is the upward version (Session 4).

### Cannibalisation and naming

The professor gave an example of how naming affects cannibalisation. If Colgate launches a sensitive-teeth toothpaste as "Colgate Sensitive", more of its sales tend to come from Colgate's own customers switching from other Colgate variants. Give the product a different name and build its awareness, and more of the sales come from competitors' customers. If cannibalisation is the worry, a new name is safer.

A participant asked about a <strong>fighter brand</strong>: a brand launched to fight low-priced competitors. Is it an extension? Only if it carries the parent's name. If cannibalisation is a concern, launch the fighter brand under a new name, so that it does not pull down the parent or steal its customers. And franchising, another participant asked? No: franchising is a distribution decision, a way to make the brand accessible, not an extension.

<strong>On a manager's desk.</strong> Write down what you gain and what you risk before choosing the name. A product company launching a cheaper line should ask whether it wants those buyers associated with its main brand. A service firm launching a low-cost offer should consider a separate name, so that the premium practice is not judged by it.

## Perceived fit: why some extensions work

Every example in this part of the class carried the same heading: <strong>perceived fit</strong> between the parent brand and the extension (a meaningful connection, from the customer's point of view).

| Extension | What happened | Why: the fit |
|---|---|---|
| Crystal Pepsi (Pepsi) | A clear, lighter-flavoured cola. A small group of loyal buyers formed, but most people were confused: a cola is brown; was this like Sprite? It failed. | "Cola" means brown; "clear" broke the association |
| Tab Clear (Coca-Cola) | Seeing Crystal Pepsi gain a niche, Coke launched Tab Clear under its diet brand and placed it next to Crystal Pepsi, adding to the confusion. Both failed, and Tab was damaged. | A "kamikaze" strategy: destroying the rival at a cost to itself |
| Diet Coke and Coke Zero | Both succeeded. Coke Zero, launched later, came because Diet Coke was seen as feminine and men avoided it (Session 0). | Fit within cola; gender-based segments |
| Bic lighters and razors | Success. | Bic stood for affordable, disposable products: a fit for lighters and razors |
| Bic perfume | Failure. | Perfume is hedonic and needs a lasting brand image; "cheap and disposable" hurt |
| Huggies to adult diapers | Kimberly-Clark used a new name, Depend, rather than a Huggies extension. | Adults would not want a toddlers' brand |
| Pond's toothpaste | Failed, though powder and cream succeeded. | Pond's means external beauty, not oral care |
| Lifebuoy talcum powder | Failed, while prickly-heat powder and hand wash worked. | Lifebuoy means hygiene and killing germs, not beauty |
| Amul pizza | Test-marketed in Gujarat in its own outlets, reasonably priced; it was dropped. | Amul means milk and dairy: ice cream, cheese, butter all fit; pizza did not |

<strong>Amul Happy Treats</strong> (ready-to-cook French fries, burger patties and aloo tikki, test-marketed in Ahmedabad) divided the class. One participant liked it because she trusts Amul. The professor's question was not whether Amul is a good brand, but whether these products are good for Amul: could the same products have been launched simply as Happy Treats, with a little more time and money, without risking Amul's strong dairy association?

<div class="callout">
<p class="callout-title">The framework for deciding</p>

<strong>1.</strong> Bring out the parent brand's associations from customers: all of them, not just the product category (Bic's "affordable and disposable" mattered more than "stationery").

<strong>2.</strong> Ask whether those associations help or hurt in the new category, and whether the company has the skills customers will expect there.

<strong>3.</strong> If they help, extend; if not, create a new brand. Support the decision with research: conjoint analysis, for example, can test how much buyers would pay for a product under different names.

</div>

The professor illustrated the framework with <strong>Jio</strong>. Its associations, from the class: free, cheap mobile plans, freedom. Fine for telecom, but would you send your child to a Jio-branded educational institute? Those associations do not help a premium education brand. By contrast, Tata has built an association with trust that can travel across very different categories.

### Using the parent, then letting go

Brands often use the parent name at launch and drop it later: <strong>Vivanta by Taj</strong> became Vivanta; <strong>Fastrack</strong> early ads carried "by Titan". Reusing a known name can also help: Maruti brought back the <strong>Grand Vitara</strong> name (from an earlier model that had not done well) for its strong hybrid SUV in 2022. Customers recognised the name without negative associations, and the car became a success.

<strong>On a manager's desk.</strong> Ask customers what your brand means before you ask what else it could sell. A product company should test whether its associations travel. A service firm extending from, say, monitoring into advisory should check whether clients associate it with expertise (which travels) or with low cost (which may not).

## Branding approaches: house of brands to branded house

Whenever a company launches a product under an existing name, it must also decide <strong>how much emphasis</strong> to put on the parent brand. Think of it as a spectrum.

<Fig title="Branding approaches" caption="From the new name carrying the product (left) to the parent name carrying it (right). Umbrella branding, meaning any use of an existing name, runs from endorser to corporate.">
  <Flow items={[
    { t: "Individual branding", s: "New name only; the parent is on the back of the pack at most. P&G: Tide, Ariel, Pampers. HUL: Dove." },
    { t: "Endorser branding", s: "The new name leads; the parent gives a light endorsement. Pulse (Pass Pass); Emami's Mantra." },
    { t: "Sub-branding", s: "Parent and new name share the stage. Honda City, Hyundai i20, Cadbury Dairy Milk, Vivanta by Taj." },
    { t: "Corporate branding", s: "Parent name plus a product description. Godrej, Dell, Virgin, LG, GE, Nike.", hl: true },
  ]} note="A firm at the left end is a house of brands. A firm at the right end is a branded house." />
</Fig>

| Approach | What it means | Examples from class |
|---|---|---|
| Individual (product) branding | Every product gets its own new name | P&G's Tide, Ariel, Pampers, Head & Shoulders, Whisper; HUL's Dove. Customers often do not know who owns them. |
| Endorser branding | The new name leads; the parent appears lightly as an endorsement | Pulse (from DS Group's Pass Pass): the pack carries Pass Pass, but the ads say Pulse. Urzza on a Bisleri can. Emami's spice brand Mantra, with "Emami Healthy & Tasty" on the pack |
| Sub-branding | Parent and new name share emphasis | Honda City, Honda Civic, Hyundai i10, i20, Creta; Cadbury Dairy Milk; Vivanta by Taj; Apple's iPod, iPad and MacBook |
| Corporate branding | The parent name plus a product description | Godrej refrigerators, Godrej locks; Dell laptops; Virgin Music, Virgin Airlines; LG; GE; Nike shoes and apparel |

<div class="callout">
<p class="callout-title">House of brands versus branded house (firm-level strategies)</p>

<strong>House of brands:</strong> a firm that tends to launch new products under new names and holds a portfolio of individual brands (P&G, HUL).

<strong>Branded house:</strong> a firm whose name is used on almost all its offerings (Dell, Virgin, Godrej, GE). Nike, which uses its name on everything, is close to a branded house even with sub-brands such as Nike Air.

</div>

How do you classify a particular brand? Look at how the company names it in its advertising, on the front of the pack and in interviews. ITC's Sunfeast, Aashirvaad and Classmate do not carry ITC on the front, so they are individual brands (the class debated this). Cadbury Dairy Milk puts Cadbury up front: sub-branding. The boundaries are blurred; judge by emphasis.

Emami's spices sparked debate. Emami, known for cosmetics (and, in the east, for its mustard oil), launched spices as Mantra with a light Emami endorsement. Participants called it endorser branding. Several said they would hesitate to buy "cosmetic-company" spices; the professor's view was that Mantra might have been better off without the Emami name, though only research could settle it.

> A rose by any other name would smell as sweet: Shakespeare. Marketers would beg to differ.
>
> <small>The professor on naming, paraphrased</small>

In categories where buyers cannot judge quality (perfume, for instance) the name may be most of what they pay for. Companies spend real time on naming.

<strong>On a manager's desk.</strong> Choose the branding approach deliberately for each launch. A product company with a trusted corporate name may gain from a branded house; one serving very different segments may need a house of brands. A services group can use its corporate name for trust and a sub-brand to signal a distinct offer: for example, the group name plus a named practice for a specialist service.

## Ingredient, store and fighter brands

| Concept | What it is | Examples |
|---|---|---|
| Ingredient branding | A form of co-branding in which a branded component inside another branded product is promoted | Intel Inside on HP and Dell laptops; Teflon non-stick coating (the pioneer: Teflon is a chemical brand that cookware makers advertised); Michelin tyres on cars; airlines advertising the Boeing Dreamliner; Tata Steelium used by furniture makers |
| Private (store) label brands | Products branded by the retailer, usually made by outsourced manufacturers | Spencer's, Shoppers Stop, Pantaloons; grocery and e-commerce chains' own snacks and staples |
| Manufacturers' brands | National brands sold through many retailers | P&G, HUL |
| Fighter (fighting) brands | A brand launched to fight low-priced competitors, ideally under a new name to limit cannibalisation | See [Extend or create a new brand?](#extend-or-create-a-new-brand) |

<div class="callout trap">
<p class="callout-title">A branded ingredient must be a component</p>

Food cooked with a branded spice mix, or labelled organic, is not ingredient branding by default. Ingredient branding requires a branded component inside the product to be promoted, and buyers to value it enough to pay more, as they did for "Intel Inside".

</div>

### Why retailers push their own labels

Store brands are priced lower, yet their margins for the retailer are higher. The retailer has already built trust through the store, so it saves on brand building and marketing, and the chain has fewer middlemen. Meanwhile manufacturers' brands, with their bargaining power, leave retailers thin margins. So retailers promote their own labels, offering customers good quality at a reasonable price.

<strong>On a manager's desk.</strong> Know which side of the shelf you are on. A manufacturer should expect retailers to push store brands and should make its own brand worth the price gap. A service firm can play the ingredient role: a managed service built on a well-known platform can name it, as laptops named Intel, if clients value it.

## Brand portfolio rationalisation

Brands multiply. Companies add them year after year until there are too many, dissipating resources on brands that contribute little. <strong>Brand portfolio rationalisation</strong> means optimising, usually cutting down, the number of brands. The usual rule of thumb is <strong>80/20</strong>: about 20% of a firm's brands bring in about 80% of its sales or profits.

| Company | What it did (from the slide) |
|---|---|
| HLL (now HUL), 2001 | Chose to focus on 30 of its 110 brands |
| P&G, 2014 | Announced it would focus on 70-80 brands and drop about 100 (Wall Street Journal, August 2014) |

### How to decide which brands to keep

| Criterion | What it means | Example |
|---|---|---|
| Contribution, sales and profit | Keep brands that cover their variable costs and contribute; a brand with negative contribution is not even recovering its variable costs |  |
| Market position | Keep brands that rank in the top three (or five) in their category | HUL's 2001 cut |
| Clear, non-overlapping positioning | Two brands with the same positioning compete with each other; keep one | Detergents: Rin (whitening), Surf (stain removal), Wheel (value). Soaps: Jai and Breeze overlapped, so Breeze went |
| Future growth potential | Keep a brand outside the top five if it has a strong image to build on | Pears, not top five on price, but a strong image, later extended into body wash |
| Strong regional brands | Keep brands with a strong regional franchise | Hamam, with medicinal and herbal associations in the south |

### Contribution is not profit

A participant described contribution as a share of total sales; the professor clarified. <strong>Contribution</strong> is price minus variable cost. Fixed costs should not drive the keep-or-drop decision, because they remain whether or not the brand exists. A brand can show a loss after fixed costs are allocated and still have a positive contribution: it is helping to cover fixed costs.

| Per unit | Brand X | Reading |
|---|---|---|
| Selling price | Rs. 100 |   |
| Variable cost | Rs. 70 |   |
| Contribution | Rs. 30 | Positive: every unit sold helps cover fixed costs |
| Allocated fixed cost | Rs. 40 | Would be incurred anyway |
| Profit after fixed costs | Minus Rs. 10 | A loss on paper, yet dropping X loses Rs. 30 a unit of cover |

The exercise must be repeated periodically; even with regular reviews, giants such as Nestle, P&G and HUL have found their brand count creeping into three digits.

<strong>On a manager's desk.</strong> Review your brand or service portfolio every year with the same five tests. A product company should drop overlapping brands rather than let them fight each other. A service firm with a dozen overlapping offerings should merge those that clients cannot tell apart, and keep the small one with a strong niche or growth story.

## Lessons from the case: extending a service brand

The session's case was an Indian hospital brand built on <strong>emergency care</strong> (its communication had long shown ambulances and burn patients) now expanding into areas such as cancer and cardiac care. The case is not retold here; its lesson is how to extend a service brand whose associations were built for something else.

|   | Emergency care | Cancer care |
|---|---|---|
| Nature | Unplanned, urgent | Planned, long-term |
| What the provider is | The saviour | A long-term partner |
| Decision | Top-of-mind recall at the moment of crisis | Considered choice, often after research |

A participant worried about cannibalisation. The professor corrected the term: cannibalisation means a new product's sales coming from your existing product's sales, and cancer patients do not substitute for emergency patients. The real risk is <strong>dilution</strong>: a weak cancer service damaging the emergency image.

So what should the cancer unit be called? Participants proposed endorser branding, to protect the core if the new unit struggled; others, simply using the existing name. The professor leaned towards <strong>sub-branding</strong> with the parent name prominent, because in services <strong>trust</strong> matters even more and takes very long to build, and excellence in emergency care does not conflict with cancer care. In practice the hospital used the parent name with descriptors (its cancer institute, academy, labs) and used different media for different segments: more direct, targeted marketing for oncology, while emergency messaging stayed broad.

<div class="callout">
<p class="callout-title">The delicate balance</p>

Every brand faces pressure to grow, and growth often comes at the cost of diluting the parent. The answer is to manage it delicately. Starbucks added tea and food, but chose cold sandwiches that do not interfere with the smell of coffee. Even so, the more it sells food, the more it risks being compared with McDonald's rather than coffee chains.

</div>

<strong>On a manager's desk.</strong> Services lean on trust, so the parent name usually earns its place on a new service, but only if the new service can live up to it. A hospital, a bank or a security provider extending into a new specialty should launch with the parent name, invest so that the new unit delivers to the same standard, and speak to each segment through the media it actually uses.

<div class="callout">
<p class="callout-title">The session in one breath</p>

A brand began as a mark burned on cattle and is now a customer experience, a promise and a network of associations; strong brands (Apple's queues, Harley's tattoos and licensing) act as shortcuts for buyers, earn trust that survives crises (Tylenol's recall, Maggi's comeback), shape even taste (the blind beer test) and command a price premium, but building one means beating four challenges: cash (decades of investment against managers' three-year horizons and the lure of promotions), staying contemporary (Kodak, Ambassador and HMT failed; Lux and Olay kept up), breaking clutter (Nirma, Nivea's tin, Amul's girl, "it's toasted") and delivering consistently at every touch point (only 12% of the 1955 Fortune 500 survived to 2017). Brand extension uses an existing name for a new product, as a line or a category extension, saving time and money but risking cannibalisation and dilution; success depends on perceived fit with the parent's associations (Bic's lighters worked, its perfume did not; Pond's toothpaste, Lifebuoy talc and Amul pizza failed). Branding approaches run from individual brands (house of brands) through endorser and sub-brands to corporate branding (branded house); ingredient, store and fighter brands fill out the toolkit; portfolios should be rationalised on contribution, rank, non-overlapping positioning, growth and regional strength; and a service brand extending into new specialties should usually lead with its trusted name, while managing dilution.

</div>
