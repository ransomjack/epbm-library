Session 4 covered the P of STP. It opened with one last segmentation story, the same pregnancy test sold as two brands at two prices, and then turned to positioning: what it is, how to see where brands sit in customers' minds, how to choose what to stand for, and how to write it down in one sentence.

## One product, two segments: the pregnancy test

The class began by finishing the segmentation discussion with a striking example. A US company that made home pregnancy tests (Quidel) was not doing well, and asked whether value-based segmentation could raise its profits. Are there groups of buyers who want different value from the same test?

There were two. Some women desperately hope for a <strong>positive</strong> result; others desperately hope for a <strong>negative</strong> one. The company called them the <strong>hopefuls</strong> and the <strong>fearfuls</strong>. Segmentation alone achieves nothing unless you build a different strategy for each segment, so it did, with exactly the same test inside.

|   | Fearfuls (want a negative result) | Hopefuls (want a positive result) |
|---|---|---|
| Product name | RapidVue | BabyStart |
| Packaging | Clinical, white | Pink |
| Placement in the store | Next to condoms and contraceptives | Next to ovulation kits |
| Price (as the professor recalled) | About $6.99 | About $9.99: roughly 30% more |
| Why that price | A grudge purchase: like replacing a tyre, you look for the cheapest | A planned, wished-for moment; they are ready, settled and willing to pay |

A participant expected the fearfuls to pay more, to avoid a clinic visit. The company's research showed the opposite: hopefuls were willing to pay more. Note that the whole strategy depends on each segment <strong>not discovering</strong> the other product, which is why the name, the pack and above all the shelf location differ. A fearful shopper never walks down the ovulation-kit aisle.

What if customers find out? That risk is real, especially today. The professor's view: for a small brand, the risk may be worth taking; for a big brand, the downside if it is exposed is too large. Yet companies do it all the time:

| Example | What was done | How it was justified, or exposed |
|---|---|---|
| Gillette women's razors | Essentially the same razor in a pink casing, priced about 30% higher, sometimes with a highlighted moisturising strip | Exposed when Gillette's ad on toxic masculinity drew attention to its own pink pricing; the price gap has since narrowed |
| A migraine pain reliever | The same tablet sold under a migraine label at a higher price than the standard pain-relief version, on a different shelf | Challenged in court, the company argued the migraine pack carries different and important dosage information, and offers psychological value. As the professor recalled, the company won |
| Branded versus generic drugs | The same salt sold at very different prices | Trust and psychology, and margins passed on through the distribution chain |

<div class="callout trap">
<p class="callout-title">Same product, different value</p>

Charging two segments different prices for the same product is price discrimination. It works only if the segments genuinely value the product differently, and if the segments can be kept apart: by name, packaging and placement. If either condition fails, it backfires.

</div>

<strong>On a manager's desk.</strong> Value-based segmentation can justify different prices for very similar offerings: if each segment truly gets different value and you are comfortable defending it in public. A product company should ask what happens to the brand if the two versions are compared side by side. A service firm offering the same monitoring at two price tiers must make sure the higher tier carries real extra value (faster response, a named analyst) not just a different name.

## What positioning is

Asked what positioning is, a participant said "advertising our product in the market". The professor corrected her: that is the <strong>means</strong>. Positioning is the end: the image you want to create in the minds of consumers.

<div class="callout">
<p class="callout-title">Positioning (the slide)</p>

The marketer's effort to occupy a clear, distinctive and attractive position relative to competing products in the minds of target consumers.

As Ries and Trout put it in 1972, positioning is not what you do to a product; it is what you do to the mind of the prospect.

</div>

The professor recalled the discussion of car brands from the campus session. Many car brands are expensive, but beyond price each wants a distinct image: Rolls-Royce stands for luxury, BMW for the pleasure of driving, Maruti for affordability and economic value. Safety is the image another brand chooses to own. Once the image is chosen, the whole strategy (product features, communication, channels) follows from it.

<div class="callout">
<p class="callout-title">A formula worth remembering</p>

<strong>Positioning = target segment + differentiation.</strong> You must know whom you are speaking to, and what you offer them that rivals do not. This is why positioning follows targeting in STP.

</div>

<strong>On a manager's desk.</strong> A brand's position is the one sentence customers would use to describe it. A product company should be able to finish "we are the brand that..." without a list. A service firm should be able to say what clients think of first when they hear its name, and whether that matches what the firm wants them to think.

## Perceptual maps

How do you see where brands actually sit in customers' minds? If you know the two attributes customers care about most in the category (customers usually focus on about two) the method is simple. Run a quick survey asking customers to rate each brand on those two attributes, and plot the average ratings. Price and quality is a common pair that works across many categories.

The map shows two things. First, whether customers see your brand where you intended. Second, any <strong>vacant space</strong>: if no brand sits in a combination customers find desirable (say, decent quality at a low price) that gap may be an opportunity.

When Subway entered, it did this exercise with fast-food brands. Customers evaluated them on taste and health, and the existing brands were all bunched in the tasty-but-unhealthy quadrant. Subway positioned itself in the empty, desirable one: fast food that is both healthy and tasty. Its communication followed that position.

<strong>On a manager's desk.</strong> Draw the map before you write the ad. A product company that finds every rival in one corner has found its positioning opportunity, provided customers actually want the empty corner. A service firm can map competitors on, say, depth of expertise and speed of response, and see where clients feel underserved.

## When you do not know the attributes: MDS

What if you do not know which two attributes customers use? A statistical technique called <strong>multidimensional scaling (MDS)</strong> finds them from one simple question. The class did not go into the mathematics; managers should know it exists.

| Step | What happens |
|---|---|
| 1. Ask one question | "How similar are these two brands?" on a 1-5 or 1-7 scale, for every pair of brands in the category. |
| 2. Average the ratings | A table of average similarity for each pair: brand 1 with brand 2, brand 1 with brand 3, and so on. |
| 3. Run MDS | Feed the table into statistical software (SPSS, R, Stata and others) and request an output in two dimensions. Two is easiest to interpret, and in most categories two dimensions stand out. |
| 4. Name the axes | The software places similar brands close together, but it does not name the axes. The researcher must interpret them: from experience, or with help from experts. |
| 5. Look for gaps | As with any perceptual map, find desirable unoccupied space. |

The professor showed an MDS output for cars, as Tata Motors might have obtained it. Why do customers see Lamborghini and Porsche as close, but Lamborghini and Rolls-Royce as far apart? Participants interpreted one axis as <strong>price</strong> and the other as something like chauffeur-driven, traditional and conservative versus self-driven and sporty. With that map, a company launching a new car can look for a desirable gap: such as a sporty car at a lower price.

### How do you know you have named the axes correctly?

A participant asked how to validate the interpretation, since a wrong reading would mislead the whole exercise. The professor's answers: ask experienced experts in the field; check the interpretation against the brands' product features and against what their advertising says. Remember too that positions are <strong>relative</strong> (the centre is the average of all brands rated) so a brand that sits on the sporty side is sportier than average, not necessarily a sports car.

Checking customer perception also catches positioning that has not landed. The professor's example was Goodyear in the 1990s. Tyre buyers mainly look at tread life (how long the tyre lasts) and wet traction. Goodyear invested heavily in a tyre that stopped far better in wet conditions, yet when customers rated it against other tyres, they rated it no differently. The innovation had not translated into perception, and the company had to work out why.

<strong>On a manager's desk.</strong> Measure the position you actually hold, not the one in your strategy deck. A product company that has invested in a real advantage should check, with customers, whether they noticed it. A service firm that believes it is seen as the specialist can ask clients to rate it against rivals: the answer may be "about the same".

## Points of parity and points of difference

A simpler tool for choosing a position is the three-circle framework from the slide. Draw one circle for <strong>customer needs</strong>, one for the <strong>firm's offering</strong> and one for the <strong>competitor's offering</strong>. Where should you position yourself?

<Fig title="The three-circle framework" caption="The positioning framework from the class slide. Position yourself at B.">
  <Circles3 />
  <Cards min={190} items={[
    { k: "A", title: "Points of parity", body: "We, the competitor and the customer's needs all share these. They get you considered." },
    { k: "B", title: "Our points of difference", body: "What we offer that customers need and the competitor lacks. This is the positioning.", hl: true },
    { k: "C", title: "Their points of difference", body: "What the competitor offers that customers need and we lack." },
  ]} />
</Fig>

A participant chose the area where all three overlap: it meets every need. The professor asked why you would want to stand exactly where the competitor stands. The right answer is <strong>B</strong>: what you offer, that customers need, and that competitors do not. That is your <strong>point of difference (POD)</strong>, the heart of your positioning. The central area <strong>A</strong>, shared by everyone, holds the <strong>points of parity (POP)</strong>: things you must have to be considered at all, but which give no reason to choose you. Area <strong>C</strong> is the competitor's point of difference: something customers need that you lack.

The professor's example: suppose you launch a weight-loss drug. It must be as effective as the others: that is parity. If it brings nothing more, why does it exist? But if it is more convenient to take, or has fewer side effects (no gastric trouble, say), that is the point of difference you call out.

<div class="callout trap">
<p class="callout-title">Parity gets you considered; difference gets you chosen</p>

Leading with a point of parity ("effective", "reliable", "good mileage") says nothing that competitors cannot also say. Points of parity must be met, but the positioning is built on the point of difference that customers value.

</div>

<strong>On a manager's desk.</strong> List your POPs and PODs separately and be honest about both. A product company that cannot name a POD customers care about is competing on price whether it likes it or not. A service provider might find that 24x7 monitoring is now parity, while deep knowledge of industrial control systems is a genuine difference worth leading with.

## A new lens on an old segment: Renault Kwid

A participant argued that Indian car buyers look mainly at mileage and reliability, and that most brands market themselves that way. The professor used it to tell the story of <strong>Renault Kwid</strong>.

For years, carmakers in India saw two segments: low-price buyers and high-price buyers (really a proxy for income). High-price buyers were sold features and space. Low-price buyers, the thinking went, care only about economy: "kitna deti hai?" (how much mileage does it give).

Kwid was launched as an affordable small car, but it segmented differently: by <strong>psychographics</strong>. Why should a buyer on a tight budget not also want aspirational features? Kwid brought SUV-like styling and features usually found in bigger cars into an entry-level car, aimed at young buyers. It became one of the best-selling small cars for a while, and sent Maruti's and Hyundai's engineers back to the drawing board. The industry's assumption that low price means mileage alone had been broken.

A participant asked whether this hurt margins. Possibly (that would need analysis) but the car sold very well. The professor added that the entry-level segment later shrank as tighter emission and safety rules and GST raised small-car prices, and has only recently begun to recover.

<strong>On a manager's desk.</strong> Question the segment definitions everyone in your industry takes for granted. A product company that assumes budget buyers want only the basics may be leaving an aspirational segment unserved. A service firm that assumes smaller clients want only the cheapest package may find many will pay for a premium feature that makes them feel looked after.

<Decide q="For years carmakers assumed that buyers of low-priced cars cared only about mileage. What did Renault Kwid do differently?" options={[

  { label: "It looked at the same buyers through psychographics and offered aspirational features.", good: true, why: "Why should a buyer on a tight budget not want the look and features of a bigger car?" },
  { label: "It aimed at high-income buyers.", why: "It stayed in the entry-level segment and saw it differently." },

## The Tata Nano: intended versus actual

The professor returned to the Nano with the new vocabulary. The slide quoted Ratan Tata in 2012: "Nano got wrongly positioned as a poor man's car". He said that was not the intention; it was meant to be an affordable family car. So what was the intended positioning? Use the formula: target segment plus differentiation.

| Element | The Nano's intended positioning (from its launch ad) |
|---|---|
| Behaviour (the main variable) | <strong>Non-users</strong>: families who did not own a car (two-wheeler owners) |
| Geography | Rural areas, smaller towns, semi-urban India |
| Demographics | Lower-middle income, small households |
| Psychographics | Aspiring to move up from two wheels to four |
| Differentiation | Quality and robustness at that price, value, not "lowest price" |

Targeting non-users is a legitimate and sometimes brilliant strategy. The professor's example was CavinKare's <strong>Chik</strong> shampoo, which went after rural, low-income consumers who did not use shampoo because they could not afford a bottle, and sold it in sachets. Sachets went on to revolutionise the Indian shampoo industry.

But the Nano's intended position did not land. Because it was the cheapest car in the world, media attention fixed on the price; Ratan Tata was interviewed constantly by Indian and international media about the Rs. 1 lakh car. The coverage, not the company, positioned it as the poor man's car, and in India a car is an aspirational purchase.

And the 4Ps did not follow the positioning, as Session 0 also noted ([Great product, wrong fit: the Tata Nano](/marketing-management/the-marketing-plan/#great-product-wrong-fit-the-tata-nano)): there were no dealerships in rural India, so the target customers would have had to travel to the city; and the first cars, allotted by lottery, went largely to people who already owned cars, who compared them with their bigger cars and spread negative word of mouth.

<div class="callout">
<p class="callout-title">Two lessons in one car</p>

<strong>Intended positioning is not actual positioning.</strong> You can read the intended position from the company's own communication; the actual position is whatever forms in customers' minds.

<strong>The 4Ps must follow the positioning.</strong> After positioning comes the strategy. A rural, first-time-buyer position needs rural distribution and a way of selling that reaches first-time buyers.

</div>

<strong>On a manager's desk.</strong> When a product is newsworthy, plan for how the news will describe it. A product company should decide in advance which story it wants told and seed it. A service firm launching a low-cost tier should expect the market to talk about the price, and make sure the value story is just as easy to repeat.

## How far can a brand stretch?

How was Volkswagen positioned? In India, participants said a German car with a slightly premium image. Around the world, the name means "people's car": affordable, aimed at the young. When Volkswagen launched the <strong>Phaeton</strong>, an ultra-luxury car, it had all the features and was competitively priced, yet it failed. Buyers in the ultra-luxury segment did not want to be associated with the people's car. And as with the Nano, the strategy did not help: the Phaeton was sold from the same showrooms as Volkswagen's cheaper cars, while a buyer paying for ultra-luxury expects to be treated differently from someone buying an entry-level car.

Maruti faced the same wall. Its early attempts at the premium segment, such as the Kizashi, struggled. Its response was a strategy change: a separate premium retail channel, <strong>Nexa</strong>, with its own showrooms and experience, gradually building a premium presence.

On a price-quality map, the professor asked: is it harder for a brand to move up or down? <strong>Up.</strong> A premium brand can offer a cheaper line under its name and customers accept it: the Taj group's Vivanta and Ginger. A mass brand that moves up is rarely believed. When Toyota went into the ultra-premium segment, it did not call the car a Toyota; it created <strong>Lexus</strong>.

<strong>On a manager's desk.</strong> Do not ask a brand to stand for something its customers will not believe. A product company moving upmarket should consider a separate brand and a separate channel, as Toyota and Maruti did. A service firm known for low-cost delivery that wants to sell premium consulting will usually need a distinct practice name, different people in front of clients, and a different buying experience.

## The positioning statement

A positioning statement puts the positioning into one sentence with three elements: <strong>who</strong>, <strong>what</strong> and <strong>why</strong>.

<div class="callout">
<p class="callout-title">Requirements of a good positioning statement (the slide)</p>

<strong>Who:</strong> the target segment.

<strong>What:</strong> the point of difference and the frame of reference.

<strong>Why:</strong> the reason to believe the brand is best at meeting the need.

</div>

The <strong>frame of reference</strong> is the competitive frame: the category or alternatives you are benchmarking against. It tells customers what kind of thing you are, so that the point of difference makes sense.

### Who is it for?

Is a positioning statement for customers? A participant thought it was a brand differentiator to announce. The professor explained it is mostly an <strong>internal document</strong>, so that everyone in the company (sales, product, engineering, production) understands what the brand stands for and is on the same page. Otherwise, as in the Goodyear story, the sales team says one thing and the brand manager another. It can also form the basis of the brief to an advertising agency.

### The templates

The slides offered several templates; they mix and match the elements, and some omit one:

| Template | Elements included |
|---|---|
| "For [target market], [x] provides [point of difference], because [reason to believe]." | Target, POD, reason to believe: no frame of reference |
| "To customers who are [target summary], our product offers [customer need], relative to [competitive alternatives]." | Target, need, frame of reference: no reason to believe |
| "Among [target market], [x] is the brand of [frame of reference] that [point of difference] because [reason to believe]." | All four |

<Fig title="The four elements of a positioning statement" caption="Template: Among [target market], [x] is the brand of [frame of reference] that [point of difference] because [reason to believe]. (Chernev, 2011, from the class slide, with the slide's Snickers example.)">

Asked to write one for Snickers, a participant targeted "anyone who needs to complete a meal". The professor accepted that a customer might see it that way, but showed the brand's own version: among <strong>snackers</strong>, Snickers is the brand of <strong>candy bar</strong> that <strong>satisfies your hunger</strong> because it is <strong>packed with peanuts</strong>. The point of difference is not taste; it is hunger, which is why the ads show people who are not themselves until they eat one.

### Working backwards from advertisements

| Brand | Who | Frame of reference | Point of difference | Reason to believe |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| Maggi Oats Noodles (ad shown in class) | Health-conscious families; parents choosing for children | Breakfast options such as cereals | Healthy, tasty and quick | Fibre from oats, real vegetables, ready in two minutes |
| Mountain Dew (slide example) | Young, active consumers who have little time for sleep | Soft drinks | More energy than other soft drinks | The highest level of caffeine |
| Gatorade (slide example) | Athletes | Water | Hydrates, replenishes and refuels in ways water can't | <em>Not stated</em> |

Why would Gatorade leave out the reason to believe? A participant suggested electrolytes. But sports drinks refuel largely through <strong>sugar</strong> (energy drinks rely more on caffeine). Highlighting only part of the reason may not look good, and highlighting sugar would carry negative connotations. Sometimes a brand deliberately leaves an element unsaid.

The Maggi example also showed the brand's own frame mattering. Participants first said the target was children. The professor pointed out that the brand would not want to be seen as only for children; by talking about oats and health, it positions itself as a <strong>breakfast</strong> option parents can choose for the family.

<strong>On a manager's desk.</strong> Write the statement before anything else, and check every activity against it. A product team's packaging, pricing and ad brief should all trace back to the same four elements. A service firm's proposals, website and sales pitch should name the same target, the same frame and the same reason to believe: if the sales team and the marketing team would write different statements, the position is not yet set.
