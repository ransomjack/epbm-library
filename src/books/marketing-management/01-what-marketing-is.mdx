This book starts where the module started: on campus, with the orientation class. It asked three things. Why does a good product still need marketing? What is marketing, exactly? And how do you read a situation before deciding anything? The two frameworks that answer the third question, the 5Cs and PESTLE, come back in every later chapter.

## Why marketing matters: the Samsung story

In 2011 Samsung was already one of the largest electronics makers in the world. Its televisions were in living rooms everywhere and its smartphones were genuinely good. Yet when Todd Pendleton joined that year as chief marketing officer of Samsung's American business, he later testified that he had not even been aware the company made smartphones. If the man hired to market the phones had not noticed them, the customer certainly had not. People knew Samsung for televisions; in phones, he said, there was no recognition of what the product was or what it stood for.

Within about a year that changed completely. In 2012 the Galaxy S III briefly outsold the iPhone and became the best-selling smartphone in the world. When the New York Times reported his testimony in 2014, the headline on the professor's slide read: <em>"Samsung Executive Says Marketing Drove Phone Sales to No.1"</em>. The phones had been good all along. What changed between 2011 and 2012 was marketing.

The professor asked the class exactly that question (what changed?) and then played the answer. Samsung ran a campaign called <strong>The Next Big Thing</strong>. In the ad the class watched, a long queue of people waits for hours outside a store for the new iPhone, talking themselves into the wait. People walking past are using a Samsung Galaxy: a bigger screen, 4G speed. The people in the queue grow curious, then envious, and one of them can't quite admit he would ever buy a Samsung. The series poked fun at Apple fans, went viral, and put Samsung on the smartphone map.

<div class="callout">
<p class="callout-title">Key idea: a good product is not enough</p>

A product that customers have not heard of, or cannot place in their minds, does not sell. Samsung did not reinvent its phones between 2011 and 2012; it changed what people knew and felt about them. Marketing is the work of making a product <strong>mean something</strong> to the customer it is meant for.

</div>

### Who mocks the leader? The challenger's playbook

A participant called the campaign "disruptive marketing". The professor turned it into a question: which kind of company runs ads that take a swipe at a rival (the market leader or the challenger)? The <strong>challenger</strong>. Comparative advertising is the tool of the brand that is behind. It grabs attention fast, and it borrows the leader's fame: every time the ad mentions the iPhone, Samsung gets to stand next to it in the customer's mind. A leader rarely does this, because naming a smaller rival only gives the rival free publicity.

Keep this in mind for [The product life cycle](/marketing-management/the-marketing-plan/#the-product-life-cycle). Attention-grabbing, even offensive, advertising is typical of the <strong>introduction stage</strong> of a product's life, when the only job is to get noticed. It stops working once the brand needs customers to actually like it.

<div class="callout trap">
<p class="callout-title">Marketing is not advertising</p>

The Samsung story is told through an ad campaign, but the professor was careful to say that marketing is far more than one ad. It is every decision about the product, its price, where it is sold and how it is promoted, and, before all of these, the choice of whom to serve. The ad worked because it sat on top of a good product, a clear target (people who wanted a big screen and speed) and phones that were easy to find in shops.

</div>

<strong>On a manager's desk.</strong> A better product does not announce itself. A product company with a feature the leader lacks can use comparison, as Samsung did, to get onto the customer's shortlist. A service business faces the same problem in a harder form, because its product cannot be seen in a shop window: a managed security provider with an excellent monitoring centre stays invisible until it can say in one sentence what it stands for, and get that sentence into the buyer's head.

## From the factory to the customer

Picture three generations of the same sweet shop. The grandfather, a hundred years ago, had the only halwai in town. Whatever he made by evening was gone by night; his only problem was making enough. His son faced ten sweet shops on the same street. He made what he was good at and then had to push it: a boy at the door calling people in, discounts at festival time, a little pressure at the counter. The granddaughter runs the shop today. She starts by asking who walks down the street, what they want (sugar-free for the diabetic uncle, small boxes for office gifting, kaju katli that travels well to Dubai), and only then decides what to make. Customers now come in asking for her boxes by name.

Those three generations are the three ways businesses have looked at the marketplace, which the professor's slide laid out in order.

| Approach | Era | The belief behind it | The sweet shop version |
|---|---|---|---|
| The production concept | 1890s to 1920s | Customers want products that are available and affordable. Make them efficiently and they will sell. | The grandfather: the only shop in town; the job is to make enough. |
| The selling concept | 1920s to 1950s | Customers will not buy enough on their own. They must be persuaded. This is <strong>push</strong>. | The father: too many shops, so push what you make with offers and pressure. |
| The marketing concept | 1950s to present | Find out what customers need and value, and build the offering around it. This is <strong>pull</strong>. | The granddaughter: start with the customer; they come asking for you. |
| The societal marketing concept | 1980s to present | Satisfy customers in a way that also protects the long-term interests of society. | Less sugar, no plastic boxes, honest labels, because the town is watching. |

The selling concept begins in the factory with products that already exist, uses selling and promotion as its means, and hopes to make money through <strong>volume</strong>. The marketing concept begins in the market with customer needs, uses every part of the business together (integrated marketing), and makes money through <strong>customer satisfaction</strong>: the customer who comes back and tells a friend.

### Drucker's two sentences

The professor anchored the shift with Peter Drucker, the management thinker. In 1954 he argued that there is only one valid purpose of a business: <strong>to create a customer</strong>. What a business thinks it produces matters far less than what the customer thinks he is buying and what he counts as value: that decides what the business is, what it makes, and whether it survives.

> The aim of marketing is to make selling superfluous.
>
> <small>Peter Drucker, on the class slide</small>

Drucker did not say selling disappears; he said there will always be some need for it. His point was that if you understand the customer well enough, the product fits him so well that it nearly sells itself. Ideally, marketing produces a customer who is ready to buy, and all that is left is to make the product available. That is the meaning of <strong>pull</strong>: demand comes looking for you.

<div class="callout">
<p class="callout-title">Push and pull in one picture</p>

<strong>Push:</strong> a salesman going door to door with a vacuum cleaner the family never asked for. <strong>Pull:</strong> a customer walking into a kirana store and asking for a particular brand by name. Push works on the customer; pull works because of what the customer already wants.

</div>

<div class="callout trap">
<p class="callout-title">Sales is not marketing</p>

When participants later listed the 4Ps as "sales", the professor stopped them: sales falls under marketing; it is just one part of it. Selling starts with what the firm has made and looks for a buyer. Marketing starts with what the buyer needs and shapes what the firm makes, prices, places and promotes.

</div>

<strong>On a manager's desk.</strong> Ask which arrow your business lives on. A product company that measures success only by units pushed into dealers is on the selling arrow, and discounts will become its habit. A service company that measures renewals and referrals is on the marketing arrow. A security operations team that keeps selling more alerts to a customer who wanted fewer, better ones is pushing; one that asks what the plant manager actually loses sleep over is pulling.

## What is marketing? The official definition

The professor opened with a challenge rather than a definition: by the end of the module, each participant should be able to write a better definition of marketing (in their own words) than the one they walked in with. She admitted that definitions are the boring part ("like the 3 Idiots scene: what is a machine?"), but showed why this one is worth a few minutes.

The official definition comes from the <strong>American Marketing Association (AMA)</strong>. It is not written by one author. Every few years a group drawn from industry and academia meets, debates whether the role of marketing has changed, and revises the wording only if it has. The first official definition dates from the 1930s. The current one was adopted in 2007, and the AMA has not felt the need to change it since. The one before it was from 2004. Putting the two side by side shows how the idea of marketing has grown.

| Element | 2004 definition | 2007 definition (current) | What the change means |
|---|---|---|---|
| What marketing is | An <em>organizational function</em> and a set of processes | An <em>activity</em>, a set of institutions, and processes | No longer a department. It is a verb: anyone can do it, even a person preparing a CV is marketing himself. |
| What it does | Creating, communicating and delivering value; managing customer relationships | Creating, communicating, delivering and <strong>exchanging</strong> offerings | "Exchanging offerings that have value" brings in price: value is traded, not just handed over. |
| Who benefits | The organization and its stakeholders | Customers, clients, partners and <strong>society at large</strong> | Society was implied before; now it is explicit. This is the societal concept of [From the factory to the customer](#from-the-factory-to-the-customer) written into the definition. |

Look closely at the four verbs in the current definition. The professor asked the class what each one refers to, and the answer turns the definition into a framework you will use for the rest of the module.

And the very first definition, from the 1930s? It described marketing as the business activities that direct the <strong>flow of goods and services from producers to consumers</strong>. In 4P terms that is almost entirely <strong>Place</strong>: getting the product to where the customer is. Over eighty years the definition widened from moving goods to creating value for the customer and for society.

<div class="callout trap">
<p class="callout-title">Communicating is not only advertising</p>

When a participant said "communicating" meant advertisement, the professor corrected him: not only advertising. It covers every way a firm talks to customers: sales promotion, packaging copy, the salesperson, social media, the store. Equally, "marketing" in the 2007 sense is not a department: the engineer who designs a feature customers love is doing marketing too.

</div>

<div class="callout beyond">
<p class="callout-title">Good to know: dates and wording</p>

The professor recalled the first definition as 1937. Most histories date it to 1935, when it was written by the National Association of Marketing Teachers, a forerunner of the AMA, which adopted it in 1948. The 2007 definition was reviewed and reaffirmed by the AMA in 2017. <strong>What matters is the direction of change:</strong> from a function to an activity, from delivering value to exchanging offerings, and from the organization to society at large.

</div>

<strong>On a manager's desk.</strong> If marketing is an activity and not a department, every function owns a piece of it. In a product company the service engineer who fixes a machine on the first visit is creating and delivering value as surely as the brand team. In a service company the analyst who writes a clear incident report is communicating the offering every week. Ask of each team: which of the four verbs do they perform?

## The opening exercise: pricing a smartphone

Before any framework appeared on the screen, the professor set a problem. You are launching a new smartphone. How will you decide its price? What will you look at? Participants called out factors, and the board filled up quickly.

| Factor raised in class | What it means |
|---|---|
| Competitors' prices | Where rival phones with similar features sit. |
| Market gaps | Price bands or needs that no phone serves well yet. |
| USP / differentiation | The unique selling proposition: what this phone does that others do not. |
| Target customers and willingness to pay | Who the phone is for, and what they would pay for its unique features. |
| Brand name and brand value | What the name on the back of the phone is already worth, or not, if it is new. |
| Demand and its elasticity | How strongly sales respond to price (discussed below). |
| Distribution route | Online, through retailers, or direct to the consumer: each passes on a different margin. |
| Durability | How long the phone lasts, which shapes value for money. |
| Target margins | The margin the company wants for itself. |
| Costs | Production, R&D, and marketing and advertising costs. |
| Country and geography | Which market: prices and buyers differ by country and even by city. |
| Seasonality | Raised later for products whose demand rises and falls with the season. |

### Does price follow demand, or demand follow price?

One participant suggested looking at demand: if demand is elastic, price low; if it is inelastic, like Apple's, you can charge whatever you like. The professor first reminded the class that this was a <strong>new</strong> brand and a new product: nobody should assume Apple's pricing power. Then she asked a sharper question: does demand govern price, or does price govern demand?

The answer the class reached is that demand is not a separate input you look up before setting the price: demand <strong>changes with</strong> the price you set. What a manager really needs to know is the threshold: the price above which customers walk away. How do you find it? By looking at what competitors charge, at the USP, and at what the target customer is willing to pay for that unique feature. Those are the things you can actually study.

### Distribution changes the price

A participant pointed out that how you distribute matters, and the professor agreed strongly. Every middleman (distributor, wholesaler, retailer) needs a margin, and that margin sits inside the price the customer pays. Going <strong>direct to consumer (D2C)</strong> can lower the price point; going through the regular channel adds cost but buys reach. Many new consumer brands, cosmetics in particular, started D2C online and only later moved into shops.

The slides carried a striking example from the other end of the market. In June 2021 Mercedes-Benz announced that it would sell cars in India directly to customers, an industry first, under a plan called <strong>Retail of the Future</strong>, part of its "Reimagine Excellence" programme.

Under the old model, each dealer bought cars and sold them at its own price, and customers haggled from showroom to showroom. Under the new one, Mercedes-Benz India sets one price, holds all the inventory and invoices the customer itself. Dealers become franchise partners who provide the showroom, the test drive, the sales advice and the handover, and compete only on the quality of the customer experience. The company said the change would pass on benefits of Rs. 1-2 lakh on the selling price, and it reported orders worth about Rs. 825 crore in less than a month. A decision about <strong>Place</strong> changed the <strong>Price</strong>.

Then the professor made the point of the whole exercise. Many in the room had missed distribution entirely: an important driver of price. Others had missed context: the state of the economy, the country, the regulations. Nobody missed these things through lack of intelligence. They missed them because they were listing factors from memory, with no structure.

<div class="callout">
<p class="callout-title">Why frameworks exist</p>

A framework is a checklist that stops you missing things. Listing factors from memory, a smart group still forgot distribution. Run the same problem through a framework and distribution cannot be forgotten, because the framework has a box for it. That framework is the 5Cs.

</div>

<strong>On a manager's desk.</strong> Price is an output of many decisions, not a number picked from a competitor's tag. A product company that adds a distributor layer must find that margin somewhere: from the customer or from itself. A service company selling through channel partners or system integrators faces the same arithmetic: the partner's share is part of the price the end client sees, so the channel decision and the pricing decision are one decision.

## The 5Cs: reading the situation

The 5Cs is a very general framework that can be applied to almost any marketing problem: pricing a phone, launching a cereal, entering a new city. Before deciding anything, it asks you to look in five directions. The professor drew the Cs out of the class one by one.

| The C | The question on the slide | What to look at |
|---|---|---|
| Customers | What needs do we satisfy? Who are the potential buyers? | Their needs, how much they are willing to pay, which features they value most. |
| Company | What skills do we possess? | Strengths, costs (production, R&D, marketing), target margins, brand image. |
| Collaborators | Who can help us? | Distributors, retailers, suppliers, agencies, and what will motivate them to partner with you. |
| Competitors | Who competes with us? | Their prices, products, USPs and likely reactions. |
| Context | PESTLE analysis | The macro environment the whole industry works in ([PESTLE: reading the context](#pestle-reading-the-context)). |

Notice where distribution falls: under <strong>collaborators</strong>. Distributors and retailers do not work for you; they choose to partner with you. You have to incentivize and motivate them (with margin, support, exclusivity) and that cost feeds straight back into price. Once the 5Cs is in front of you, the factor the class missed has an obvious home.

### Customer or consumer?

A participant used the two words interchangeably, and the professor separated them. The <strong>consumer</strong> is the person who actually uses the product. The <strong>customer</strong> is the person who buys it. They are often the same person, but not always: a mother buys the cereal her child eats; a company buys laptops its staff use. Marketing has to understand both: the buyer pays, the user decides whether the product is any good.

### Some factors sit in two Cs at once

The class then placed its own pricing factors into the 5Cs, and found that some belong in two places. That is not a flaw in the framework: it is the point.

| Factor from the exercise | Which C | Why |
|---|---|---|
| Production, R&D, marketing costs; target margins | Company | They are the firm's own numbers. |
| Features | Company <strong>and</strong> Customer | What a feature costs the company to provide, and what customers will pay for it. Companies sometimes build great features that customers are simply not willing to pay for. |
| Brand image | Company <strong>and</strong> Customer | The image the firm holds, and, for an existing brand, the image already in customers' minds. |
| USP | Company <strong>and</strong> Customer | The firm's difference matters only if customers value it. |
| Distribution | Collaborators | Channel partners must be motivated to carry the product. |
| Competitors' prices | Competitors | The reference points customers will compare you with. |
| Economy, country, regulation | Context | Forces outside any single firm's control. |

### Why engineers struggle with marketing

Midway through, the professor asked how many in the room were engineers. Most hands went up. "You are going to be in big trouble in my module," she said, only half joking. Engineers like to solve equations and arrive at one answer to the decimal point. Marketing is the other way round: there is rarely a yes or no, rarely a single right answer.

> Marketing is not black and white. You have to start appreciating shades of grey.
>
> <small>The professor, paraphrased from the class</small>

<strong>On a manager's desk.</strong> Run the 5Cs before every launch, price change or market entry: it takes ten minutes and catches the factor nobody mentioned. For a product firm the C most often forgotten is collaborators: the dealer who will not push your product because a rival pays a better margin. For a service firm it is often the customer-versus-consumer split: the CFO signs the contract for a security service, but the plant engineers who live with it every day decide whether it is renewed.

## PESTLE: reading the context

The fifth C, context, is large enough to need a framework of its own. It is the macro environment the whole industry works in: nothing a single firm can change, but everything it must read. The class unpacked it with <strong>PESTLE</strong> (sometimes written PESTEL).

| Letter | Covers | Smartphone example from class | Breakfast cereal example |
|---|---|---|---|
| P: Political | Government stability, policy, trade relations | Launching in a neighbouring country? Check how stable the government is. | Import duties on ingredients; state food policies. |
| E: Economic | Growth, incomes, inflation, interest rates | If the economy is weak, is this the time for a high-end phone? | Food inflation raising the cost of grain and milk. |
| S: Social | Trends, habits, values, demographics | Changing usage habits of young buyers. | People moving towards organic and health-conscious eating, and willing to pay for it. |
| T: Technological | New technology that changes the product or market | 5G versus 4G: will a 4G phone look outdated at launch? | Packaging that keeps cereal fresh longer. |
| L: Legal | Laws, standards, norms | Safety, emission and certification norms for devices. | Food safety and labelling rules. |
| E: Environmental | Climate, sustainability, waste | E-waste rules and recycling expectations. | Plastic packaging rules; monsoon and crop risk. |

The professor's practical advice: you need not work through all six letters with equal care for every problem. Pick the ones that matter for this product and this market, and put more weight on them. For a breakfast cereal, social trends deserve real attention; for a smartphone, technology does.

<strong>On a manager's desk.</strong> PESTLE is the part of planning most often skipped because it feels like general knowledge. A product company that ignored the 5G rollout or an e-waste rule can find its inventory stranded. A service company reads the same forces as shifts in what clients must buy: a new cybersecurity regulation for critical infrastructure, for instance, turns a nice-to-have monitoring service into a compliance requirement overnight.

## Same framework, any product

Having worked through the smartphone, the professor changed the problem. You are now launching a <strong>new breakfast cereal</strong>. How would you decide its price point? Participants offered competition, value, raw material prices, additional features. Then she asked: how different is this exercise from the smartphone? Someone said, very different. Why?

It turned out not to be very different at all. The <strong>framework stays the same</strong>; what changes is where you put the emphasis. A cereal is perishable, so shelf life enters the cost picture; it may be seasonal; social trends like health and organic eating weigh heavily in its context. A smartphone brings technology and product life to the front instead. That is the beauty of frameworks: they are very generic, and with a little tweaking at your end they apply across problems.

|   | Smartphone | Breakfast cereal |
|---|---|---|
| Customers | Features valued, willingness to pay for them | Health benefits valued; who buys versus who eats |
| Company | R&D cost, brand, margins | Raw material cost, perishability, shelf life |
| Collaborators | Online versus retail versus D2C | Modern trade, kirana stores, distributors with cold or dry storage |
| Competitors | Rival phones in the same price band | Existing cereals, and also poha, idli and parathas |
| Context: heaviest letter | Technological (5G vs 4G) | Social (health and organic trends) |

<div class="callout">
<p class="callout-title">Key idea: frameworks travel</p>

The 5Cs and PESTLE do not belong to any industry. If a product has seasonal demand, give that more weight; if it is perishable, look harder at costs and collaborators. The skill is not memorising the list: it is knowing which boxes matter most for the problem in front of you.

<strong>On a manager's desk.</strong> A manager who has learned one framework well can enter an unfamiliar category without panic.
