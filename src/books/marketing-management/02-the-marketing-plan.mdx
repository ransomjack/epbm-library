The first part of the orientation class read the situation with the 5Cs. This part turns to the decisions: what a marketing manager actually decides, the two frameworks those decisions fall into, why one must come before the other, and what happens when they do not line up.

## What does a marketing manager decide?

The next question sounded simple: on a day-to-day basis, what <strong>decisions</strong> does a marketing manager take? The professor insisted that every answer be framed as a decision. "Communication" is not a decision; "which language to communicate in" is. That one rule sorted the class's answers into two piles.

| Answer from class | As a decision | Comment |
|---|---|---|
| Production and demand | How much to produce? | Marketing gives estimates to production periodically, not a daily marketing decision. |
| Market selection, geography | Which markets and segments to enter? | A real decision (targeting). |
| Target audience | Which customers to serve? | A real decision (targeting). |
| Advertising | How, where and on which platform to advertise? | A real decision (promotion). |
| Positioning | How do we want the brand to be seen? | A real decision (positioning). |
| Promotions | Which offer or discount to run this week? | A real decision (sales promotion / price). |
| Distribution | Which channel, which distributor? | A real decision (place). |
| Budget | How much to spend, and where? | A real decision (promotion budget). |
| Acquisition cost | How much to spend to acquire a customer? | A real decision, and the root of CLV ([Calculating customer lifetime value](/marketing-management/what-a-customer-is-worth/#calculating-customer-lifetime-value)). |
| Packaging | What packaging to use? | A real decision (product). |
| Launch timing | When to launch, and when to pull out? | A real decision: timing and duration in the market. |
| Features | What features and capabilities to offer? | A real decision (product). |
| Communication | In which language, with what message? | A real decision (promotion). |
| Identifying the USP | How to differentiate? | A real decision (positioning). |
| Competitor analysis, market research |  | <strong>Not decisions.</strong> Activities that feed decisions. The professor's question back: to take what decision? |
| Revenue tracking |  | <strong>Not a decision.</strong> A measurement. |

<div class="callout trap">
<p class="callout-title">An activity is not a decision</p>

Market research, competitor analysis and revenue tracking are important, but they are inputs. Every time a participant offered one, the professor asked: to take what decision? A good marketing plan is a list of choices (whom to serve, what to offer, at what price, through whom, with what message) and everything else exists to inform those choices.

</div>

A participant then asked the professor to sort the decisions into a framework, and two frameworks fell out of the list: STP and the 4Ps.

<strong>On a manager's desk.</strong> Review your own marketing plan and underline the decisions. If most of the page is activities ("conduct research", "analyse competitors", "track performance") it is a to-do list, not a plan. This holds for a product launch and for a service proposal alike: the client wants to know what you chose and why, not how busy you were.

## STP and the 4Ps

The slide titled <strong>Marketing Management (The Plan</strong> put the whole module on one line: developing the action plan to create value involves three key activities) situation analysis (the 5Cs), developing strategy (STP) and designing tactics (the 4Ps).

### STP: the strategy

| Step | What it means (from the slide) | Example |
|---|---|---|
| Segmentation | Identifying groups of customers with similar characteristics, preferences or behaviour (segments). | Divide India by geography (north, south, east, west) or by age, income or usage. |
| Targeting | Deciding which segments to serve. | One segment, a few, or all of them. "Market selection" belongs here. |
| Positioning | Developing a distinct image in the minds of target customers. | The image you want to own: value, luxury, innovation, safety. Differentiation and the USP support it. |

### The 4Ps: the tactics (the marketing mix)

The slide called the 4Ps the <strong>exchange facilitators</strong>: the tools that make the exchange between firm and customer happen. The professor placed the class's decisions into them.

| P | Decisions it covers | Class decisions that belong here |
|---|---|---|
| Product | Features, quality, variants, packaging, brand | Features and capabilities; packaging |
| Price | List price, price points, discounts, payment terms | Launch price; discount offers |
| Place | Channels: where and how the customer can buy | Distribution; which distributor; online or retail |
| Promotion | Advertising <strong>and</strong> sales promotion; media; budget | Where and how to advertise; campaigns; budget |

<div class="callout trap">
<p class="callout-title">Place means channels, not markets</p>

A participant put "market selection" under Place. The professor corrected it: Place refers to <strong>channels</strong> (how the product reaches the customer). Choosing which market to enter is <strong>targeting</strong>, part of STP. Likewise, packaging belongs to <strong>Product</strong> (some argue it is so important it should be a fifth P, but the class did not go there), and "how to differentiate" belongs to <strong>positioning</strong>.

</div>

<div class="callout beyond">
<p class="callout-title">Good to know: more Ps for services</p>

Someone suggested "People". The professor noted that people do matter, and that a later session on services covers them. In services, the mix is usually extended to <strong>7Ps</strong>: the original four plus People, Process and Physical evidence, because a service is produced and consumed in the same moment, by staff the customer can see.

</div>

<strong>On a manager's desk.</strong> Keep two separate pages in any plan: one for the strategic choices (who, and what image) and one for the mix (what, at what price, where, with what message). A product team that argues about price before agreeing whom it is for will argue forever. A service team pitching to a new client sector should settle the segment and the positioning first; the service catalogue and rate card follow.

## Which comes first? STP before the 4Ps

Is there an order to these decisions? Most of the class answered quickly: STP first. One participant argued for product first: surely you need a product before you can choose whom to sell it to? The professor treated this as a fair point and drew a useful line.

The <strong>broad product idea</strong> (we are entering the SUV market, not the sedan market) is a business decision taken with people from many functions, not by the marketing manager alone. Within that, the marketing manager's product decisions are more specific: what kind of airbags, what music system, which features. Those come <strong>after</strong> the segment and positioning are clear, because they have to serve them.

|   | STP | The 4Ps |
|---|---|---|
| Nature | Strategic | Tactical / operational |
| Time horizon | Long term | Shorter term, adjusted often |
| How easy to change | Hard and risky: an image takes years to build | Easier: a price can move with competition |
| Role | Decides whom you serve and what you stand for | Delivers that choice in the market |
| Inputs | The 5Cs | The 5Cs, and the STP choices already made |

Positioning is strategic because it must be <strong>consistent</strong>: the same image has to show up in every ad, in where the product is sold, in how it is priced and packaged, for years. A price, by contrast, can be changed next month if a competitor moves.

### Repositioning: very risky territory

Changing a brand's positioning once it is established is called <strong>repositioning</strong>, and the professor called it very risky territory. Once customers hold an image of a brand, moving it is slow, costly and often fails. Many case studies are about brands that spent heavily to change their image and did not succeed.

Her example was <strong>Maruti</strong>. What does Maruti stand for in India? The class answered at once: economy, value for money, low maintenance. When Maruti tried to stretch into a lifestyle off-roader with the <strong>Jimny</strong>, it struggled: an established image of sensible economy does not easily carry a car bought for adventure and style. (A participant noted he liked the Jimny, which is exactly the point: a good product does not overcome a positioning mismatch for most buyers.)

<div class="callout beyond">
<p class="callout-title">Good to know: the Jimny in numbers</p>

The five-door Jimny was launched in India in mid-2023. Domestic sales were widely reported to be far below early expectations, and dealers offered large discounts to move stock, even as exports from India did well. Treat this as background, not class material.

</div>

<div class="callout trap">
<p class="callout-title">Repositioning is not a new ad campaign</p>

It is tempting to think a brand can be repositioned by changing its advertising. The image sits in customers' minds, built by years of product, price, place and promotion together. Changing one ad rarely moves it; changing it properly means changing much of the mix, and spending heavily with no guarantee.

</div>

<strong>On a manager's desk.</strong> Get positioning right the first time, because the second time is expensive. A product company entering a premium segment under a value brand should ask whether a separate brand is safer. A service company known as the low-cost option will find it hard to sell a premium advisory practice under the same name without changing who delivers it, how it is priced and where it is sold.

## Everything must line up: Apple and Samsung

To show what alignment means, the professor asked a very local question. Sitting on the Joka campus, if you wanted to walk into a store and hold the latest iPhone before buying it, how far would you have to travel? The answers ran to well over ten kilometres, to a mall in the city, to a store selling Apple products. And a Samsung? Much closer: almost any mobile shop or electronics chain would have one.

Is one of these strategies good and the other bad? Neither. Each <strong>follows from its own STP</strong>.

|   | Apple | Samsung |
|---|---|---|
| Target | Premium customers | Customers at many price points |
| Positioning | Innovation and a controlled customer experience | A phone for everyone, from flagship to budget |
| Product | A narrow range of premium models | A wide range across price bands |
| Price | High at launch | Spread from premium to entry level |
| Place | Few, tightly controlled outlets: Apple's own stores and selected authorised resellers | Everywhere: multi-brand chains, electronics stores, neighbourhood mobile shops, online |
| Why Place looks like this | Control over the experience protects the image | Lower-priced models need mass availability to sell |

Apple wants to own customer experience and innovation, so it wants control, so it keeps distribution narrow. Samsung sells phones at many price points, so it must be available wherever those customers shop. Product, price, place and promotion all have to be aligned to the brand's STP: who the target is and what image it wants.

> There is no right or wrong. If your targeting and positioning are clear and everything is well aligned, it works.
>
> <small>The professor, paraphrased: the line she repeated through the class</small>

### The great product nobody was targeting

The professor warned of a common pattern: a company builds a great product and only then starts wondering whom to sell it to, believing that because the product is so good it will sell on its own. A participant asked for an example of a product that was great but still failed. The professor suggested looking up a small car Ford built (she named the SportKa): a well-made car that did not have a clear segment strategy. Then she offered a closer-to-home example: the Tata Nano.

<strong>On a manager's desk.</strong> Before approving any single P, check it against the STP. A premium product sold through a discount channel, or a budget product advertised as a luxury, confuses customers and wastes money. A service firm positioned on deep specialist expertise should not be selling through a generic reseller's rate card. Alignment matters more than any one brilliant decision.

## Great product, wrong fit: the Tata Nano

Why did the Tata Nano fail? The first answer from the class was the one most people give: it was positioned as the cheapest car. The professor pushed further. Did Tata <strong>intend</strong> to position it as the cheapest car? And how would you even know what positioning a company intends?

You look at its <strong>communication</strong>: what the company says about the brand in its own advertising. So the class looked at the Nano's launch communication. Who was the target? Lower-middle-income families; small towns, villages and smaller cities; small families; and above all people who did <strong>not</strong> yet own a car: families riding a two-wheeler who would move up to four wheels. And what did the ads stress? That the car was surprisingly spacious and robust. Not that it was the lowest-priced car.

So where did "cheapest car" come from? It came from the media. Because the Nano really was the lowest-priced car in the world, it drew enormous attention. Ratan Tata was interviewed constantly, by Indian and international media, about the Rs. 1 lakh car. The coverage, not the company, fixed the image: a poor man's car. And in India a car is an aspirational purchase: a status symbol. Nobody wants to be seen arriving in the cheapest car on the road.

<div class="callout">
<p class="callout-title">Intended positioning versus actual positioning</p>

A company can only <strong>intend</strong> a position. The position a brand actually holds is whatever image forms in customers' minds: shaped by ads, but also by media, word of mouth and experience. The Nano's ads said spacious and robust; the world heard cheapest.

</div>

### Where the execution did not line up

The professor then showed that positioning was the biggest reason but not the only one. The car itself was not bad for what it cost. The trouble was that the 4Ps did not line up with the target segment.

| What the STP intended | What actually happened | The consequence |
|---|---|---|
| Target: families who did not own a car | The first cars were allotted by <strong>lottery</strong>, and many went to people who already owned a car | Owners compared the Nano with their bigger cars and found it wanting: early <strong>negative word of mouth</strong> |
| Target: small towns and rural buyers | The dealer network in those towns was thin at the time | Rural buyers were expected to travel to the city to buy, which rarely happened |
| Position: a real car for a new car owner | Media framed it as the cheapest car | An aspirational buyer did not want the poor man's car |
| A reliable first car | Reports of cars catching fire; safety questions | Further damage to an already fragile image |

There were other blows too. Production had to move from Singur in West Bengal to Sanand in Gujarat after political protests over land, delaying everything. Later, Tata tried to reposition the Nano (ads showing young people and college students, more features and a sportier look) but once an image has stuck, changing it is extremely hard ([Which comes first? STP before the 4Ps](#which-comes-first-stp-before-the-4ps)).

<div class="callout trap">
<p class="callout-title">Not just a positioning failure</p>

"It was positioned as the cheapest car" is the start of the answer, not the whole answer. The deeper lesson is <strong>misalignment</strong>: the target was first-time rural buyers, but the lottery, the dealer network and the communication environment all worked against reaching them. A good product with a clear segment still fails if the 4Ps do not serve that segment.

</div>

<strong>On a manager's desk.</strong> Plan for the image you will be given, not just the one you intend. A product that is newsworthy for its price will be described by its price, so decide early whether you can live with that label. A service firm that wins attention as the "cheapest bidder" faces the same trap: the label follows it into every later negotiation, and the clients it really wanted will not shortlist it.

## How to analyse a case

Much of this module is taught through cases, so the professor spent time on how to read one. She asked the class first, and the answers built a simple method: matching her slide of general guidelines.

<Fig title="Three steps for any case, in any subject">
  <Steps items={[
    { title: "Identify the problem", body: "Find the real dilemma: read the opening and closing paragraphs closely. Break it into parts and list probable reasons." },
    { title: "Generate options", body: "Two or three genuine alternatives, each with its pros and cons. This is where your thinking is judged." },
    { title: "Recommend and justify", body: "Pick one, say why it beats the others, and support it with the case's own numbers." },
  ]} />
</Fig>

| Guideline (from the slide) | What it means in practice |
|---|---|
| Identify the problems | Find the real dilemma and state it clearly. Break a broad problem ("sales are falling") into parts (is it the product, the price, the distribution?) and list probable reasons. |
| Generate alternatives (pros and cons) | Two or three real options, each with honest pros and cons. This is where your thinking is judged. |
| Sound arguments for your decision | Recommend one option and justify why it beats the others. |
| A reasonable approach to problem and solution | Evaluators check whether you considered the key points, not whether you reached "their" answer. |
| Thoughtful interpretation of text and numbers | Use the case's data wherever possible to support your argument. |
| No one correct answer | Different recommendations can all score well if well argued. |

<div class="callout">
<p class="callout-title">The professor's reading tip</p>

Do not try to read every line with equal care. The main dilemma a case writer wants you to focus on is almost always stated in the <strong>introduction</strong>, the <strong>conclusion</strong>, or both: writers like to close the loop by repeating it at the end. Read those two parts closely, then read the body for evidence.

</div>

<div class="callout trap">
<p class="callout-title">Stay inside the case</p>

The slide's note is explicit: restrict yourself to the information given in the case. Do not bring in what you found online about what the company did later. And do not stretch an inference into a fact: in the Axe discussion, a participant claimed a rival was taking Axe's volume (a reasonable guess, but the case did not say so, and the professor flagged it as an assumption).

</div>

<strong>On a manager's desk.</strong> The same three steps make a good board paper. A product manager proposing a price cut, or a service head proposing a new offering, should state the problem, lay out the real options with their costs and risks, and only then recommend, with numbers. Leaders trust a recommendation more when they can see the options that were rejected.

## Lessons from the first case: a leader losing its magic

The first case of the module was <strong>Axe</strong>, Unilever's deodorant brand in India. The case is not retold here; what matters is how the class worked through it, because the same steps apply to any case. The situation in three lines: a first mover and long-time leader, aimed at men of about 18 to 25 and positioned on seduction (the "Axe effect"), with ads that grabbed attention but offended many. Competitors copied the theme until the category was full of me-too brands, and then <strong>Fogg</strong> arrived on a functional platform (no gas, more sprays, lasts longer) and Axe began losing ground. The dilemma: should Axe move to a new positioning, <strong>"Find Your Magic"</strong>, which celebrates individuality instead of seduction, and would a new positioning be enough?

The professor framed the discussion two ways: if you had been Axe's brand manager, what would you have done differently back then? And what should Axe do now? The class put several options on the table, and she pressed each one for its pros and cons.

### Option 1: Broaden the target (families, and women)

One participant argued Axe had targeted too narrowly; deodorant is a universal need, so why not target whole families, including women?

| Pros | Cons |
|---|---|
| A much larger customer base; more volume | You lose your sharpness: try to be something to everybody and you end up being nothing to anyone |
| Several users in one home; more frequent purchase | Axe's image is strongly masculine: how many women would openly buy it? This needs research before any money is spent |
| The case notes some women already used it | It moves Axe towards a me-too position, losing its differentiation |

Here the professor brought in an example that cleared up the debate. What is the difference between <strong>Diet Coke</strong> and <strong>Coke Zero</strong>? The benefits are nearly the same; the taste differs a little. Diet Coke came first, on a health platform, and sold hugely, but almost entirely to women. Men stayed away from the silver can; it had a feminine image. Rather than stretch Diet Coke, Coca-Cola created Coke Zero, in a black can, with a masculine image, to reach men. Two brands with similar benefits means cannibalisation and double marketing costs, yet it was worth it to reach a segment the first brand could not.

<div class="callout">
<p class="callout-title">The lesson for Axe</p>

If you want a segment your brand's image repels, a separate brand or sub-brand is often safer than stretching the parent. Axe's masculine image could make it the wrong vehicle for women: just as Diet Coke's feminine image made it the wrong vehicle for men.

</div>

### Option 2: Keep the positioning, advertise harder

Another suggestion was to keep the same positioning and simply spend more on advertising. The professor asked what would actually change. Spending more on a message that is already losing is doing the same thing louder.

### Option 3: Extend the brand (a premium line)

The most developed argument came from a participant who looked at timing. When Axe was leader, its 18-to-25 customers were students without much money. A few years later the same people had jobs and were buying perfumes, and Axe had nothing to offer them. As brand manager he would have used the Axe name, which everyone in the age group knew, to launch a premium perfume line that customers could graduate into, with a more sophisticated positioning. This is a <strong>brand extension</strong>: a new product under the existing brand name.

The professor's counter was practical. Proposed today, while the parent brand is struggling, no top management will approve a brand extension: you cannot build on a weak base. It would need a very solid proposal backed by research showing real potential. Proposed back when Axe was strong, it would have been a smart, <strong>proactive</strong> move.

### Option 4: Act like a leader (grow the whole category)

This led to the professor's main point about leaders. A first mover with high awareness carries a responsibility: to educate customers and grow the entire market, not just defend its share. Axe could have gone after new segments, new geographies (rural markets with smaller bottles and trial sizes at lower price points), new forms (roll-ons, pocket sizes, talc) and new variants and fragrances. Many of these came, but late. Instead of acting while it was strong, Axe ended up reacting.

### Option 5: Reposition with "Find Your Magic"

Several participants supported the new positioning. Society has moved on; the idea of what it means to be a man has changed: more responsible, more open-minded. A seduction message looks dated. The professor agreed the messaging had to change, but asked whether a new campaign alone would be enough. There are two ways to reposition: change only the <strong>communication</strong> and keep the product the same, or also change the <strong>product, packaging and the rest of the mix</strong>. Axe's image is deeply masculine and attention-seeking; a new ad alone may not shift it ([Which comes first? STP before the 4Ps](#which-comes-first-stp-before-the-4ps)).

### Option 6: Innovate the product

Others argued that Fogg had won on a functional benefit (longer-lasting, no gas) so Axe needed R&D: a better-performing product, perhaps skin-friendly variants, rather than a better ad.

<div class="callout">
<p class="callout-title">Where the class landed</p>

There was no single right answer, and the professor did not give one. What she asked for was the approach: state the problem, weigh real options with pros and cons, and support the choice with evidence. Her own first step would be to <strong>get more data</strong>: research with users and non-users: why do people buy Axe, and why do people who don't, not buy it?

</div>

<strong>On a manager's desk.</strong> Leaders lose by standing still more often than by being attacked. A product leader should use its strength to open the next segment before a challenger does, including a premium line for customers who are growing up. A service leader has the same duty: a market-leading security provider that does not educate the market about new threats or offer the next service tier will find a focused challenger doing it instead.

## The product life cycle

The Axe story becomes clearer with a concept the professor drew on the board: the <strong>product life cycle</strong>. Plot a product's sales against time and the curve usually passes through four stages.

<Fig title="The product life cycle" caption="Sales rise slowly, then steeply, flatten, and fall. Profit lags sales at first and peaks earlier.">
  <LifeCycle />
</Fig>

| Stage | What happens | Marketing objective | Typical moves |
|---|---|---|---|
| Introduction | Sales start slowly; profits are negative or thin | Create awareness; grab attention | Basic product; attention-grabbing, even comparative advertising (Samsung) |
| Growth | Sales and profits rise steeply; competitors notice | Build preference and a positive attitude | Add variants and features; widen distribution |
| Maturity | Sales plateau; many competitors; me-too offers | Defend and differentiate | Competitive pricing (prices may fall); augment the product with services, models and features |
| Decline | Sales and profits fall | Squeeze out remaining sales or exit | Aggressive promotions to move volume; decide when to pull out |

Two lessons came out of applying this to Axe. First, <strong>advertising objectives must move with the stage</strong>. At introduction the job is awareness, and even offensive ads can grab it. But a brand cannot stay there; it has to move on to building a positive attitude. Axe's ads created awareness but never created that positive attitude: many people found them offensive.

Second, <strong>timing</strong>. Fogg arrived as Axe's growth was flattening. The professor's advice: the moment your sales begin to plateau (better still, while they are still climbing) is when you launch the next product, segment or variant. Waiting until decline means reacting from weakness.

<div class="callout trap">
<p class="callout-title">The curve is a description, not a destiny</p>

The life cycle describes what tends to happen if a firm does nothing new. It is not a law. Leaders who keep extending the product (new segments, forms, variants) can stretch maturity for decades. Note also that profit and sales do not peak together: profit usually peaks before sales, as competition squeezes prices in maturity.

</div>

<strong>On a manager's desk.</strong> Know which stage each of your offerings is in, because the right move differs. A product in growth needs distribution and preference, not discounts; one in maturity needs differentiation and services wrapped around it. Services age too: a managed service that was novel five years ago may now be a commodity sold on price (the time to build the next tier was while it was still growing).
