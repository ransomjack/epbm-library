The orientation class said marketing starts from the customer. Session 1 asked a harder question: once you know your customer, can you influence how that customer decides? The class ran live experiments on itself to find out, and discovered six mental shortcuts that marketers put to work. This chapter covers how buyers really decide and the first two shortcuts, anchoring and framing. The next chapter covers the other four.

## Two views of the buyer

Session 0 said marketing starts from the customer. This session asks a harder question: once you know your customer, can you <strong>influence</strong> how that customer decides? To answer it, you first need a theory of how people decide at all. The professor put two opposing theories on the table.

<strong>The economist's view.</strong> Asked what economists assume about human behaviour, participants answered: buyers look for maximum benefit at minimum cost; if the price goes up, demand falls. Exactly. The economist's buyer is <strong>rational</strong>, with no emotion attached. Shown a set of options, he weighs the pros and cons of each and picks the one that maximises his <strong>utility</strong>: his overall satisfaction. And because he is rational, his behaviour is <strong>predictable</strong>: put him in a situation and you can say what he will do.

<strong>The psychologist's view</strong> is the opposite extreme. Behaviour is <strong>subjective</strong>. It depends on upbringing, culture, ethnicity, household income, knowledge, education: everything that makes one person different from the next. So nothing about human behaviour can be predicted.

Picture two people at a kirana counter choosing a packet of biscuits. The economist sees two calculators who will both buy the best value per gram. The psychologist sees a grandmother who buys the brand her mother bought and a teenager who buys whatever his friends are eating, and says you could never have guessed either.

<strong>On a manager's desk.</strong> Neither extreme helps a manager. If buyers were perfectly rational, all you would need is the best value; if they were perfectly unpredictable, marketing would be guesswork. The useful middle is that buyers are predictably imperfect. A product company can design its price tags and shelves around those imperfections; a service company can design its proposals, renewals and waiting experiences around them.

## Bounded rationality and behavioural economics

What researchers actually found is that human behaviour lies somewhere between the two views. They called it <strong>bounded rationality</strong>: people try to be rational, but within limits. The economist's model quietly assumes three things, and in real life each one often fails.

| The economist assumes the buyer has... | In reality | Example from class |
|---|---|---|
| The cognitive ability to compare every option | Many people cannot, or will not, do the maths | Chips: 100 g for Rs. 10, 90 g for one price, 120 g for Rs. 12. Which is the best value per gram? Few shoppers calculate. |
| All the time needed to evaluate every option | Shopping time is limited | A shopper with ten minutes in a supermarket cannot compare forty products. |
| Complete information | Some options or facts are missing | The better-value pack may not be on this shelf at all. |

Because of these limits, people fall back on <strong>heuristics</strong>: mental shortcuts that make a decision quick and good enough. A heuristic does not deliver the optimal decision; it delivers a <strong>satisficing</strong> one (satisfy + suffice): acceptable, not best. And because the shortcuts work in predictable ways, they also produce predictable errors, called <strong>biases</strong>.

<div class="callout">
<p class="callout-title">Heuristic and bias</p>

<strong>Heuristic:</strong> a mental shortcut used to make a decision quickly ("the expensive one must be better", "I'll take the one I've heard of").

<strong>Bias:</strong> the systematic error a heuristic produces (paying more than you needed to, or misjudging a risk).

</div>

### The field that studies this: behavioural economics

The whole area that showed people are neither the economist's machine nor the psychologist's mystery is <strong>behavioural economics</strong>: a mix of economics and psychology. Before 2000 it was a niche. It took off after <strong>2002</strong>, when <strong>Daniel Kahneman</strong>, a psychologist, won the Nobel Prize in economics for his work, with his co-authors, showing that people use heuristics in decision making. His book <em>Thinking, Fast and Slow</em> describes many of the heuristics in this session. The professor also pointed to <em>Nudge</em>, and to the work of Richard Thaler and Dan Ariely.

<div class="callout beyond">
<p class="callout-title">Good to know: who won what</p>

Kahneman shared the 2002 Nobel. Richard Thaler, co-author of <em>Nudge</em> with Cass Sunstein, won it in 2017. Dan Ariely, author of <em>Predictably Irrational</em>, is a well-known researcher in the field but has not won a Nobel. The name to remember is <strong>Kahneman, 2002</strong>.

</div>

The marketing logic follows directly. <strong>If we know in advance</strong> which heuristics customers use and which biases follow, we can design offers that work with them, and nudge behaviour in our favour.

### How the class discovered the heuristics

Rather than define each heuristic, the professor ran small experiments. Each slide carried two versions of a question, Questionnaire A and Questionnaire B. Participants answered only one, based on the first letter of their name (A to L, later A to P, read the top half; the rest the bottom half), and typed answers in the chat. The two versions differed in one small detail. Comparing the two groups' answers revealed the shortcut at work. The professor's only request: do not peek at the other half, or the psychology will not show.

<strong>On a manager's desk.</strong> Design for the shopper you have, not the one in the textbook. A product company should assume most buyers will not compute price per gram and will lean on cues: the tag, the shelf, the pack size. A service buyer evaluating three complex proposals is just as bounded: a clear headline price and one comparison table often decide the deal more than the fifty pages behind them.

## Anchoring: the number that sets the scale

The first experiment described a food engineer in Melbourne, Australia, with a stated level of experience and qualifications, and asked participants whether his annual salary was above or below a given figure, and then for their own estimate. Both groups read the same engineer, the same city, the same experience. The only difference was the figure in the question: Questionnaire A carried a lower one, Questionnaire B a higher one.

<Fig title="The class's own anchoring result" caption="Same engineer, different suggested figure, different estimate. Average estimates of his annual salary, approximate.">
  <Bars unit="thousand a year" rows={[
    { label: "Questionnaire A", value: 90, note: "shown a lower figure" },
    { label: "Questionnaire B", value: 120, note: "shown a higher figure", mark: true },
  ]} />
</Fig>

Group A's estimates averaged about 90K; Group B's about 120K. Nothing about the engineer had changed. Whatever number was put in front of people became a <strong>reference point</strong>, and they estimated around it: a little above or a little below, but anchored to it. A participant called it benchmarking; the heuristic's name is <strong>anchoring</strong>.

<div class="callout">
<p class="callout-title">Anchoring (definition on the slide)</p>

The anchoring effect occurs when people consider a particular value for an unknown quantity before estimating the quantity or number. Whatever value comes first pulls the estimate towards it.

</div>

How strong is it? The professor described a famous kind of experiment. Before asking people how much they would pay for a pen or a phone, researchers asked them to write down the last digits of an unrelated number: in the original studies, their US social security number; in Indian terms, think of the last four digits of an Aadhaar number. People whose digits were higher quoted higher prices. The number had nothing to do with the product, and it still worked.

A participant asked whether anchoring affects negotiations: if one side opens with a very high number, does it help? Yes. The first number on the table in a negotiation or an auction becomes the anchor the rest of the discussion moves around.

<div class="callout trap">
<p class="callout-title">Anchoring is about estimating an unknown value</p>

Anchoring applies when a customer must estimate something he does not know: a fair price, a wait time, how many to buy. It is mostly used in pricing, but not every pricing trick is anchoring. The professor redirected several suggestions to their own, more specific names: popcorn pricing is the decoy effect ([The decoy: adding a dominated alternative](/marketing-management/more-mental-shortcuts/#the-decoy-adding-a-dominated-alternative)), Rs. 999 is the left-digit effect ([More price cues the class asked about](/marketing-management/more-mental-shortcuts/#more-price-cues-the-class-asked-about)), and "only 2 left" is scarcity. They can overlap with anchoring, but each has a better label.

</div>

<strong>On a manager's desk.</strong> Whoever sets the first number frames the deal. A product company should show a reference price before the selling price. A service firm in a negotiation should table its own number first (a fully scoped option, priced properly) rather than wait for the client's budget figure to become the anchor.

## Anchoring at work

Asked for applications, the class and the professor built a long list. The slide grouped them under five headings: regular price versus sale price, purchase limits, placement of products, Disney's queues, and restaurant menus.

| Application | How the anchor works | Example from class |
|---|---|---|
| Regular price vs sale price | Show the original price, then the sale price, instead of just stating a discount. The higher number becomes the yardstick, even when buyers suspect it is inflated. | Amazon and Myntra: Rs. 4,999 struck through, Rs. 2,499 below. In the US, "was / now" tags. |
| List price | A list price nobody actually pays makes the negotiated price look like a win. | Real estate and cars: "the list price is X, I'm giving you 15-20% off". |
| Competitor's price | Place the rival's higher price next to yours. | "Their price / our price" comparisons. |
| Purchase limits | A limit suggests a quantity. | A hypermarket offered cookies at a modest 20% off, "maximum 4 per customer". The professor bought 4; without the line she would have bought 2 or 3. |
| Placement of products | The first models a shopper sees reset what seems normal to pay. | Electronics stores put lakh-rupee TVs at the entrance; a shopper who came in thinking Rs. 40-50K for a 55-inch set leaves willing to pay Rs. 70K. |
| Showing above the budget | Seeing a better product above your range moves your range. | Sari shops ask your range (say Rs. 3,000-6,000), then show you Rs. 8,000-10,000 pieces "just to see the collection" (and you buy above your range). |
| Expected waiting time | A stated wait becomes the anchor for how the real wait feels. | Disney's popular rides show about 30 minutes; your turn comes in 25 and you feel you saved 5. |
| Padded schedules | A generous scheduled time makes arrival feel early. | Airlines publish a longer flight time than the actual flying time, then arrive "ahead of schedule". The professor credited IndiGo with popularising this; other airlines followed. |
| The menu anchor | One absurdly priced item makes the rest look reasonable. | A dessert menu: cheesecake at $22.50 looks steep, until the last line, a Guinness-record $1,000 sundae needing 48 hours' notice. Nobody orders it; it exists to make the cheesecake look fine. |

> You know the original price is not the true price, but you still use it to judge how much you are paying.
>
> <small>The professor on strike-through prices, paraphrased</small>

Participants raised more. <strong>No-cost EMI</strong>: buyers anchor on the small monthly figure (partly anchoring, the professor agreed, if it serves as the reference that shapes the decision). A question on real estate: an inflated list price anchors a buyer who can afford less. And from the professor, a note that all sales promotions can be seen through the anchoring lens, but some have sharper names of their own.

<strong>On a manager's desk.</strong> Choose your anchors deliberately, because customers will find one anyway. A product company controls the first price, the first shelf and the first model a shopper sees. A service company controls the first number in its proposal and the stated turnaround: promise a 4-hour response and deliver in 2, and the client feels served; promise 1 and deliver in 2, and the same performance feels like failure.

## Framing: same facts, different frame

The second experiment was harder to answer. A 65-year-old relative has a serious disease that affects her lifestyle significantly. An operation, if successful, will cure her, but it is risky. Questionnaire A said <strong>50% of patients undergoing it survive</strong>. Questionnaire B said <strong>50% of patients undergoing it die</strong>. Would you recommend she undergo it?

Group A answered mostly yes. Group B answered mostly no: only about five yeses. The two statements are mathematically identical. What changed was the frame: "survive" presents the operation as a <strong>gain</strong>; "die" presents it as a <strong>loss</strong>. The professor added that this result is not peculiar to this class; it shows up wherever the exercise is run.

<div class="callout">
<p class="callout-title">Framing effect (definition on the slide)</p>

A judgement bias resulting from the way the choice is presented. The information is the same; the decision changes depending on whether it is framed as a gain or as a loss.

</div>

### The same news, two headlines

| The fact | Positive frame | Negative frame |
|---|---|---|
| 2012 US Federal Reserve stress test of 19 big banks (a test of whether banks would survive a 2008-type downturn) | "15 of the 19 big banks pass" (the New York Times version, optimistic) | "Stress test fails 4 of the 19 big banks" (an Indian paper's version, worrying) |
| 2016: a UN list of addresses in Pakistan for a wanted fugitive | "6 of the addresses confirmed" | "3 of the addresses found incorrect" |
| COVID second doses: about 8% of people who took the first shot skipped the second | "92% are coming back for their second dose" (more motivating for those on the fence) | "Millions are skipping their second doses" (the headline actually used) |
| Meat (the slide's example) | "90 percent lean meat" | "10 percent fat meat" |

The COVID example was the professor's own observation: if officials wanted more people to return for the second dose, framing the same fact as "92% are coming back" would have worked better than headlining the 8% who did not. Food brands use the gain frame on packs: "90% fat free" rather than "contains 10% fat". A participant suggested sanitiser claims ("kills 99% of germs"); the professor said he was thinking in the right direction (the contrast would be "fails to kill 1%") but the meat example is cleaner.

<div class="callout trap">
<p class="callout-title">Framing is something the company does</p>

A participant asked whether the Tata Nano's "cheapest car" image was framing. The professor said no. Framing is a choice the <strong>company</strong> makes in how it presents information in its own communication. The Nano's cheap image was created by media coverage, not by Tata's framing: that is a question of positioning, which the module returns to in the session on positioning.

</div>

<strong>On a manager's desk.</strong> Write every claim twice (as a gain and as a loss) and choose deliberately. A product company should say "lasts 90% longer" rather than "fails less often". A service firm reporting to a client should lead with "99.2% of alerts triaged within SLA" and then explain the rest, rather than opening with "0.8% breached" (while never hiding the number itself).

## Framing a promotion: 33% more or 33% off?

Framing matters most in sales promotions. The professor showed two Coke bottles: one offering <strong>33% more</strong> for the same price, the other <strong>33% off</strong> the price. Which would you choose? Most of the class chose 33% more. Research finds the same: roughly a 60:40 split in favour of "more".

Then she asked the real question: which one leaves you better off? Not "which saves money", but on a like-for-like basis: <strong>rupees per litre</strong>. Several participants got tangled until the arithmetic was laid out:

| Offer | What you get | Price per litre | Equivalent discount |
|---|---|---|---|
| Regular | 1 litre for Rs. 100 | Rs. 100 |  |
| 33% off | 1 litre for Rs. 67 | Rs. 67 | 33% |
| 33% more | 1.33 litres for Rs. 100 | 100 / 1.33 = Rs. 75 | 25% |
| 25% more | 1.25 litres for Rs. 100 | 100 / 1.25 = Rs. 80 | 20% |

So the rational buyer should take 33% off, yet most people take 33% more. Why? A price is felt as a <strong>loss</strong>; a discount is only a reduced loss, and it still feels like a loss. "More" feels like a <strong>gain</strong> (you are getting something) and people prefer gains to reduced losses. For the company the lesson is pointed: a "25% more" offer can attract as much as a "33% off" one while costing the equivalent of only 20% off.

Not everyone is the same, of course. One participant preferred the discount because she did not want to be pushed into consuming more; another would take "more" because he was hosting guests. The point is not that everyone falls for the gain frame, but that the split moves in its favour.

### A frame that grew a bank

The class watched a Kotak Mahindra Bank advertisement built entirely on framing. A string of characters reframes facts ("recession is a state of mind", "80% of millionaires drive second-hand cars") and it ends with the bank's own offer: don't look at a savings rate as <strong>2% more</strong>, look at it as <strong>50% extra</strong> (6% interest instead of 4%). According to the professor, the campaign grew the bank's retail customer base by about 20% within two months.

<div class="callout trap">
<p class="callout-title">"33% more" is not the same as "33% off"</p>

Extra quantity must be converted into an effective discount: new price per unit = old price / (1 + extra share). 33% more is about 25% off; 25% more is 20% off; 50% more is about 33% off. Always compare price per unit, never the headline percentages.

</div>

<strong>On a manager's desk.</strong> Frame promotions as gains where you can. A product company running a bonus pack can offer "20% extra" more cheaply than a 20% price cut and often sell more. A service firm can frame a renewal incentive as "three extra months of monitoring" or "one more site covered" rather than "10% off" (the client feels a gain, and the price list stays intact).
