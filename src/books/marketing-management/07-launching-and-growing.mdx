The second half of Session 2 turned from research to a launch: a novel, low-priced product that became a runaway success. Four ideas came out of that discussion: how to price and promote a launch, what makes a category low-involvement, how to keep an advantage that others can copy, and where to grow next.

## Lessons from the case: pricing and promoting a novel product

The session's case was a novel hard-boiled candy launched by a company known for other products, with a strong existing distribution network. It became a runaway success. The case is not retold here; the class drew four general lessons from it.

### 1. Should a new product be priced high or low?

The candy was priced at twice the rupee price of the established candies. This dilemma comes with every launch, and the answer depends on several factors.

| Factor | Points towards a higher price | Points towards a lower price |
|---|---|---|
| Competitor entry | Competitors can copy quickly: recover your R&D investment while you can |   |
| Habit |   | In habitual categories (coffee, say), grabbing share first matters most |
| Goal | Margins | Market share and volume |
| Capacity | You know you cannot meet demand at a low price: price high deliberately | Capacity to serve volume |
| Nature of the product | Novelty in a hedonic (pleasure) product; the absolute amount is still tiny even if it is double |   |

A participant justified the price by the candy's greater weight. The professor disagreed that this could carry the decision: most customers cannot perceive a difference of a gram or two, so weight justifies a price only if the company communicates it.

### 2. Why not advertise at launch?

The candy spread by word of mouth and did not run a TV ad until about two years after launch. Why? Participants suggested keeping competitors unaware (the professor: do not assume dumb competitors), and that demand already exceeded supply. The professor's reasons:

| Reason | Explanation |
|---|---|
| Advertising efficiency is low | Candy margins are tiny in absolute terms. Even a regional TV campaign costs crores, recovered only through huge volumes. |
| Availability must come first | If you advertise and the product is not on the shelf, a customer asks once, maybe twice, never a third time. The launch is make-or-break. |
| Word of mouth cannot be planned | In 2016 nobody could know a candy would go viral; the company did not plan on it. |

Advertising does help impulse categories (recall at the counter matters) but only once supply is secure. The professor shared a personal example: a cement company launched smaller 25-kg bags with heavy marketing and a big launch event. Dealers unloading trucks threw the small bags as they threw the big ones; the seals broke, the product was withdrawn, and by the time the improved bags arrived, the buzz had died.

### 3. Test market or national launch?

The case raises the classic dilemma of whether to test-market a product in a limited region before a national launch. A test market reduces risk and helps forecast demand; a national launch reaches the market before competitors can react. The class considered this partly answered by the case's experience of piloting first.

<strong>On a manager's desk.</strong> Launch sequence matters as much as the launch itself. A product company should secure supply and distribution before spending on advertising, and price a novelty to match capacity. A service firm launching a new offering should make sure it can deliver at quality before marketing it: a client who asks and is told "not yet" rarely asks again.

## Low- and high-involvement products

What makes confectionery hard for marketers? Participants listed demand forecasting (stale stock is waste), loyalty to flavours rather than brands, easy copying by competitors and the unorganised market, and the need for high volumes to make up for low margins, which in turn demands strong distribution. These are typical problems of <strong>low-involvement</strong> categories.

<div class="callout">
<p class="callout-title">Involvement</p>

In a low-involvement category the customer invests little time or thought and does not carefully evaluate options. Involvement rises with perceived <strong>risk</strong>: <strong>economic</strong> (how much it costs), <strong>psychological</strong> (what will people think of me: clothes, for example) and <strong>performance</strong> (will it work: a technology product or even a toy).

### Turning a low-involvement purchase into a high-involvement one

Low-involvement buyers switch brands freely and buy on price: if they see no difference between toothpastes, they take whichever is on offer. So marketers try to <strong>raise involvement</strong>: to give buyers a reason to care and compare.

| Tactic | Example from class |
|---|---|
| Introduce an ingredient people should look for | "Does your toothpaste contain salt?" (nobody had thought toothpaste needed salt until the ad asked) |
| Introduce a new technology | Charcoal toothbrushes and toothpaste; flexi-neck brushes: most buyers never understand the science, but feel something better is there and pay Rs. 10 more |
| Dramatise a problem | Ads showing gums bleeding into an apple |

Many of these are gimmicks that later vanish. But the payoff is real: an involved customer does not buy on price alone, builds <strong>loyalty</strong>, and is willing to pay a <strong>higher price</strong>.

A participant asked why brands like Colgate keep advertising when they are already established. Out of sight is out of mind: low-involvement categories benefit greatly from advertising, because buyers choose what they remember at the counter. A smartphone buyer, by contrast, compares features and does not buy because of one ad.

<strong>On a manager's desk.</strong> If customers see no difference, give them one worth noticing. A product company in a commodity category can create involvement through an ingredient story or a visible innovation. A service firm in a commoditised market can do the same by making a hidden quality visible: showing clients the threats it stopped, not just the hours it worked.

## Sustaining a competitive advantage

The case's main dilemma, in the professor's words, was whether the company could <strong>sustain its brand differentiation</strong>: in other words, sustain its competitive advantage when large competitors and local copies were already imitating the product.

Participants suggested closing the demand-supply gap, reacting quickly to market changes, keeping customers engaged and continuing to innovate. The professor's view: product innovation and engagement are necessary, but the product alone will not give a lasting advantage, because it can be copied. The lasting advantage comes from how you <strong>serve the retail channel</strong>.

<div class="callout">
<p class="callout-title">Why the retailer is the battleground</p>

A kirana's scarcest resource is <strong>shelf space</strong>. A shopkeeper will not want two near-identical candies blocking his limited space, unless you give him a reason to stock the copy.

The reason you give him is a <strong>stock-out</strong>. If customers ask for your product and you cannot supply it, he will take the competitor's. So service level: frequent visits (three or four times a week rather than weekly), reliable replenishment, no gaps: is the real moat. Visibility at the counter matters too in an impulse category.

Retailer incentives, such as a best-retailer campaign in a locality, can help if they genuinely motivate shopkeepers.

</div>

Is there legal protection against copies? Not really, in practice: there are processes, but they are difficult to enforce. A participant added the ultimate form of brand strength: a <strong>generic brand</strong>, whose name becomes the category (people say "Xerox" for a photocopy, or ask for "Bisleri" when they mean bottled water).

<strong>On a manager's desk.</strong> When the product can be copied, compete on what is harder to copy: availability, service and relationships. A product company should measure its stock-out rate as closely as its sales. A service firm's version of shelf space is the client's attention and budget: keep earning it through reliability, and never give the client a reason to try the copycat.

## Growth options: the Ansoff matrix

Participants raised other dilemmas from the case: should the company go international, should it extend the brand into other products? These are questions of <strong>growth</strong>, and the professor introduced a simple framework for them: the <strong>Ansoff matrix</strong>, or product-market growth matrix. On one side are existing and new products; on the other, existing and new markets: four ways to grow.

    { t: "Market development", b: "Smaller towns; countries with a large Indian diaspora. Tastes travel poorly elsewhere." },
    { tag: "High risk, high reward", t: "Diversification", b: "A mango drink from a candy brand: new customers, new competitors (Coke, Pepsi). It did not do well.", tone: "bad" },
  ]} />
</Fig>

| Strategy | Product / market | Ideas from class | Risk |
|---|---|---|---|
| Market penetration | Existing product, existing market | Sharper pricing; bulk packs; selling online and in organised retail as well as kirana stores; stealing share; new usage occasions (a juice brand promoting juice for breakfast; a toothpaste brand urging people to brush twice a day) | Low |
| Product development | New product, existing market | More flavours; new formats such as a lollipop | Medium |
| Market development | Existing product, new market | Tier 3 and tier 4 towns; international markets | Medium |
| Diversification | New product, new market | A mango drink from a candy brand: new customers, a different price point and new competitors (Coke, Pepsi) | High |

Diversification is the <strong>high-risk, high-reward</strong> strategy: succeed, and you have a new set of customers in a new category, no longer confined to confectionery; but the risk of failure is high, and the mango drink did not do well. Going international also needs care: food tastes are personal, and foreign buyers' online reviews of the candy called its taste weird. Markets with a large Indian diaspora, such as Singapore, are the natural starting point. A participant added brand partnerships and co-branding as further penetration ideas.

### A side discussion: brand images that stick

A participant asked how one company can sell the same type of product (an SUV, say) both as a status symbol and as a rugged off-roader. The professor's example was Tata cars, which for years struggled to shake off the image of a taxi drivers' brand, because so many of its cars were used as cabs. A single brand rarely stands for both status and utility. (A participant asked whether Tata's ownership of Jaguar Land Rover was a fighter brand; no: a fighter brand is launched to fight low-priced competitors and protect the core brand.)

<strong>On a manager's desk.</strong> List options in all four cells before choosing, and price the risk honestly. A product company usually finds its cheapest growth in penetration. A service firm moving into a new industry with a new service is diversifying, whatever it calls it: start with a pilot.

## The session on a manager's desk

| Idea | In a product business | In a service business |
|---|---|---|
| Watch, don't just ask | P&G watching laundry | Sit in the client's control room |
| Ask the right people | Gillette researched in India, not at MIT | Hear from daily users, not just buyers |
| Social influence | New Coke's lost meaning | Clients' reaction to losing a familiar format |
| Researcher bias | ChotuKool's "what rural buyers should want" | Do not assume small clients want less |
| Continuous surveys | Sleepy Owl's flavours and packs | One question after every interaction |
| Actual buying behaviour | Tropicana's lost orange | Test portal or report changes with real users |
| Launch sequence | Availability before advertising | Deliverability before marketing |
| Involvement | Salt in toothpaste, charcoal brushes | Make hidden quality visible |
| Sustainable advantage | Service to the retail channel | Reliability that keeps the copycat out |
| Ansoff | Penetration first, diversification last | New service plus new industry = diversification |

### The manager's checklist from this session

| # | Discipline | What it looks like on Monday morning |
|---|---|---|
| 1 | Combine says and does | Pair a survey or interview with observation or a behavioural test. |
| 2 | Check the respondents | Make sure the sample matches the target segment and the real conditions of use. |
| 3 | Research the loss, not only the gain | Ask how customers will feel about what is being taken away. |
| 4 | Challenge your own assumptions | Ask what customers aspire to, not only what they can afford. |
| 5 | Keep research continuous | Short, decision-linked surveys; show customers what changed. |
| 6 | Test findability before redesigning | Shelf tests, eye tracking, A/B tests. |
| 7 | Match research speed to the market | Short cycles in fast categories. |
| 8 | Supply before advertising | Never advertise what customers cannot find. |
| 9 | Raise involvement in commodity categories | Give buyers a reason to care and compare. |
| 10 | Protect the channel | Service level and no stock-outs keep copies off the shelf. |

<div class="callout">
<p class="callout-title">The session in one breath</p>

Good marketing rests on understanding customers, which is why P&G has sent interviewers door to door since the 1920s and now watches people at home; research does not fail because customers know nothing (Jobs, "faster horses") but because companies ask the wrong people (Gillette's MIT students, the paid respondent at the bus stop), miss social influence (New Coke, where focus groups would have revealed it), filter findings through their own bias (ChotuKool), or ignore how people actually buy (Tropicana's lost orange: test with shelf flashes, eye tracking and A/B tests), while companies like Sleepy Owl show continuous, decision-linked surveys working well; research speed must match the product life cycle. From the case: price a novelty with competitor entry, habit, goals and capacity in mind; secure availability before advertising a low-margin impulse product; recognise low-involvement categories (low economic, psychological and performance risk) and raise involvement to earn loyalty and a premium; sustain advantage through service to the retail channel, since shelf space is the retailer's scarcest resource and a stock-out invites the copy; and map growth with the Ansoff matrix, from low-risk penetration to high-risk diversification.

</div>
