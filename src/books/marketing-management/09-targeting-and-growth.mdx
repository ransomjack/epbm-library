With the market divided into segments, the second half of the session asked which segments to serve, looked at how real brands chose their bases, and ended with the risks of chasing new segments.

## Targeting: attractiveness and compatibility

Once the market is segmented, the next decision is which segment or segments to serve.

<div class="callout">
<p class="callout-title">Targeting (the slide)</p>

<strong>Targeting:</strong> selecting one or more segments to enter.

<strong>Segment attractiveness:</strong> the segment's ability to deliver value to the company and its collaborators (size, growth, usage, time to close a sale, CLV). In short: how valuable is the segment to us?

<strong>Segment compatibility:</strong> the company's ability to create superior value for that segment relative to competitors. In short: can we serve it better than rivals can?

</div>

<Fig title="The class's toothpaste example" caption="Segment size against competition. A new player may prefer the smaller, less crowded segments, and then check usage, willingness to pay, and whether it can serve that need better than rivals. Sizes are the illustrative numbers used in class.">

    { label: "Cavity protection", value: 25, note: "crowded" },
    { label: "Fresh breath", value: 30, note: "most crowded", mark: true },

</Fig>

Earlier the class had judged segments by size and competition alone. The professor widened the list: <strong>usage</strong> (the small sensitive-teeth segment may brush twice a day), <strong>income and willingness to pay</strong> (a high-income segment may pay more), growth, CLV. All of these measure attractiveness. Compatibility is the other half: your strengths and weaknesses relative to competitors, and your company and brand image. An attractive segment you cannot serve better than rivals is not your segment.

### A side question: SWOT

A participant asked about frameworks for assessing competitors' weaknesses. The professor pointed to <strong>SWOT</strong> (strengths, weaknesses, opportunities, threats) and the mistake students most often make with it.

<div class="callout trap">
<p class="callout-title">Strengths and weaknesses are internal; opportunities and threats are external</p>

If a candy was launched in only one flavour, is that a weakness or a threat? A <strong>weakness</strong>: it is internal to the company. Threats and opportunities come from the external environment: a new competitor, a regulation, a trend. Do not file an internal gap as a threat, or an external trend as a strength.

</div>

<strong>On a manager's desk.</strong> Score every candidate segment twice: once for what it is worth, once for your right to win it. A product company may find the largest segment attractive but incompatible, because the leader there is far stronger. A service firm may find a niche (critical infrastructure clients, say) less attractive in size but highly compatible with its expertise. The best target scores well on both.

## Segmentation in action: age, occupation, lifestyle

The professor then showed how real brands chose their bases.

| Brand | Base used | How it showed up |
|---|---|---|
| Pepsi (1980s) | Age | Positioned itself as the drink of the young ("the choice of a new generation", with Michael Jackson) and cast Coke as the drink of older generations. Young or young at heart: drink Pepsi. |
| Fastrack (Titan) | Age | A separate youth brand from Titan, so that the two do not chase the same buyers. Brands must keep overlap between their own target groups to a minimum, or they cannibalise each other. |
| Mountain Dew | Psychographic | Targets adventure seekers: a lifestyle, not an age. Add age and you get young adventure seekers. |
| Dove | Value-based | Skin care: soft, moisturised skin. |
| Lifebuoy and Dettol | Value-based | Hygiene: clean hands after play, parents and children in the ads; both claim to kill 99.9% of germs. During COVID, Dettol rose from outside the top five soap brands to a leading one. |
| Gap Inc. | Several | Old Navy for budget-conscious families; Athleta for women's workout gear; Banana Republic for office wear for young people; Gap for casual, affordable wear for young people. |

### Madura Garments: one company, four brands, no overlap

Madura Garments, now part of the Aditya Birla group, owns Louis Philippe, Van Heusen, Allen Solly and Peter England. Four men's formal-wear brands could easily eat into one another. Participants described them by price and formality: premium versus mass. The professor asked how the brands themselves communicate the difference. No brand says "we are for the masses". The answer is in the advertising: Madura segments by <strong>occupation and career stage</strong>.

<Fig title="Madura Garments' brands on the career ladder" caption="The four brands are separated by occupation and career stage.">
  <Flow items={[

Peter England's ads were set at job interviews: a young man starting his corporate career, giving an honest answer to "where do you see yourself in five years?". Van Heusen showed people at mid-level, climbing: "power dressing". Louis Philippe showed people who already hold the top jobs and corner offices. Allen Solly targeted smart-casual workplaces such as advertising and research agencies, and introduced the idea of Friday dressing. Career stage maps onto income, which is why price points follow naturally.

<strong>On a manager's desk.</strong> If you run more than one brand, write down each one's target and check for overlap. A product company with a premium and a value brand should make sure the value brand is not quietly taking premium customers. A services group with an advisory arm and a managed-services arm should be clear which client and which stage of the relationship each one serves.

## Gender-based segmentation: Hero Pleasure and Bic for Her

Two gender-based launches, one success and one failure, made the class think hard about when a basis works.

### Hero Pleasure

The professor asked what had happened to India's two-wheeler market over three decades. In the early 1990s scooters held roughly 85% of the market and motorcycles 15%. From the mid-1990s motorcycles grew steeply and scooters fell, until by the mid-2000s the split had roughly reversed. Bajaj, once synonymous with scooters, announced that the future lay in motorcycles and stopped making scooters around 2009.

  <Bars unit="% of the market" rows={[
    { label: "Scooters, early 1990s", value: 85, note: "about 85%" },
    { label: "Motorcycles, early 1990s", value: 15, note: "about 15%" },
    { label: "Scooters, mid-2000s", value: 20, note: "about 20%" },
    { label: "Motorcycles, mid-2000s", value: 80, note: "about 80%", mark: true },

Within the shrunken scooter segment one brand dominated: <strong>Honda's Activa</strong>, positioned as a utilitarian, unisex scooter (its ads showed men carrying luggage too). Hero was number one in two-wheelers overall, on the strength of its motorcycles. To take on Honda in scooters, Hero launched <strong>Pleasure</strong> in 2006, aimed at women, with Priyanka Chopra and the line <em>"Why should boys have all the fun?"</em>. Hero's scooter share rose from 9.8% to 16.9%. When that growth flattened, Hero launched <strong>Maestro</strong> aimed at men, with Ranbir Kapoor, and its share rose again to about 19%.

### Bic for Her

Bic, the French company that is the world's number one pen brand, is known for value-for-money products. It launched <strong>Bic for Her</strong>: pens in pastel pink, purple and green, with the same blue or black ink, at almost twice the price of a normal pen. The class was split: some would buy them for daughters who like fancy stationery, others thought it pointless. The market's response was a social-media backlash, and the comedian Ellen DeGeneres mocked the pens on her show: pens "designed to fit a woman's hand", in lady colours, costing twice as much.

Why did a gender-based launch work for Hero and fail for Bic? The class's answers added up to five reasons.

|   | Hero Pleasure | Bic for Her |
|---|---|---|
| Did it meet a real need? | Yes: a lightweight scooter for women, with colours that let them make a statement | No: the ink and function were the same; only the casing changed |
| What kind of product? | Partly a lifestyle product: riding one was a statement | Utilitarian: people buy pens to write |
| How did it frame women? | As gaining freedom: "why should boys have all the fun?" | As needing a special pen: stereotyping, with a label that said so |
| Was the price justified? | Priced as a scooter | About double the price for a coloured casing |
| Context | India, where few women rode two-wheelers then | The US, where such stereotyping is quickly called out |

Participants suggested what Bic could have done: drop the "for her" label and simply put the pens on the shelf, so girls could choose them freely; or position them as fun gifts for children of any gender. The professor's own view was that the labelling was the biggest problem: quietly placed, the pens might have found a small segment.

She also mentioned <strong>Gillette</strong>, which sold razors for women at a premium of around 30% over the men's version for an essentially similar product: the so-called <strong>pink tax</strong> seen in many categories. It worked for years, until Gillette ran an advertisement criticising toxic masculinity during the #MeToo period and activists pointed to its own pink pricing. Gillette has since narrowed the gap.

<div class="callout trap">
<p class="callout-title">What works in one category may not work in yours</p>

Gender worked as a basis for scooters and failed for pens. A segmentation variable is not good or bad in itself: it is good only if the segment has a different need that the product meets better, at a price that need justifies.

</div>

<strong>On a manager's desk.</strong> Before launching a version "for" any group, ask what it does for them that the standard version does not. A product company that changes only colour and doubles the price is inviting a backlash. A service firm that creates a "small business edition" with fewer features and no lower price will meet the same scepticism.

## The product life cycle, revisited

The Hero story brought back the <strong>product life cycle</strong> from Session 0 ([The product life cycle](/marketing-management/the-marketing-plan/#the-product-life-cycle)): introduction (slow sales growth), growth (steep), maturity (sales stabilise), decline. The professor read Hero's numbers through it: Pleasure's share rose, then plateaued, and at that point Hero launched Maestro, which started its own cycle. The general lesson: when sales start to plateau, launch the next product so that it takes off as the first one matures.

The shape is not fixed. An Apple launch has almost no slow introductory phase (people queue to buy on day one) so its curve shoots straight up.

### Should you launch a new product, or fix the old one?

A participant challenged the rule: if sales are falling because a competitor has a better product or price, why rush into new product development? Why not improve the existing product? The professor accepted the criticism (it is a known weakness of the life cycle concept) and gave two clarifications.

<div class="callout">
<p class="callout-title">Where the life cycle applies, and where it does not</p>

<strong>It applies best at the sub-category level.</strong> Black-and-white televisions went through a full life cycle and vanished; pagers did too. That decline came from technology, not from a single brand's mistakes.

<strong>For a single brand, diagnose before acting.</strong> If your sales plateau while the category grows, the problem is your brand (competitors, product, price) and the answer is to fix your strategy, not to assume the product has reached its natural end.

</div>

<strong>On a manager's desk.</strong> Read the category curve and your own curve separately. A product company whose sales flatten while the category keeps growing has a competitiveness problem; one whose whole sub-category is flattening needs its next product. A service firm whose renewals fall while the market grows should look at its service before it invents a new one.

## Growing without losing your base

Every brand is under pressure to grow, and growth often means reaching new segments. The professor told two stories of what happens when that reach costs a brand its existing customers.

| Company | What was tried | What happened |
|---|---|---|
| Gap | Sonia Syngal, an Indian-origin executive who had led Old Navy, became CEO of Gap Inc. in 2020. She set out to make Gap an all-inclusive brand, stocking extra-small and plus sizes so that women of all sizes could shop there. | Store space is limited. The extra sizes came at the cost of variety in the regular sizes Gap's usual customers wanted; a meme captured the empty racks. Among other reasons, the strategy cost her the job within about two years. |
| JCPenney | Ron Johnson, who had succeeded at Target and built Apple's retail stores, was brought in around 2011. He gave the department store a younger look (new labels for younger shoppers, a new layout in the style of Apple's stores) on a plan meant to run three to four years. | JCPenney's typical customer was a woman buying for her family. She felt lost in the new layout and merchandise. Johnson was removed after about a year and a half. |

<div class="callout">
<p class="callout-title">The lesson</p>

Reaching new segments should not come at the cost of your existing, loyal customers. The moment it does, it becomes a disaster: serious enough to remove a CEO, even one with a multi-year plan.

</div>

The professor added a quieter version of the same dilemma: <strong>Old Spice</strong>. Asked how they saw the brand, participants said it felt old ("my father used it") though one insisted old is gold. Young people rarely want their parents' brands. A brand whose loyal base is ageing must attract younger customers without alienating the ones it has.

<strong>On a manager's desk.</strong> Growth that pushes out the core customer is not growth. A product company launching a youth range should protect shelf space and attention for its core range. A service firm chasing a new sector should not pull its best people off the clients who pay the bills today.

## Applying the Ansoff matrix

The session's case was an Indian ethnic-wear brand for women: a first mover now facing many rivals, with a loyal customer base growing older and younger women drifting to Western and fusion brands. The case is not retold here. What it taught was how to apply the framework from the previous session ([Growth options: the Ansoff matrix](/marketing-management/launching-and-growing/#growth-options-the-ansoff-matrix)), the <strong>Ansoff matrix</strong>, and where the judgement calls lie.

### Judgement at the borders

The professor made several points about placing options in the grid.

<strong>Check what already exists.</strong> If the brand already sells a line (kids' wear or unstitched fabric, say) expanding it is penetration, not product development.

<strong>Ask whether skills transfer.</strong> Menswear may look like a small step from womenswear, but tailoring for men is a different craft with different cuts and different tailors; the skills do not transfer easily, so she placed it under diversification. Home decor is further still. Western wear brings an entirely new set of competitors and puts the brand's ethnic positioning at risk: high risk, high reward.

<strong>Pilot quietly.</strong> Participants doubted that menswear was worth it: a small market, bought mainly at festivals. The professor suggested a low-risk test: a small menswear counter inside existing stores, basic solid pieces, no labels or fanfare, and watch how it moves. A participant noted that men accompanying women shoppers have time to browse: an untapped occasion. If it moves, expand, perhaps under a new brand.

<strong>Retain the loyal base.</strong> One participant's central point was that the brand must keep its existing loyal customers while growing: the Gap lesson again ([Growing without losing your base](#growing-without-losing-your-base)).

<div class="callout trap">
<p class="callout-title">The cells are a spectrum, not boxes</p>

The lines in the Ansoff matrix are blurred. Whether an option counts as product development or diversification depends on how far the new product is from your skills and how different the new customers and competitors are. Justify the placement; do not just assert it.

</div>

<strong>On a manager's desk.</strong> List growth options in all four cells before choosing, and price the risk honestly. A product company usually finds its cheapest growth in penetration. A service firm moving from monitoring into advisory is nearer diversification than it looks, because the skills, buyers and competitors all change: pilot it small before you rebrand around it.

## The session on a manager's desk

| Idea | In a product business | In a service business |
|---|---|---|
| Why segment | Six variants, not one or sixty-five | Three service tiers, not one or bespoke for all |
| Value first | Toothpaste by need: whitening, sensitivity, cavity | Clients by what they value: speed, reporting, price |
| Profile the segment | Persona sets media, price and packaging | Who signs, who uses, what each fears |
| Business markets | Hillrom: key vs prime hospitals | Segment by buying approach and benefits, not just size |
| Cluster analysis | Survey shoppers' priorities | Cluster clients by what they rate important |
| Targeting | Attractive and compatible segments | Niches where your expertise wins |
| Multi-brand discipline | Madura's career ladder; Titan and Fastrack | Advisory vs managed services, clearly split |
| Gender as a basis | Works for Pleasure; failed for Bic for Her | "Editions" must add value, not just labels |
| Protect the core | Gap and JCPenney | Do not strip service from paying clients to chase new ones |
| Ansoff | Penetration first; diversification last | Monitoring to advisory is closer to diversification |

### The manager's checklist from this session

| # | Discipline | What it looks like on Monday morning |
|---|---|---|
| 1 | Segment on what changes buying | Drop any variable that does not change preferences or behaviour. |
| 2 | Start with value | List the needs customers bring to the category before slicing by age or city. |
| 3 | Then profile | Write a persona for each target segment before designing the 4Ps. |
| 4 | Use data, but do not be led by it | Behaviour data shows what; research shows why. |
| 5 | Keep segments few and distinct | Three or four, with no customer in two. |
| 6 | Score attractiveness and compatibility | Size, growth, usage, CLV, and your right to win. |
| 7 | Keep SWOT honest | Internal = strengths and weaknesses; external = opportunities and threats. |
| 8 | Avoid cannibalising your own brands | Separate targets for each brand in the portfolio. |
| 9 | Add value, not labels | A segment-specific version must meet a different need. |
| 10 | Grow without losing the core | Map options on Ansoff, pilot the risky ones, protect loyal customers. |

<div class="callout">
<p class="callout-title">The session in one breath</p>

Segmentation divides the actual and potential market into groups that are alike inside and different outside, balancing effectiveness (a mix that fits) against efficiency (dropping differences that do not change buying); its bases are value-based and profile-based (geographic, demographic, psychographic and behavioural, with firmographics, buying approach and benefits sought for business markets) and a good approach starts with the needs customers bring (toothpaste: whitening, sensitivity, cavities, fresh breath), sizes each need, then profiles who is in it so the 4Ps can be designed, even though marketers lean on demographics and behaviour because those are visible or already in their data. Segments are found by judgement or by cluster analysis (Euclidean distance; hierarchical or k-means), read as deviations from the average, and chosen by attractiveness (size, growth, usage, CLV) and compatibility (can we serve them better than rivals). Pepsi and Fastrack used age, Mountain Dew lifestyle, Madura career stage, Hero gender with success and Bic gender with a backlash; Gap and JCPenney show that new segments must not cost you the core; and the Ansoff matrix maps growth from low-risk penetration to high-risk diversification.

</div>
