## Why research the customer?

Session 0 said marketing starts with the customer. But how does a company with thousands or millions of customers actually know what they want? It researches them, and the best companies have done so for a very long time.

The slides opened with Procter & Gamble. As early as the 1920s and through the 1940s, P&G's market research department, led by Paul "Doc" Smelser, sent thousands of interviewers door to door to talk to housewives about how they cooked, cleaned and washed. The slide's photograph shows one of those interviewers, notebook in hand, on a doorstep.

Decades later the method had evolved but the spirit had not. Under its CEO A.G. Lafley in the 2000s, P&G employees spent hours with women at home, <strong>watching</strong> them do the laundry, clean the floor, apply make-up and change their babies' nappies. They looked for small nuisances a new product could remove, then went back to the labs to solve the problem the customer cared about most. Lafley told his executives that women did not care about P&G's technology or which machine made a product. His summary, on the slide, was to <strong>find out what she wants and give it to her</strong>: a principle, he joked, that had worked in his marriage for 35 years and worked in laundry too.

<div class="callout">
<p class="callout-title">The shift in P&G's approach</p>

From asking people what they want, to watching what they actually do. Observation reveals the nuisances people have stopped noticing and so never mention in an interview.

</div>

<strong>On a manager's desk.</strong> Spend time where your customer uses the product, not only where they buy it. A product company learns more from an hour watching someone clean a floor than from a day of surveys. A service firm learns more from sitting in a client's control room during a shift than from its annual satisfaction score.

## Do customers know what they want?

Not everyone believes in research. Steve Jobs famously argued that customers do not know what they want until you show it to them: how could a customer have asked for an iPhone? Henry Ford is often quoted as saying that if he had asked people what they wanted, they would have said faster horses.

Yet when Apple and Samsung fought their long court battles over design, the documents disclosed showed how much customer research Apple actually did, including surveys of its own buyers about why they chose its products. Whatever Apple said in public, it did the research.

So the lesson the class drew was not that research is useless, but that research done badly misleads. Skip it and the odds of failure rise; do it badly and you can be confidently wrong. The rest of the session is really a tour of <strong>how</strong> research goes wrong, and how to avoid it.

Just before the break a participant put the other side: some products succeed by solving a problem people did not know they had, with no customer asking for them (he cited Google Maps) while the Tata Nano also set out to solve a problem and failed. Both halves of that observation are true, and they point the same way: nobody asks for a map on a phone, but everybody can describe the trouble of being lost. Research finds the trouble; the company invents the answer.

She gave an example from a guest session by a senior executive of Maruti, who had earlier worked with Nissan. Before launching a new car, his company had paid to bring in respondents to give feedback on the design. One evening he saw one of those well-paid respondents waiting at a bus stop, not remotely the target customer for the car. The data was coming from the wrong people.

<div class="callout beyond">
<p class="callout-title">Good to know: the Ford quote</p>

There is no solid evidence Henry Ford ever said the "faster horses" line; it is widely attributed to him. The point stands either way: customers are good at describing problems, less good at inventing solutions. Research should uncover the problem; designing the solution is the company's job.

</div>

<div class="callout trap">
<p class="callout-title">Bad research is not the same as no research</p>

Research can go wrong at every step: whom you ask (the bus-stop respondent), what you ask, how you ask, and how you read the answer. The lesson from a failure is to fix the method, not to abandon research.

</div>

<strong>On a manager's desk.</strong> Check who your respondents are before you trust their answers. A product company recruiting a panel should verify that panellists match the target segment. A service firm asking for feedback should hear from the people who use the service daily, not only the executive who signs the contract.

## The researcher's toolkit

Across the session the class met many research methods. They can be organised along two questions: is the method about what people <strong>say</strong> or what they <strong>do</strong>; and is it <strong>qualitative</strong> (a few people, in depth) or <strong>quantitative</strong> (many people, measurable)?

<div class="callout">
<p class="callout-title">Observation versus ethnography</p>

The professor drew the line clearly: ethnography is a form of observation research carried out over a <strong>longer period</strong>, in which the researcher immerses himself in the community (how people live and how the product fits into their lives).

</div>

<div class="callout beyond">
<p class="callout-title">Good to know: A standard way to classify research</p>

Textbooks also sort research by purpose. <strong>Exploratory</strong> research (interviews, focus groups, ethnography) discovers problems and ideas; <strong>descriptive</strong> research (surveys) measures how many people think or do what; <strong>causal</strong> research (experiments, A/B tests, test markets) tests whether a change causes an effect. A good programme usually uses more than one.

</div>

<strong>On a manager's desk.</strong> Use at least one "says" method and one "does" method before a big decision. A product company that only surveys will miss the shelf behaviour that sank Tropicana. A service firm that only reads usage logs will miss why clients are unhappy; one that only interviews will miss what they actually do.

## Who you ask matters: Gillette in India

The slide carried a headline about Gillette executives spending a fortune developing a razor for India using focus groups of students at MIT, without considering that much of India lacked running water.

Gillette's first attempt, the <strong>Vector</strong> razor (2002), was designed with input from Indian students at MIT in the United States. Those students shaved like Americans, with running water to rinse the blade. In much of India, men shaved with a small cup of water, often in poor light. The Vector's twin blades clogged without running water. It was a massive flop.

For the next attempt, Gillette's developers went to India and watched men shave in their own homes and neighbourhoods: the "aha" moment that taught them about the market. The result was the <strong>Gillette Guard</strong>, a single-blade razor that was easy to rinse with little water, safe, and cheap. Launched in October 2010, it had over 50% of the market by volume within six months, and it came to account for two of every three razors sold in India.

<div class="callout beyond">
<p class="callout-title">Good to know: the Guard's price</p>

The Guard was launched at about Rs. 15 for the razor and Rs. 5 for a replacement cartridge: a price built around the buyer the research had found.

</div>

<div class="callout trap">
<p class="callout-title">Convenient respondents are not representative respondents</p>

Indian students at MIT were Indian, convenient and articulate, and completely unrepresentative of how most Indian men shaved. Recall Session 1's lesson about research done in the head-office city: convenience is the most common source of bias.

</div>

<strong>On a manager's desk.</strong> Go where the customer is. A product company designing for rural or emerging markets should research there, under real conditions of water, power and light. A service firm designing for plants in another country should visit those plants, not interview colleagues at headquarters who once worked there.

## What a taste test misses: New Coke

The slide called it the biggest marketing failure. In the early 1980s Coca-Cola was losing ground to Pepsi, whose "Pepsi Challenge" blind taste tests kept showing people preferring the sweeter Pepsi. Coca-Cola developed a new, sweeter formula and tested it with around 200,000 consumers in blind taste tests, where it beat both old Coke and Pepsi.

In April 1985 the company replaced its original formula with <strong>New Coke</strong>. The reaction was furious: protest calls, letters, hoarding of the old drink, and media outrage. Within about three months, 79 days, Coca-Cola brought the original back as Coca-Cola Classic.

What had the research missed? The taste tests asked one question, about taste, to people sitting alone. They never asked how people would feel if the original Coke they had grown up with was <strong>taken away</strong>: its meaning, nostalgia and identity. And they could not show how people's views would change once they heard others' anger. In Coca-Cola's own group discussions, a few outspoken participants could swing the whole group against the change.

<div class="callout">
<p class="callout-title">The lesson (from the slide)</p>

Consideration of the effects of <strong>social influence</strong> should become a standard part of the new product development process.

The <strong>focus group</strong> is an important qualitative technique precisely because it can reveal these social interaction effects, which individual taste tests and surveys cannot.

</div>

Session 5 returned to the same story from the brand side: when people knew which drink they were tasting, brand loyalty changed their answers; in blind tests, it did not. Real buying happens with the brand visible.

<strong>On a manager's desk.</strong> Test the change, not just the product. A product company replacing a loved product should research the loss as well as the gain. A service firm changing a familiar report format or support process should ask how clients will feel about losing the old one, and listen to the group, not only the individual.

## Ethnography and its blind spot: ChotuKool

Godrej's <strong>ChotuKool</strong> is the slide's showcase of ethnography. Two Godrej executives (Sunderraman and Lonial, on the slide) travelled for many months, staying in villages in every part of India, observing what people needed and how they would use a fridge. As engineers produced prototypes, these were sent to villages in Maharashtra to be stress-tested in real conditions and to collect users' feedback. The professor knew one of the people behind it, who had been associated with an IIM programme on manufacturing run jointly with IIT Madras and IIT Kanpur, in which Godrej is an industry partner.

| What the ethnography found | How ChotuKool answered it |
|---|---|
| The cheapest fridge cost Rs. 8,000-10,000; a 10-20% cut would not do: the price had to fall drastically | Priced at about Rs. 3,250-3,790: less than half |
| Electricity bills must not run into three digits | Runs on a 12V DC supply with thermoelectric cooling and high-end insulation; low running cost |
| Frequent power cuts | Insulation and design to hold cool through outages |
| Servicing: where would we take it, and at what cost? | Few parts to fail; light enough (10 kg) to carry to a repair point on a bicycle |
| Tiny homes; small, frequent purchases | A compact 43-litre box; enough for milk, vegetables, fruit and water |
| Physics | Opens from the top, so cold air (which sinks) stays in |

It came out around 2010. It keeps contents at 5-15 degrees: cool, not cold: it cannot make ice. The running cost was designed to stay around Rs. 50, and moving it for a repair to cost no more than a few hundred rupees. It won the 2012 Edison Award (gold, social impact category), many other innovation awards, case studies and international media attention.

Yet it did not succeed as hoped. One reason, from the professor's conversations with the team, was technical: in states where summer temperatures rise above 40-45 degrees, such as Rajasthan and Gujarat, it could not hold a consistently low temperature, and the team was working with MIT on better materials. Participants added price (even Rs. 3,000-4,000 seemed high to some for such a small box, with Rs. 2,000 suggested as nearer the mark) and doubts about whether that market needed the product at all.

The professor then showed the research team's own list of insights and asked where the launch went wrong, since every stated concern (price, running cost, space, servicing, power cuts) had been answered. Her answer was <strong>researcher bias</strong>. The researchers had read the needs through their own assumptions about what rural buyers should want. Why should a village family not want the same fridge as an urban family? A fridge is also aspirational. A small cooler that cannot make ice may meet every listed need and still not be what people want: an echo of the Tata Nano.

<div class="callout trap">
<p class="callout-title">Meeting every stated need is not the same as meeting the real one</p>

Research can be meticulous and still be filtered through the researcher's own picture of the customer. Ask what the customer aspires to, not only what the customer can afford.

</div>

<strong>On a manager's desk.</strong> Have someone challenge the research team's assumptions before launch. A product company designing a "low-cost version" for poorer customers should test whether they would rather save for the real thing. A service firm designing a stripped-down offer for smaller clients should check that those clients do not simply want the full service at a fair price.

## Surveys done well: Sleepy Owl

The slides showed the opposite of research as a one-off event: Sleepy Owl, an Indian coffee start-up that runs short, friendly surveys with its customers ("Hey there, Sleepy Head!") and acts on the results. Its founders have said that nearly everything the company does comes from customer feedback and surveys, which help it shake off its own biases.

| Area | What the surveys showed | What Sleepy Owl did |
|---|---|---|
| Formats | Even in peak summer, people did not want something cold first thing in the morning | Launched a hot brew |
| Flavours | Indians love flavours (Starbucks had popularised flavoured coffee) | Launched French Vanilla and Hazelnut: now bestsellers |
| Sustainable packaging | Over 60% of customers felt guilty about Sleepy Owl's plastic | Created recyclable, eco-friendly packs after a year of research |
| Advertising | Customers prefer low acidity, great aroma and a smooth taste | Used the words with the most appeal in ads, raising click-through rates |

<div class="callout">
<p class="callout-title">Why it works</p>

The surveys are short, frequent and tied to a specific decision: a format, a flavour, a pack, a word in an ad. Each answer changes something customers can see, which keeps them willing to answer the next survey.

</div>

<strong>On a manager's desk.</strong> Make research continuous and decision-linked. A product company can poll customers on two pack designs before printing either. A service firm can ask clients one question after each interaction and change something visible in response: customers keep answering when they see their answers used.

## How people actually buy: Tropicana

The professor showed two Tropicana orange-juice packs side by side and asked which the class preferred. The chat split roughly evenly. The left was the old pack; the right, the new one. The juice was the same. Only the pack changed:

- "Pure Premium, No Pulp" became "Pulp Free".
- The horizontal Tropicana logo became vertical.
- "100% pure and natural" became "100% orange".
- The famous orange pierced by a straw was replaced by a glass of juice.
- The cap was redesigned to look like an orange.

Several participants preferred the new pack: it looked more modern, its claims were clearer, and packaging should evolve with a new generation. Then the professor revealed what happened. Tropicana spent about $35 million on advertising, $50 million in all, on the relaunch, and its sales fell 20% in less than two months, while the category's sales did not change.

Why? A participant suggested trust: a changed pack can look like a fake. That happens: it is exactly what hit Fair & Lovely in rural markets (Session 4), but here the explanation was simpler. Shoppers had been buying Tropicana by scanning the shelf for that orange with a straw. Remove it, and Tropicana blended into every other brand; shoppers settled for whichever juice was in front of them.

<div class="callout trap">
<p class="callout-title">Habit is not emotion</p>

A participant called the loss of the familiar orange an emotional loss. The professor corrected the label: reaching for the orange is buying <strong>behaviour</strong> (a learned habit of search) not emotion. The process she described was right; the name was wrong.

</div>

<div class="callout">
<p class="callout-title">The lessons (from the slide and class)</p>

<strong>Research how people actually buy</strong>, not only what they like. Liking a pack and finding it on a shelf are different things.

<strong>Use the right tools:</strong> shelf flash images that test recall and recognition alongside emotions; eye-tracking studies; A/B testing; computer-simulated shelves where respondents are asked to buy a juice; observation in store followed by a post-purchase interview.

<strong>Don't fix what isn't broken.</strong> Tropicana was the leader, and it was never clear why the change was needed. Low-involvement brands sometimes change things just to create noise.

<strong>Change in steps, and communicate.</strong> A participant suggested gradual change; the professor agreed it is safer, though repeated pack changes are expensive: each needs new packaging and advertising so customers do not think the product is fake.

</div>

A participant raised advertising that overstates reality: a law in Japan, and the Maaza-versus-Slice debate about showing real mangoes. The professor mentioned a US lawsuit against McDonald's over burgers that looked bigger and fresher in ads than in real life; the court found for the company, reasoning that any reasonable person understands advertising images and must interpret them sensibly.

<strong>On a manager's desk.</strong> Before changing anything customers use to find you (a pack, a colour, a logo, a website layout) test whether they can still find you. A product company should run shelf tests. A service firm redesigning its portal or report format should watch real users try to find the three things they always look for.

## How long can research take?

A participant asked a practical question: if a product takes two or three years to develop, the market may have changed by launch. Electric vehicles were his example: many carmakers researched, invested, and then found demand slower than expected. How should research handle this?

The professor separated two issues: how research is conducted, and how fast the market moves. The answer depends on the <strong>product life cycle</strong>. FMCG products have long lives (Lux and Pears still exist) so research can take its time. Technology products have short lives: when Apple launches a new feature in September, competitors copy it within months, which is why Apple and Samsung keep accusing each other of copying. Fast fashion works the same way: a design seen on the runway is in Zara's stores days later, tweaked just enough to avoid infringement. In fast-moving categories you cannot spend two years researching: by then someone else has launched.

<strong>On a manager's desk.</strong> Match the depth of research to the speed of the market. A product company in a slow category can afford long studies; one in a fast category should research in short cycles and launch, learn and adjust. A technology service firm should do the same: pilot quickly with a few clients rather than study for a year.
