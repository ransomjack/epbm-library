## Where every tool fits

Think of how a good doctor works. She examines you, then decides what is wrong, then treats it, then asks you to come back in two weeks. A marketing plan makes the same four moves: <strong>understand</strong> the situation, <strong>choose</strong> whom to serve and what to stand for, <strong>deliver</strong> the offer, and <strong>sustain</strong> the relationship. Session 0 gave the first three their names: the 5Cs, STP and the 4Ps ([STP and the 4Ps](/marketing-management/the-marketing-plan/#stp-and-the-4ps)). Every framework in this book belongs to one of the four moves.

## How buyers decide: the buying process

Suppose your family decides to buy its first car. Nobody wakes up one morning and buys one. First the scooter stops being enough for four people. Then you ask colleagues, watch reviews and visit showrooms. You shortlist three models and compare mileage, space, price and service. You book one, unless a last-minute offer from another dealer, or your father-in-law's firm opinion, changes your mind. And for weeks afterwards you keep checking whether you chose right. Textbooks call this the <strong>five-stage buying decision process</strong>.

| 2. | 3. | 5. Post-purchase behaviour | Uses the product, judges it against expectations, and talks about it | Deliver what was promised; reassure; handle complaints well | Crisis handling and recovery ([Availability: what comes to mind feels likely](/marketing-management/more-mental-shortcuts/#availability-what-comes-to-mind-feels-likely)); retention and CLV ([Customer lifetime value: why it matters](/marketing-management/what-a-customer-is-worth/#customer-lifetime-value-why-it-matters)) |

Two points turn this from a diagram into a tool. First, buyers walk through all five stages only when <strong>involvement is high</strong>. For a candy or a packet of salt the buyer jumps from need to purchase, deciding by habit or by what is visible, which is why availability and recall did so much of the work in Session 2 ([Low- and high-involvement products](/marketing-management/launching-and-growing/#low--and-high-involvement-products)). Second, <strong>satisfaction is a comparison</strong>: what the buyer got against what the buyer expected. Promise too much in the advertisement and even a good product disappoints. After a big purchase buyers often feel a nagging doubt (did I choose right?) called <strong>post-purchase dissonance</strong>. That is why good companies keep talking to the customer after the sale: a welcome call, a first free service, a note confirming a good choice.

### Who is "the buyer"? Five roles

In the car example, the person who signed the cheque was not the only one who mattered. In most purchases larger than a packet of biscuits, up to five roles are played, sometimes by five different people.

| Role | What the person does | The family car | A business purchase |
|---|---|---|---|
| Initiator | First suggests buying | The teenager who says the scooter is embarrassing | The plant engineer who reports a problem |
| Influencer | Shapes the choice with advice | A colleague who owns the model; a reviewer | Consultants; the technical team |
| Decider | Makes the final call | The parents, together | The business head or a purchase committee |
| Buyer | Carries out the purchase | Whoever signs at the dealership | The procurement department |
| User | Uses the product | The whole family | The operations team |

This extends the customer-versus-consumer distinction of [The 5Cs: reading the situation](/marketing-management/what-marketing-is/#the-5cs-reading-the-situation), and it is what the healthcare-equipment maker of [Segmenting business markets](/marketing-management/segmentation/#segmenting-business-markets) discovered: nurses drove the decision in one segment and administrators in the other. A message aimed only at the person who pays misses the people who actually steer the decision.

<div class="callout beyond">
<p class="callout-title">Good to know: needs come in layers</p>

<strong>Maslow's hierarchy</strong> arranges human needs in five layers: physiological, safety, social (belonging), esteem and self-actualisation, and suggests people attend to the higher layers once the lower ones are reasonably met. For a marketer its use is simple: the same product can be sold on different layers. A car is transport (a basic need), airbags (safety), a family outing (belonging) or a badge (esteem). The Tata Nano was offered as transport to buyers who were shopping for esteem ([Great product, wrong fit: the Tata Nano](/marketing-management/the-marketing-plan/#great-product-wrong-fit-the-tata-nano)).

</div>

<strong>On a manager's desk.</strong> Map the stages and the roles for your own customer before writing a plan. A product company should know where its buyers search and what puts a brand into, or out of, the consideration set. A service firm selling to businesses should list who initiates, influences, decides, buys and uses, and prepare something for each, because the user's complaint and the finance head's question are two different conversations.

## Product: more than the thing in the box

What does a person buying a pressure cooker actually buy? Not aluminium. She buys dal cooked in fifteen minutes instead of an hour. A line the Harvard professor Theodore Levitt made famous says the same about tools: people do not want a quarter-inch drill, they want a quarter-inch hole. A product is best seen as three layers, from the inside out.

<Fig title="The three levels of a product" caption="A pressure cooker, from the outside in.">
  <Nest items={[
    ["Augmented product", "Everything added around it: a warranty, spare gaskets in any shop, a recipe book, service centres. Mature categories compete mostly here."],
    ["Actual product", "The thing itself: a 5-litre cooker, a trusted brand name, a safety valve, the box it comes in."],
    ["Core benefit", "What the buyer is really buying: dal on the table in fifteen minutes instead of an hour."],
  ]} />
</Fig>

| Level | What it is | A pressure cooker | A managed service |
|---|---|---|---|
| Core benefit | The problem solved | A meal cooked fast | Confidence that the plant keeps running |
| Actual product | The thing itself: features, design, quality, brand name, packaging | A 5-litre cooker, a trusted name, a safety valve | Monitoring, reports, response times, the provider's name |
| Augmented product | Everything added around it | Warranty, spares in any shop, a recipe book, service centres | Onboarding, training, a named account manager, a quarterly review |

In mature categories competition happens mostly at the outer layer: when all cookers cook, spares and service decide. That is the life cycle's advice for maturity: augment the product ([The product life cycle](/marketing-management/the-marketing-plan/#the-product-life-cycle)). But the layers are built from the inside out. No outer layer rescues a product that misses the core benefit the buyer wanted, which is one way to read the small cooler of [Ethnography and its blind spot: ChotuKool](/marketing-management/understanding-customers/#ethnography-and-its-blind-spot-chotukool).

<div class="callout beyond">
<p class="callout-title">Good to know: three levels or five</p>

Kotler's fuller version has five levels: core benefit, basic product, expected product, augmented product and potential product (everything it might become). The three-level version is the one most managers use.

</div>

### The product mix

Few companies sell one product. A <strong>product line</strong> is a group of closely related products; the <strong>product mix</strong> is all the lines a company sells. It is described by four dimensions.

| Dimension | What it measures | A dairy brand |
|---|---|---|
| Width | The number of product lines | Milk, butter, cheese, ice cream, chocolate |
| Length | The total number of items across all lines | Every pack and variant, counted |
| Depth | The variants within one line | Butter in 100 g and 500 g, salted, unsalted, garlic |
| Consistency | How closely the lines are related | All dairy: the same buyers, the same cold chain |

These words connect to Session 5. A line extension adds depth; a category extension adds width ([Brand extension: line and category](/marketing-management/brand-extension-and-portfolio/#brand-extension-line-and-category)); brand portfolio rationalisation trims length ([Brand portfolio rationalisation](/marketing-management/brand-extension-and-portfolio/#brand-portfolio-rationalisation)).

### How new products spread

A new product is not adopted by everyone at once. Think of how paying by phone spread: first the technology enthusiasts, then the shopkeepers who saw customers asking for it, and finally the vegetable vendor with a QR code taped to his cart. Everett Rogers described this pattern as the <strong>diffusion of innovations</strong>.

<Fig title="Who adopts when" caption="Adopter categories, from the first to try a new product to the last. The early market and the mass market are different people wanting different things.">
  <Bars unit="% of eventual adopters" rows={[
    { label: "Innovators", value: 2.5, note: "try anything new" },
    { label: "Early adopters", value: 13.5, note: "opinion leaders" },
    { label: "Early majority", value: 34, note: "wait for proof", mark: true },
    { label: "Late majority", value: 34, note: "adopt once it is the norm" },
    { label: "Laggards", value: 16, note: "come last" },
  ]} />
</Fig>

Innovators will try anything new. Early adopters are the opinion leaders others watch. The early majority wait for proof; the late majority adopt once the product is the norm; laggards come last. The early buyers forgive rough edges for the sake of novelty, while the majority want proof, ease and a fair price, so the marketing that wins the first group rarely wins the second. This sits underneath the product life cycle ([The product life cycle](/marketing-management/the-marketing-plan/#the-product-life-cycle)): introduction is innovators and early adopters; growth is the early majority arriving.

<strong>On a manager's desk.</strong> Describe your offer at all three levels before you describe its features. A product company should compete on the augmented layer only once the core benefit is right, and change its message as it moves from early adopters to the majority. A service firm's actual product is invisible, so the augmented layer (the reports, the reviews, the people the client meets) often is the product in the client's eyes.

## Price: the only P that earns

Product, place and promotion all cost money. Price is the only element of the marketing mix that brings money in, and it is also the quickest to change. That is reason enough not to set it by adding a margin to cost and moving on. Session 0's opening exercise produced a dozen factors that bear on the price of a new phone ([The opening exercise: pricing a smartphone](/marketing-management/what-marketing-is/#the-opening-exercise-pricing-a-smartphone)). They reduce to three.

<div class="callout">
<p class="callout-title">The three Cs of pricing</p>

<strong>Costs</strong> set the floor: below them, every unit sold loses money.

<strong>Customers'</strong> perceived value sets the ceiling: above it, nobody buys.

<strong>Competitors'</strong> prices (and the prices of substitutes) mark the reference points in between. Pricing is the choice of a point between the floor and the ceiling.

</div>

<Fig title="Where a price can sit" caption="Every price sits somewhere between cost and perceived value.">
  <Flow items={[
    { t: "Floor: cost", s: "Cost per unit. Below this, every sale loses money." },
    { t: "Reference points", s: "Competitors' and substitutes' prices: what buyers compare you with." },
    { t: "Ceiling: perceived value", s: "The value the customer perceives. Above this, nobody buys.", hl: true },
  ]} />
</Fig>

| Method | How the price is set | Good for | Its weakness |
|---|---|---|---|
| Cost-plus (markup) | Unit cost plus a standard margin | Simplicity; common in retail and in contracts | Ignores what customers will pay and what rivals charge |
| Target-return | The price that gives a target return on investment at an expected volume | Capital-heavy businesses | Rests on a volume forecast that the price itself changes |
| Value-based | Start from the value the customer perceives, then design the cost to fit | Differentiated products; business markets where savings can be shown | Needs research into customer value |
| Competition-based (going rate) | At, above or below rivals' prices | Commodities; tenders | Follows the market; margins can vanish |

The class had already met the strategic choices that sit on top of these methods:

- Skimming versus penetration for a new product ([Lessons from the case: pricing and promoting a novel product](/marketing-management/launching-and-growing/#lessons-from-the-case-pricing-and-promoting-a-novel-product)).
- Psychological pricing: anchors, charm prices, price as a signal of quality ([Anchoring at work](/marketing-management/how-buyers-decide/#anchoring-at-work) and [More price cues the class asked about](/marketing-management/more-mental-shortcuts/#more-price-cues-the-class-asked-about)).
- Charging two segments differently for the same product ([One product, two segments: the pregnancy test](/marketing-management/positioning/#one-product-two-segments-the-pregnancy-test)).

### Two numbers every pricing decision needs

<strong>Break-even volume.</strong> A chai stall pays Rs. 12,000 a month in rent and wages whether it sells one cup or a thousand: these are fixed costs. Each cup costs Rs. 4 in milk, tea, sugar and the cup itself, the variable cost, and sells at Rs. 10. Every cup therefore contributes Rs. 6 towards the fixed costs, and the stall must sell 12,000 / 6 = 2,000 cups a month, about 67 a day, before it earns anything. That Rs. 6 is the <strong>contribution</strong> per unit, the same idea the professor used for deciding which brands to keep ([Brand portfolio rationalisation](/marketing-management/brand-extension-and-portfolio/#brand-portfolio-rationalisation)).

<div class="callout">
<p class="callout-title">Break-even</p>

Break-even volume = fixed costs / (price - variable cost per unit)

</div>

<strong>Price elasticity.</strong> Suppose the stall sells 2,600 cups a month at Rs. 10: a profit of 2,600 × 6 - 12,000 = Rs. 3,600. It raises the price to Rs. 12, a rise of 20%, and sales fall to 2,200 cups, a fall of about 15%. Profit is now 2,200 × 8 - 12,000 = Rs. 5,600. The price rise paid because demand was not very sensitive to price. That sensitivity is the <strong>price elasticity of demand</strong>: the percentage change in quantity divided by the percentage change in price (here 15 / 20, or about 0.8). Below 1, demand is inelastic and a higher price raises revenue; above 1, it is elastic and a higher price lowers revenue.

Demand is less elastic when a product is differentiated, hard to compare, or a small part of the buyer's budget. That is what brand building buys (Session 5): room to charge more without losing the customer.

<div class="callout trap">
<p class="callout-title">Cost-plus feels safe and is not</p>

Cost tells you the least you must charge, never the most you could. A product that saves its buyer a lakh of rupees a year, priced at cost plus twenty per cent, gives most of its value away. And remember that a price cut is the easiest move for a rival to copy: it often ends with the same market shares at lower margins for everyone.

</div>

<strong>On a manager's desk.</strong> Find the ceiling before you look at the floor. A product company should research what the benefit is worth to the customer and know its break-even volume before approving any promotion. A service firm should price on the value delivered (downtime avoided, risk reduced) rather than on hours spent, and offer tiers so that buyers can choose their own point between floor and ceiling ([The decoy: adding a dominated alternative](/marketing-management/more-mental-shortcuts/#the-decoy-adding-a-dominated-alternative)).

## Place: getting to the customer

A biscuit baked in a factory in one state reaches a kirana shop in a village a thousand kilometres away within days, is sold one packet at a time, and often on credit. No manufacturer could do that alone. The organisations that carry a product from maker to buyer are its <strong>marketing channel</strong>: the collaborators of Session 0 ([The 5Cs: reading the situation](/marketing-management/what-marketing-is/#the-5cs-reading-the-situation)).

Intermediaries exist because they do things a maker cannot do cheaply: reach lakhs of outlets, break a truckload into packets, offer the shopper many makers' goods in one place, extend credit, and know the local market. Each takes a margin for it, which is why a decision about Place changes the Price ([The opening exercise: pricing a smartphone](/marketing-management/what-marketing-is/#the-opening-exercise-pricing-a-smartphone)).

### How widely to distribute

| Intensity | What it means | Suits | Seen in these notes |
|---|---|---|---|
| Intensive | Every possible outlet | Low-priced, frequently bought and impulse goods | The candy that must be on every counter ([Sustaining a competitive advantage](/marketing-management/launching-and-growing/#sustaining-a-competitive-advantage)); Samsung's phones ([Everything must line up: Apple and Samsung](/marketing-management/the-marketing-plan/#everything-must-line-up-apple-and-samsung)) |
| Selective | A few chosen outlets in each area | Goods that need display, advice or service | Appliances; branded apparel |
| Exclusive | One or very few outlets, tightly controlled | Luxury and high-service goods | Apple's controlled outlets ([Everything must line up: Apple and Samsung](/marketing-management/the-marketing-plan/#everything-must-line-up-apple-and-samsung)); a separate premium channel ([How far can a brand stretch?](/marketing-management/positioning/#how-far-can-a-brand-stretch)) |

The choice is not a matter of taste. It follows from the positioning, as [Everything must line up: Apple and Samsung](/marketing-management/the-marketing-plan/#everything-must-line-up-apple-and-samsung) showed: narrow distribution protects a premium experience, and wide distribution is what a mass position requires.

### Push and pull, again

Session 0 used push and pull to contrast the selling concept with the marketing concept ([From the factory to the customer](/marketing-management/what-marketing-is/#from-the-factory-to-the-customer)). The same words name two ways of moving goods through a channel. A <strong>push strategy</strong> spends on the channel (trade margins, dealer schemes, a sales force) so that the channel pushes the product to buyers. A <strong>pull strategy</strong> spends on the buyer (advertising and brand building) so that buyers ask for the product and the channel has to stock it. Most firms use both. Push dominates where products are complex or unbranded; pull dominates for strong brands in low-involvement categories.

### Channel conflict and the online question

When a brand sells online for less than its dealers can, or opens its own store beside its distributor, the channel feels betrayed. This is <strong>channel conflict</strong>. The usual remedies are separate products or packs for separate channels, one price everywhere (the carmaker of [The opening exercise: pricing a smartphone](/marketing-management/what-marketing-is/#the-opening-exercise-pricing-a-smartphone) took over pricing for exactly this reason), and clear rules about who serves whom. Buyers, meanwhile, expect to discover a product in one place, buy it in another and return it to a third, and to be treated as one customer throughout: what the trade calls <strong>omnichannel</strong> retailing.

<strong>On a manager's desk.</strong> Choose channels after positioning, and budget for keeping them motivated. A product company should decide how intensively to distribute by asking where its target customer expects to find it. A service firm has channels too (its own sales team, partners, resellers, its website) and faces the same choices: sell direct to large accounts, use partners for reach, and settle in advance who owns which client.

## Promotion: the communication mix

Twice in class the professor corrected the idea that promotion means advertising ([What is marketing? The official definition](/marketing-management/what-marketing-is/#what-is-marketing-the-official-definition) and [Where to start: value first, then profile](/marketing-management/segmentation/#where-to-start-value-first-then-profile)). Promotion is everything a firm does to communicate with its market, and it has five main tools.

| Tool | What it is | Its strength | Its limit |
|---|---|---|---|
| Advertising | Paid, non-personal messages in mass or digital media | Reach; builds an image over time | Costly; easy to ignore |
| Sales promotion | Short-term incentives: discounts, bonus packs, coupons, contests, dealer schemes | A fast lift in sales | Trains buyers to wait for offers ([The hard part (1): cash and staying contemporary](/marketing-management/strong-brands/#the-hard-part-1-cash-and-staying-contemporary)) |
| Public relations and publicity | Earning favourable coverage; managing reputation and crises | Credible, because it is not paid for | Cannot be controlled (the Nano, [Great product, wrong fit: the Tata Nano](/marketing-management/the-marketing-plan/#great-product-wrong-fit-the-tata-nano)) |
| Personal selling | Face-to-face persuasion and relationship building | Tailored; closes complex sales | The most expensive per contact |
| Direct and digital marketing | Messages to identified individuals: email, apps, search, social media | Targeted and measurable | Intrusive if overdone |

Behind all five stands the one channel no firm can buy: word of mouth, which is how a candy became a national success before its first television advertisement ([Lessons from the case: pricing and promoting a novel product](/marketing-management/launching-and-growing/#lessons-from-the-case-pricing-and-promoting-a-novel-product)).

### From stranger to buyer: AIDA

A message has to move a person through stages before anything is bought. The oldest model of those stages is <strong>AIDA</strong>: attention, interest, desire, action.

A fuller version, the <strong>hierarchy of effects</strong>, has six steps: awareness, knowledge, liking, preference, conviction, purchase. The use of either model is the same: find the stage at which your target customers are stuck, and set the objective of the communication accordingly. Session 0 made this point through the life cycle: at introduction the job is awareness, and after that it is liking and preference: the step the deodorant leader's attention-grabbing advertisements never took ([The product life cycle](/marketing-management/the-marketing-plan/#the-product-life-cycle)).

### One voice, and a budget

<strong>Integrated marketing communications</strong> means that every tool says the same thing. The positioning statement of [The positioning statement](/marketing-management/positioning/#the-positioning-statement) is the brief that keeps them together. Media are often sorted into <strong>paid</strong> (advertising you buy), <strong>owned</strong> (your packs, stores, website) and <strong>earned</strong> (press coverage, reviews, shares).

| Budget method | The rule | The catch |
|---|---|---|
| Affordable | Spend what can be spared | Ignores the task to be done |
| Percentage of sales | A fixed share of revenue | Cuts spending exactly when sales fall |
| Competitive parity | Match what rivals spend | Assumes rivals know better |
| Objective and task | Set the objective, list the tasks needed, cost them | The logical method; needs homework |

<strong>On a manager's desk.</strong> Pick the tool for the stage. A product company in an impulse category needs advertising for attention and display for action; one selling a considered purchase needs trials and proof for desire. A service firm lives on personal selling and reputation: case studies and referrals are its advertising, and a rehearsed plan for the bad day is its public relations ([Availability: what comes to mind feels likely](/marketing-management/more-mental-shortcuts/#availability-what-comes-to-mind-feels-likely)).

## Services: selling what cannot be seen

A haircut cannot be inspected before you buy it. It is made while you sit there. It depends on who is holding the scissors that day. And the chair that stood empty at three o'clock cannot be sold at six. Those four observations are the theory of services.

| Characteristic | What it means | The problem it creates | What good firms do |
|---|---|---|---|
| Intangibility | Cannot be seen, touched or tried before buying | Buyers feel risk and judge by clues | Make it tangible: clean premises, uniforms, certificates, reports, guarantees |
| Inseparability | Produced and consumed at the same moment, with the customer present | The employee is the service | Hire and train front-line people with care; design the customer's part too |
| Variability | Quality depends on who delivers it, when and where | An uneven experience | Standard processes, checklists, measurement |
| Perishability | Cannot be stored | Idle capacity is lost for ever; peaks create queues | Shape demand (off-peak prices, bookings) and flex supply (part-time staff) |

Because of these four, the marketing mix for a service is extended from four Ps to seven, as [STP and the 4Ps](/marketing-management/the-marketing-plan/#stp-and-the-4ps) noted: <strong>People</strong>, the staff the customer meets; <strong>Process</strong>, the steps by which the service is delivered; and <strong>Physical evidence</strong>, everything visible that signals quality.

### What customers mean by quality

Research on service quality by Parasuraman, Zeithaml and Berry, known as SERVQUAL, found that customers judge a service on five dimensions. The first letters spell RATER.

| Dimension | What it means | An airline | A managed service |
|---|---|---|---|
| Reliability | Doing what was promised, dependably and accurately | Leaves and arrives on time | Every alert handled within the agreed time |
| Assurance | Knowledge and courtesy that inspire trust | A calm, competent crew | Certified analysts who can explain what they found |
| Tangibles | The physical evidence | A clean aircraft; a clear app | Clear dashboards and reports |
| Empathy | Caring, individual attention | Help for a passenger travelling with a child | Knowing each client's plant and priorities |
| Responsiveness | Willingness to help promptly | Quick rebooking after a cancellation | A human answer within minutes during an incident |

Reliability regularly comes out as the most important of the five. Customers arrive at their judgement by comparing what they <strong>expected</strong> with what they <strong>perceived</strong>; the difference is the service-quality gap, so managing expectations is half the job. The theme park that posts a thirty-minute wait and delivers twenty-five is managing expectations ([Anchoring at work](/marketing-management/how-buyers-decide/#anchoring-at-work)). And when a service fails, how it is put right matters enormously: the research the professor cited in Session 1, in which a failure handled well lifted a brand above its earlier rating ([Availability: what comes to mind feels likely](/marketing-management/more-mental-shortcuts/#availability-what-comes-to-mind-feels-likely)), is known as the <strong>service recovery paradox</strong>.

<div class="callout trap">
<p class="callout-title">Do not rely on the recovery paradox</p>

The effect is real but fragile. It appears after a first, modest failure that is fixed quickly and generously. Repeated failures destroy trust however well each is handled. Reliability comes first; recovery is the safety net.

</div>

<strong>On a manager's desk.</strong> Make the invisible visible and the variable consistent. A product company increasingly sells service around its product (installation, repair, advice) and should run it on the same five dimensions. A service firm should write down what it promises, measure reliability before anything else, turn its work into evidence the client can see, and treat its front-line people as the product.
