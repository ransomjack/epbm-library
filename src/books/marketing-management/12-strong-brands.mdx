The last regular session moved from STP to the brand itself. What makes brands like Apple and Harley-Davidson so powerful that people queue overnight for them, or tattoo the logo on their arms? What is a brand? Why do strong ones pay, and why are they so rare?

## Where we are in the framework

The professor opened with a recap. The broad framework of marketing management is STP followed by the 4Ps, and the 4Ps (product, price, place, promotion) follow from the decisions on target and positioning. That is why so much time went to STP; the 4Ps have been met mostly through cases, and the 5Cs support every one of these decisions.

Asked to define positioning without using the word "position", a participant offered "placement". The professor separated the terms: placement means shelf placement or product placement in films, each with its own technical meaning. Positioning is the <strong>image you want to create in customers' minds</strong>, different from competitors: target market plus point of difference. The positioning statement (who, what, why) aligns the organisation, and repositioning is hard and usually succeeds only in small steps. And expanding into a new segment while the point of difference stays the same is not repositioning; Cadbury's move was, because its target and its frame of reference both changed.

Today the focus moves to the brand. Brand management sits within the product P, and services are part of product too; a dedicated session on services, and one on pricing, were planned for the campus visit.

<strong>On a manager's desk.</strong> Brand decisions are not separate from STP; they are how STP lives on in customers' minds over years. A product brand that drifts from its positioning loses its meaning. A service brand is even more exposed, because every delivery is a fresh test of the promise.

## What strong brands look like

The professor showed a photograph: a long queue of tents outside an Apple store in Sydney, in the rain, the night before the iPhone 6 launch. One woman even sent a robot to stand in the queue for her. Similar queues have formed in India for later iPhones. How do you build a brand people will wait all night for?

She asked whose brands the participants were fans of. Answers included Apple, IFB (for the quality of its appliances), IKEA, Sony and a limited Adidas Yeezy drop that drew a queue at Connaught Place with people paying double. One example drew a distinction: queues outside a clothing store for a one-day 70% discount show the power of the discount, not the brand. A real fan gets excited about every new launch, reads about it, and will pay more: in money and in time.

Participants also described what makes IKEA special: it changed what furniture means. Furniture used to be bought to last a lifetime, with a salesperson by your side; IKEA offered good value and good design that you could change more often, chosen and carried by yourself. It changed buying behaviour in a whole category.

### Harley-Davidson: the poster child of branding

Harley-Davidson, founded in 1903, spends very little on advertising. At one point it did away with a separate branding department, because its employees and owners do the branding: they dress the part, ride together, and staff join the owners' rides. Its owners' club, the Harley Owners Group, goes by HOG. When the company turned 100 in 2003, around a lakh riders came to its home city, Milwaukee, to celebrate. Some owners tattoo the logo on their arms: a level of loyalty few brands could imagine.

Twice the company came close to bankruptcy, and twice the brand saved it. When Japanese motorcycles flooded the US (efficient, technologically advanced, cheaper) Harley tried to match them on volume and cost, and its quality suffered. It was acquired by AMF (American Machine and Foundry), which pushed production further and played down the Harley name; that did not sit well with riders, and debt mounted. A group of executives, with outside funding, bought the company back. Since then Harley has competed on legacy rather than on Japanese terms, and it now earns steady income by <strong>licensing</strong> its name to products from fragrances to cafes, even a Barbie collaboration.

A participant asked whether celebrities are brands too. Yes: marketing has moved from products to places to people. You yourself can build a brand, as strong as you make it.

<strong>On a manager's desk.</strong> The test of a strong brand is what customers will give up for it: time, money, or the cheaper option. A product company whose customers come back only for discounts has a promotion, not a brand. A service firm has a brand when clients ask for it by name in a tender and its own people describe it the same way the clients do.

## What a brand is

The word comes from the Old Norse <em>brandr</em>, meaning "to burn". In Scandinavian cattle-rearing communities, where nearly everyone kept cattle, owners burned a mark onto their animals with a hot iron so that each could tell his own from his neighbour's. A brand began as a mark of ownership and difference.

| View of a brand (from the slide) | What it stresses |
|---|---|
| The AMA: a customer experience represented by a collection of images and ideas; often a symbol such as a name, logo or slogan, or a combination, that identifies a seller's goods and services and differentiates them from competitors' | Both the experience and the identifying mark |
| A promise made by a firm to its customers | What the customer can expect, every time |
| A network of thoughts or associations, positive or negative, in consumer memory | Everything that comes to mind |

In the business press, "brand" and "rebranding" often refer only to the logo or symbol, which is the narrow sense. The fuller sense is the network of associations. Asked what Shah Rukh Khan stands for, participants said romance and dreams; someone else might think of his smoking. A strong brand needs mostly positive associations.

### Where brand management came from

In 1931 Neil McElroy, a young Harvard graduate at Procter & Gamble, was assigned to sell Camay soap. He found himself competing against P&G's own flagship soap, Ivory, with no dedicated resources. He wrote a memo to top management asking for a dedicated team for his brand. Management agreed, and the memo (still easy to find and read) became the birth certificate of <strong>brand management</strong>. Companies began to organise around brands, not just functions, with separate teams for separate brands, and the idea that each brand must be differentiated took hold.

<strong>On a manager's desk.</strong> Define your brand by the associations you want in customers' memories, not by the logo. A product company should list the five words it wants customers to think of, and check them in research. A service firm should do the same, and remember that its people are the most powerful source of those associations.

## Why build a strong brand

Participants listed the benefits:

- Customers proud to own it.
- Trust, loyalty and repeat sales.
- An emotional connection when the customer's values match the brand's.
- Easier launches of new products.
- Value for the company: brand equity and valuation.
- More impact from marketing and distribution, because distributors are keener to stock a strong brand. The slides added three more.

### 1. Brands are shortcuts for buyers

Recall the heuristics of Session 1. A buyer facing three televisions with similar features cannot tell them apart, so the brand becomes the deciding shortcut. A Sony fan or an IFB fan simply buys the brand again.

### 2. Brands earn the benefit of the doubt

In 1982, several people in the Chicago area died after taking <strong>Tylenol</strong>, Johnson & Johnson's leading pain reliever. Investigators found that someone had opened capsules and replaced the contents with cyanide; an employee was suspected, but it was never proved. Johnson & Johnson did not merely tell distributors to stop selling. It recalled Tylenol from the shelves (31 million bottles, per the slide) worked with agencies including the government to warn people, advertised heavily so that nobody would take it, and relaunched with tamper-proof packaging and tablets. As one adviser put it on the slide, before 1982 nobody ever recalled anything. It was the first major product recall, and the handling is still the model.

One would expect Tylenol's sales to have collapsed. In the professor's account, once it returned it regained its market share within a short time. Trust built over years carried the brand through.

<strong>Maggi</strong> is the Indian parallel. In 2015 it was accused of excess lead and MSG and taken off the shelves for about six months before being cleared. With about 75% of the market gone, who should have gained? Participants said pasta and macaroni; really, other noodle brands such as Yippee. Yet people lost trust in the whole noodle category and simply ate fewer noodles. When Maggi returned, it climbed back to about 60% of the market quickly. The brand, not the category, held the trust.

### 3. Brands shape perception, even taste

A study cited on the slide asked drinkers to rate six beer brands on taste. When they knew which brand they were drinking, the brands appeared spread across the taste map. When the brand names were hidden, five mass-market brands collapsed into one cluster: people could not taste the difference. Only Guinness, an Irish stout with a distinctly different texture and flavour, stayed apart. The brand name was doing much of the "tasting".

The professor linked this to the Coke and Pepsi blind tests: when the labels were hidden, people often preferred Pepsi; when they knew what they were drinking, the outcome changed. And a strong brand can charge a <strong>higher price</strong>.

<strong>On a manager's desk.</strong> Brand equity is insurance you buy before you need it. A product company that has earned trust can recall a faulty batch and survive, as Tylenol and Maggi did. A service provider that has built trust over years will be given time to explain an incident, provided it acts openly and fast, as Johnson & Johnson did.

## The hard part (1): cash and staying contemporary

If brands are so valuable, why are strong ones rare? Participants named maintaining the image, standing out, delivering on promises and earning trust. The professor organised the challenges as four Cs.

### Cash: the timing problem

The professor asked how long an IIM graduate typically stays in a first job: two to two and a half years. In the class the estimate was around three and a half. And how long does it take to build a strong brand: one that pulls customers, not one propped up by discounts? Participants guessed six months, then two to five years. The answer: <strong>decades</strong>. The few brands that seem to rise in two or three years are the survivors of thousands that failed and are never mentioned.

The costs of brand building are quantifiable, certain and immediate. The benefits are hard to quantify, uncertain and in the future. A manager with quarterly targets who expects to move on in three years has every reason to choose short-term measures instead.

</div>

The most common short-term measure is the frequent sales promotion. Run them often enough and customers stop buying the brand and start buying the promotion; raise the price later and nobody will pay it. In the professor's experience, this tenure mismatch is one of the strongest reasons brands decline.

### Contemporary: staying relevant

| Brand | What happened |
|---|---|
| Kodak (failure) | So dominant that a captured moment was called a "Kodak moment". Kodak actually invented the digital camera and sat on many patents, but feared hurting its film business. Engineers who showed new digital products were told to keep them in the drawer. Kodak was in the business of preserving memories; people found new ways to do that. |
| Ambassador (failure) | For over 30 years the king of Indian roads and a symbol of high office: prime ministers rode in one until Atal Bihari Vajpayee moved to a BMW. No change in design, poor fuel efficiency, little innovation; Maruti arrived with efficient modern cars and took the market. Today you see a few as Kolkata taxis. |
| HMT watches (failure) | The leading watch brand and the default gift. Titan arrived with quartz technology. Like Kodak, HMT had the technology but did not move in time; it was perhaps also badly timed and priced. (A participant noted a recent revival of interest in some HMT models.) |
| Lux (success) | The core product has barely changed for decades, but Lux keeps itself current by changing its endorsers: a long line of film heroines, from Leela Chitnis, the first Indian actress in a Lux ad, to Katrina Kaif. |
| Olay (success) | Once "Oil of Olay", with a mature woman's face on the pack. Over time "oil" came to suggest something sticky rather than good cosmetics. P&G dropped the word, refreshed the look and brought in younger faces: a successful repositioning. |

<strong>On a manager's desk.</strong> Fund the brand as an investment, and protect it from quarterly raids. A product company should cap how often a brand can be discounted. A service firm should keep investing in thought leadership and client experience even in a tight quarter, and keep watching whether its core service, like Kodak's film, is being made obsolete by something it already knows how to do.

## The hard part (2): clutter and consistency

### Clutter: standing out

Differentiation does not always need a new technology. Fogg's "no gas" product change (Session 0) is one route, but the slide listed others.

| Route to stand out | Examples (from the slide and class) |
|---|---|
| Advertising | Nirma's jingle, which people can still sing; Surf Excel |
| Packaging | Sunfeast Dark Fantasy; Nivea's blue tin, so distinctive that Nivea fought a competitor who launched in a similar blue pack |
| Logos | Thums Up, Nike |
| Mascot | Amul's butter girl: the product is the same butter, but the topical ads on politics, sport and the news keep the brand from looking old |
| Design | Pentonic pens: no technological breakthrough, but a fresh design in a dull category |

The professor played a scene from the TV series <em>Mad Men</em>. Cigarette makers can no longer make health claims, and a tobacco client wonders what is left to say. Six companies make six nearly identical products, so the agency can say anything, as long as it says it first. How is Lucky Strike made? The tobacco is grown, cut, cured and <strong>toasted</strong>. Everyone toasts tobacco, but the slogan becomes "It's toasted". The point: in a cluttered category, being the first to claim something can be enough to differentiate.

### Consistency: delivering the promise every time

Every customer touch point is a <strong>moment of truth</strong>, and the brand must deliver at each one: Starbucks was the slide's example. The difficulty of doing this for decades shows in the Fortune 500: of the companies on the first list in 1955, only about 60, roughly 12%, were still on it in 2017.

The Axe case (Session 0) is the cautionary tale. Axe was the first brand to make deodorant a real category in India, and led for years. But it kept saying the same thing, attract women, while copycats said it too, so it never found its "it's toasted". As leader, it also had a duty to grow the category: educate Indians on how to use a deodorant, reach rural buyers with small packs as sachet shampoos had done, move into perfumes. It did none of it in time, and a much smaller rival, Fogg, dislodged it.

<strong>On a manager's desk.</strong> Pick one memorable way to stand out, then deliver it the same way everywhere. A product company might own a pack shape or a mascot. A service firm stands out through a distinctive report, a named method or a response promise, but only if every analyst, every shift and every country delivers it identically.
