## Why stakeholders matter

Every firm's first job is obvious: return value to its shareholders. But no firm operates alone. It sits in a web of relationships, and each person or organisation in that web can help it succeed or quietly hold it back. The choice is not whether to deal with stakeholders. It is whether you do it deliberately, or get blindsided when you don't.

<div class="callout">
<p class="callout-title">Definition</p>

A **stakeholder** is any person or institution that is (a) **affected** by what the firm does, or (b) whose **approval** the firm may need in order to act. There is often no legal requirement to engage them. Ignoring them is simply expensive.

</div>

**Why it matters more now.** ESG (Environmental, Social and Governance) has turned stakeholder management from a box-ticking exercise into a boardroom priority. Investors screen companies on how they treat employees, communities and the environment, so getting stakeholders right now affects which investors will back a firm and on what terms.

## The eight stakeholder groups

Prof. Abraham's slides list exactly eight groups. Two of them are where most mistakes happen: **Regulatory Agencies** are a separate group from Government, and **Others** includes competitors.

<div class="callout beyond">
<p class="callout-title">Beyond the notes</p>

The idea goes back to R. Edward Freeman, whose 1984 book defined a stakeholder as any group or individual who can affect, or is affected by, the achievement of the organisation's objectives. The PPT's two-part definition (affected by you, or needed to approve you) is a practical version of the same idea.

</div>

## What do you want from each stakeholder?

Naming stakeholders is step one. The harder step is being clear about what each relationship is **for**. Four questions to ask:

- **Why does this stakeholder matter?** Name the specific value they make possible.
- **What role do they play in our success?** Customers give revenue. Employees give execution. Government gives the licence to operate.
- **How does our relationship with one affect the others?** This is the interdependency that Crane builds his whole argument on.
- **Can we meet everyone's needs at once?** Often not. But knowing the trade-off is very different from ignoring it.

Every response to one group is also a message to the rest, whether you intend it or not.

## The map keeps changing

A stakeholder map is a living document, not a one-off exercise:

- **Needs shift.** A regulator's priorities in 2019 are not its priorities in 2025.
- **Groups appear and disappear.** In Covid-19, vacuum-cleaner makers built ventilators and distilleries made hand sanitiser. Their stakeholder worlds changed in weeks.
- **Stakeholders can be substituted.** An NGO partnership can be replaced by a government one.
- **So mapping is continuous.** The PPT is explicit: identification and mapping is an ongoing process for the firm.

## Crane (2020): connected stakeholders and trust

Managers have long ranked stakeholders because resources are limited. Crane argues that this is increasingly dangerous, because in the information age **stakeholders are interconnected**. What you do to one group is visible to the others and becomes evidence of how trustworthy you are.

<div class="callout">
<p class="callout-title">Trust, defined</p>

Trust is a stakeholder's **willingness to be vulnerable** to a business (Pirson & Malhotra, 2011). Buying from a firm, working for it or investing in it all involve risk. Trust is what makes that risk acceptable.

</div>

Trust rests on three core dimensions (Mayer et al., 1995):

| Dimension | Meaning | Done well | Done badly |
|---|---|---|---|
| **Ability** | Competence to deliver on promises | Mattel: fast, global recall of faulty toys | Toyota: quick statement, slow corrective action |
| **Benevolence** | Genuine care for stakeholders | Siemens: voluntary USD 100M anti-corruption programme | Kia/Hyundai: refused a voluntary recall despite faulty parts |
| **Integrity** | Fairness, consistency, keeping promises | McDonald's Canada: videos explaining why ad food looks different | Toyota: regulator NHTSA called its statement "inaccurate and misleading" |

## How firms show trust: three signals

Stakeholders can't see ability, benevolence or integrity directly. They infer them from three kinds of behaviour.

**Voluntary action.** Freeman (1984): a solution imposed by a government agency or court must be seen as a managerial failure. Siemens, after its bribery scandal, voluntarily funded a 15-year, USD 100M anti-corruption programme. Kia and Hyundai waited to be forced into a recall and lost trust.

**Transparency.** McDonald's Canada published videos showing why food in ads looks different from what you are served. Toyota, by contrast, told dealers to inspect floor mats rather than warning customers directly.

**Responsiveness** has two parts: **timing** (act fast, because without information stakeholders assume the worst) and **ability** (you can actually fix it). Mattel recalled two-thirds of affected toys worldwide and backed it with a full campaign. Toyota spoke quickly but acted slowly, a lag blamed on its hierarchical structure.

## Crane's six propositions

| Prop. | What it says | Why it matters |
|---|---|---|
| P1 | Actions toward **one** stakeholder give trust cues to **all** others. | The foundation: nothing happens in isolation. |
| P2 | **Voluntary** actions raise the trust of observing stakeholders. | Benevolence shown to one group is seen by everyone. |
| P3 | **Transparent** actions raise the trust of observing stakeholders. | Open with one group, seen as open with all. |
| P4 | **Responsive** actions raise the trust of observing stakeholders. | Competence and care in one relationship reassure the rest. |
| P5 | **Proximity** amplifies the effect: closer stakeholders feel it most. | After Flint's water crisis, Americans who drink tap water felt the distrust. |
| P6 ★ | **Prioritising** stakeholders **lowers** trust in the firm. | Counter-intuitive: it undermines ability, benevolence and integrity all at once. |

<div class="callout trap">
<p class="callout-title">Why prioritisation backfires (P6)</p>

- **Ability:** lower-ranked stakeholders wonder whether the firm's resources will ever reach them.
- **Benevolence:** it creates a zero-sum game, helping some at others' expense. Boeing's demand for a USD 9B tax break damaged its image as a good corporate citizen.
- **Integrity:** anyone left out can call it favouritism. Wells Fargo's shareholder-first targets led staff to open 1.5M fake accounts.

</div>

<div class="callout beyond">
<p class="callout-title">Beyond the notes: don't mix up two models</p>

You may meet the **stakeholder salience** model of Mitchell, Agle & Wood (1997), which ranks stakeholders by **power, legitimacy and urgency**. It is a tool for prioritising, which is exactly what Crane warns about. "Power, legitimacy and urgency" are not the three dimensions of trust. Those are ability, benevolence and integrity.

</div>

## The class case: a dengue vaccine launched in emerging markets first

The session's discussion case followed a vaccine maker that spent about twenty years and a very large sum developing the world's first licensed dengue vaccine, and then reversed the industry's usual order of launch. The usual order is rich countries first, at high prices, and poorer countries years later. Dengue isn't a rich-country disease, so the firm ran its trials and launched first in the emerging markets where the disease lives.

The case earns its place because the launch depended almost entirely on stakeholders whose approval the firm needed and couldn't command.

| Stakeholders inside each country | Why they mattered |
|---|---|
| Ministry of Health | Champions a vaccine but usually has no budget of its own |
| Ministry of Finance | Holds the money. A vaccine can be approved and still not be bought |
| The national immunisation advisory group | The scientific gatekeeper: disease burden, safety, cost-effectiveness |
| The regulator | Market approval, country by country |
| Politicians | Blamed for outbreaks, eager for credit, afraid of side effects |
| Doctors and vaccinators | Have to be convinced and trained to deliver a multi-dose schedule |
| Parents and communities | Have to come back for every dose |
| Schools and the media | A channel for awareness, and a source of pressure |

| International stakeholders | Why they mattered |
|---|---|
| The World Health Organization and its expert advisers | One positive recommendation opens many countries at once |
| The global vaccine alliance | Funds vaccines for the poorest countries only. Most dengue-hit countries are middle-income: too rich for that funding, too poor for market prices |
| Pooled buyers such as UNICEF and the Pan-American fund | Bulk purchasing for groups of countries |
| Foundations and scientific partnerships | Credibility from third parties, and open data on the disease |

Read through Crane, the firm did a good deal right. It engaged communities during the trials, before anyone required it (voluntary action). It built open information platforms and answered health authorities from several countries together (transparency). It adapted programmes country by country (responsiveness). The gaps are just as instructive. The funding problem of middle-income countries, the largest stakeholder need, was never solved. And a vaccine that worked for about six people in ten was hard to explain to the public; one country that had taken part in the trials announced it would offer the vaccine and then reversed. A failure with one group sends its signal to all the others.

## Case reference map

Every company example from the Crane reading, with the one lesson to remember.

| Company | What happened | Lesson |
|---|---|---|
| Mattel ✔ | Lead paint in toys | All three dimensions at once: global recall (ability), CEO focus on child safety (benevolence), consistent campaign (integrity). 75% of Americans approved. |
| Toyota ✘ | Acceleration and floor-mat crisis | Failed on transparency (misleading statement) and responsiveness (slow action after fast words). |
| Volkswagen ✘ | Emissions scandal | Connectedness in action: one failure hit customers, employees, suppliers, investors and governments at once. |
| Nike ✘ | Conditions in Asian supplier factories | The workers weren't Nike's, yet customers, activists and regulators held Nike responsible. The supply chain is not a shield. |
| Siemens ✔ | Bribery scandal | Voluntary action rebuilt some trust: the chairman resigned, and a 15-year USD 100M programme followed. |
| Wells Fargo ✘ | 1.5M fake accounts | Shareholder-first targets drove the fraud; integrity collapsed and 5,300 staff were fired. |
| Boeing ✘ | Threat to leave Washington state | A USD 9B tax break plus pension concessions looked like zero-sum hardball and damaged benevolence across groups. |
| McDonald's ✔ | Food-appearance campaign | Voluntary transparency improved reputation and trust. |
| Flint, Michigan EPA ✘ | Lead in the water supply | Proximity (P5): a local failure became national distrust. Only 47% of Americans believed tap water was safe. |

<div class="callout beyond">
<p class="callout-title">Beyond the notes: using this at work</p>

Before a decision that affects one group, ask three questions. Who else will see this? What will they conclude about our ability, benevolence and integrity? Would we rather act now, voluntarily, than later, under pressure? It takes a minute and catches most of the failures in the table above.

</div>
