## Why firms invest abroad: four motives

Every cross-border investment fits one or more of four motives. "Tax avoidance" and "competitive retaliation" are sometimes suggested as a fifth. Neither is on Prof. Abraham's list.

<Cards min={200} items={[
  { k: '1', title: 'Resources', body: 'Energy, commodities or people that are scarce or costly at home.', foot: 'ONGC Videsh in Russian and Sudanese oil fields' },
  { k: '2', title: 'Markets', body: 'New customers, or spreading sales across markets.', foot: 'Tata Motors buying JLR for premium European and US buyers' },
  { k: '3', title: 'Efficiency', body: 'Lower cost, better quality, closeness to the customer.', foot: 'Apple manufacturing in India: cost, market access, incentives' },
  { k: '4', title: 'Strategic assets', body: 'R&D, capabilities, brands; a way round institutional limits or into a trading bloc.', foot: 'Wipro and HCL buying European IT firms for talent and clients' },
]} />

<div class="callout beyond">
<p class="callout-title">Beyond the notes</p>

These four come from John Dunning's well-known classification of FDI: resource-seeking, market-seeking, efficiency-seeking and strategic-asset-seeking. A single deal often has more than one motive. Tata–JLR was about markets **and** strategic assets (the brands).

</div>

## Horizontal vs vertical FDI

<Fig caption="Horizontal FDI repeats the chain; vertical FDI spreads its stages across countries.">
  <FDIChains />
</Fig>

Horizontal FDI repeats the firm's whole value chain in another country: a restaurant chain opening outlets in India sells the same product in a different place. Vertical FDI puts different stages of production in different countries, to use what each is best at. Take an airliner whose wings come from Japan, fuselage sections from Italy, tail from South Korea and trailing edges from Australia, with final assembly in the United States (the Boeing 787, put together at Everett).

The difference matters most when something breaks:

| Type | If one country is disrupted | Why |
|---|---|---|
| Horizontal | Only that country's market is hit | Each operation is self-contained. A problem in India doesn't stop Germany. |
| Vertical | Third countries, sometimes the whole world, are hit | Each stage feeds the next. Break one link and the chain stops. |

**Why vertical FDI grew so much:**

1. **Transport and communication got far cheaper.** By 2005, sea freight cost about 20% of what it did in 1930.
2. **Trade barriers fell** through GATT/WTO rounds and regional blocs.
3. **Competitive pressure.** Once one rival cut costs by going abroad, the others had to follow.

## Six country risks

Before investing anywhere, assess these six.

<Cards min={220} items={[
  { k: '1', title: 'Political', body: 'Instability, expropriation, policy reversals, sanctions.', foot: 'Venezuela nationalising oil; sanctions on Russia after 2022' },
  { k: '2', title: 'Economic', body: 'Recession, high inflation, shrinking GDP: the market gets less attractive.', foot: 'Eurozone debt crisis spreading from Greece to Spain, Portugal, Cyprus' },
  { k: '3', title: 'Transfer', body: 'Limits on sending profits, dividends, royalties or capital home.', foot: 'China capital controls on profit repatriation' },
  { k: '4', title: 'Exchange rate', body: 'Currency moves shrink earnings in home-currency terms.', foot: 'A Tata subsidiary earning GBP, reported in INR' },
  { k: '5', title: 'Location / Neighbourhood', body: 'Instability next door, even if the host country is stable.', foot: 'Investing in Jordan, beside Syria and Iraq' },
  { k: '6', title: 'Sovereign', body: 'The government defaults on its debt or repudiates obligations.', foot: "Argentina's repeated defaults hit every business there" },
]} />

<div class="callout trap">
<p class="callout-title">Common mix-up</p>

**Cultural risk** and **regulatory risk** are sometimes suggested as a seventh category. Neither is on Prof. Abraham's list. The six above are the complete set.

</div>

<div class="callout beyond">
<p class="callout-title">Beyond the notes: how firms manage each risk</p>

- **Political:** political-risk insurance (for example from the World Bank's MIGA), local joint-venture partners.
- **Economic:** spreading investment across markets; staged entry.
- **Transfer:** taking money out as royalties or fees, or reinvesting locally.
- **Exchange rate:** hedging, or matching local costs to local revenue (a "natural hedge").
- **Location:** contingency plans for routes and staff that run through the neighbourhood.
- **Sovereign:** limiting dependence on government contracts and payments.

</div>

## PEST: scanning the big picture

PEST identifies the external, macro-level factors that shape a firm's competitive position. Its output goes straight into the **Opportunities** and **Threats** of a SWOT.

<div class="callout">
<p class="callout-title">The golden rule</p>

Only include a factor if it (a) **directly affects** how attractive the industry is, and (b) is **likely to change** in the foreseeable future. Everything else is noise. Without this filter PEST turns into an endless list with no conclusion.

</div>

  { k: 'S', title: 'Social', body: 'Demographics and ageing, lifestyles and attitudes, the pull of advertising and influencers, cultural and religious factors.', foot: 'Ryanair made flying a no-frills commodity; ageing populations lift medicine demand' },
  { k: 'T', title: 'Technological', body: 'New materials, processes and products; new channels (e-commerce) and pricing (dynamic pricing, auctions); standard wars, where two formats fight to become the industry standard.', foot: 'VHS vs Betamax' },

**Disintermediation and re-intermediation** are easy to confuse:

- **Disintermediation:** technology **removes** a middleman. Travel agents replaced by online booking.
- **Re-intermediation:** technology **creates** a new middleman. Amazon, eBay and Expedia are re-intermediation, not disintermediation.

## Where foreign investment went in 2024

| Region | FDI in 2024 | Change |
|---|---|---|
| Developed economies | USD 867 billion | Flat. The largest in absolute value |
| Developing Asia | USD 605 billion | −3%. The largest among developing regions |
| North America | USD 343 billion | +23% |
| Europe | USD 182 billion | −58%. The steepest fall |
| Latin America and the Caribbean | USD 164 billion | −12% |
| Africa | USD 97 billion | +75%. The fastest growth, not the largest amount |
| Least developed countries | USD 37 billion | +9% |

Figures from UNCTAD, as given in class. Investments in developing economies earned roughly 8 to 11% a year between 2010 and 2018, against about 6% in developed ones: higher risk, higher reward. And "born global" firms don't spend a decade building strength at home first; they go international almost from the start.

## The class case: a record fine in Nigeria

The session's discussion case was a South African mobile operator that had become the largest in Africa and, in October 2015, was fined about USD 5.2 billion by Nigeria's telecom regulator over 5.1 million SIM cards that hadn't been properly registered. It was the largest fine imposed on a telecom company anywhere at the time, about a fifth of the group's market value, and its shares fell more than 12% that day.

**How it had grown.** It entered markets early, before rivals. It built the prepaid model for customers without bank accounts. It was willing to operate in difficult, even war-torn places that others avoided. It had scale, and it bought its way into new markets through a large acquisition. In the four motives above, this is market-seeking investment, at scale.

**Why compliance was hard.** The business had been built on anonymous prepaid SIMs, so registering tens of millions of users with fingerprints and photographs had no precedent. Scanners and reliable power were scarce. Many people were reluctant to give biometric data, or simply didn't turn up. Dealers pre-registered SIMs in their own names and resold them. The operators believed they were still negotiating numbers with the regulator when the deadline passed. And switching off millions of lines meant losing revenue to rivals.

**What it could have done differently.** Treated registration as a board-level risk and not a local administrative task; taken registration to customers with vans and agents, and rewarded them for completing it; argued, with data, for standards the country's infrastructure could support. Above all, it could have read the context. Oil revenue had collapsed, a new government had just taken office on an anti-corruption platform, and the operator's licence was coming up for renewal. Every one of those is a PEST signal. It waited to be fined before engaging at the political level.

| PEST | What the case shows |
|---|---|
| Political | A security-driven registration rule; a new government; a regulator with something to prove |
| Economic | A fiscal crisis that made a very large fine attractive to the state |
| Social | Distrust of handing personal data to government; an unbanked, prepaid customer base |
| Technological | Biometric equipment and power that weren't there at the scale required |

The lesson ties this session to the first: in frontier markets, regulatory compliance and stakeholder relationships are the same job. A regulator is a stakeholder whose approval you need, and waiting for it to act is the managerial failure Freeman described.

<div class="callout beyond">
<p class="callout-title">Beyond the notes: where PEST sits</p>

PEST looks at the **macro** environment. Porter's Five Forces looks at the **industry** (rivals, buyers, suppliers, entrants, substitutes). SWOT pulls both together with the firm's own strengths and weaknesses. A good strategy paper usually moves in that order: PEST, then Five Forces, then SWOT.

</div>
