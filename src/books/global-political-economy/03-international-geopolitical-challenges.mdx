## New nationalism and trade wars

The densest part of the slides.

- **Growing nationalism:** governments screen foreign investment, promote domestic investment, stress national resilience, and put hurdles in front of M&A, FDI and R&D.
- **More trade restrictions:** higher tariffs (US tariffs on Chinese goods), withdrawn concessions (the US ended India's GSP benefits in **2019**), tighter rules of origin.
- **Uncertain trade with China:** growth slowing from about 10% to about 5%; an India–China trade deficit of **USD 112.60 billion in 2025-26** despite political tension; chip wars, with the US restricting semiconductors to China and Taiwan's TSMC at the centre; technology decoupling; pressure to exclude Huawei from 5G.
- **A weaker WTO:** the US has blocked appointments to the Appellate Body, so the dispute-settlement "court" can't function, and countries fall back on bilateral or unilateral action.

## Bozonelos & Tsagdis (2023): geopolitical resilience

**The problem they address:** even after the financial crisis, Covid and Russia–Ukraine, international business research has treated geopolitics in a fragmented way. The word is mostly used as an adjective, with little practical guidance.

<div class="callout">
<p class="callout-title">Definition</p>

**Geopolitics** (Caldara & Iacoviello, 2022): how geography affects politics and relations between states. It is shaped by geography, natural and man-made resources, demographics and international relations.

</div>

### Six levels of analysis

<Fig caption="Each level sits inside the next. Spheres of influence belong to the Global level, not National or Supra-national.">
  <Levels />
</Fig>

<div class="callout trap">
<p class="callout-title">Common trap</p>

The order is Individual → Firm → Sub-national → National → Supra-national → Global. Individual means "strong men", leaders whose personal decisions shape events. Firm means multinationals, joint ventures and alliances. Sub-national means clusters and entrepreneurial ecosystems such as Silicon Valley or Bangalore. National means varieties of capitalism and institutions. Supra-national means regions and trading blocs such as the EU, ASEAN and RCEP. Global means spheres of influence. Don't confuse these six levels with McKinsey's six dimensions of resilience, which is a different list.

</div>

The backdrop to all of this is the **Liberal International Order**: the system of free trade and multilateral institutions built by the US and its allies after the Second World War, which the first phase took for granted.

The framework's most practical tool. It asks where the firm stands (inside or outside itself) and which way it looks (in or out).

## Four actionable insights

1. **Treat geopolitics as continuous, not occasional.** It is not a shock that interrupts normal business; like climate change, it is always running in the background.
2. **Anticipate fat-tail events.** They look unexpected but are not actually rare; conventional risk models just give them too little weight. Russia's invasion of Ukraine had plenty of warning signs, and Western firms in Russia ignored them.
3. **Move from risk to resilience.** Risk analysis is not enough. Build resilience with scenario planning, contrarian thinking and insurance, across McKinsey's six dimensions: reputational, organisational, financial, technological, operational and business model.
4. **Pursue multilevel analysis.** All six levels matter together, and every dimension of resilience plays out across them.

### "Bouncing with": the paper's big idea

<Fig caption="Illustrative shapes, not data. The dashed line is pre-setback performance; the vertical line marks the setback.">
  <Bounce />
</Fig>

The first three all assume the setback has already happened. **Bouncing with** means anticipating and managing so that the big hit never lands: continuous, proactive, built in.

<div class="callout">
<p class="callout-title">The turkey analogy (Taleb, 2010)</p>

A turkey is fed every day for 1,000 days and concludes that it is safe and loved, until Thanksgiving. Most Western firms in Russia were the turkey: years of normal business, months of warning signs ignored, then caught completely off guard by the invasion.

</div>

Inside-In is the trap: perfect internal optimisation with no view of the world outside is rearranging the deckchairs on the Titanic.

## The class case: rethinking "Made in China"

The session's discussion case was a Chinese maker of grilling and kitchen products, founded in 2003, with about USD 170 million in revenue, some 200 customers (large Western brands and retailers) and eleven factories in four countries, most of them in China. In January 2023 its two founders flew to Mexico to look for factory sites, because every customer had told them the same thing: some part of production has to leave China, and 30% is the number heard most.

**Why the customers worried.** Tariffs of 7.5 to 25% on a very large volume of Chinese goods since 2018. The risk of conflict around Taiwan and the shipping lanes. The fragility that zero-Covid lockdowns exposed. Consumers saying they would avoid Chinese-made products. And export controls that create legal and reputational exposure. These are the three events at the top of this chapter, all at once.

**Why moving is hard.** Making complex products elsewhere can cost well over half as much again, and customers want diversification without paying for it. China's manufacturing ecosystem, built over forty years, isn't quickly copied: the firm relied on hundreds of Chinese suppliers. Each new country takes capital, a year or more to ramp up, and new systems and skills. "We have to learn global operation from scratch."

**Should it go further than Asia?** For: every customer demands it; four Asian locations could all be hit by the same shock; North American customers need production near them to avoid tariffs, and European customers need it near Europe; and the shift looks structural, not temporary. Against: scarce capital spread too thin, lower quality at first, a stretched management team, and slowing demand. The sensible path discussed was to diversify step by step, deepening existing sites and entering new regions through partners, not by building alone.

| Idea from this chapter | In the case |
|---|---|
| Near-shoring and reshoring | Mexico for North America, a site near Europe for European customers |
| Just-in-case | Spare capacity outside China as a hedge, not an endpoint |
| The four views | The firm had to move from fixing its own factories (Inside-In) to building a portfolio of locations (Outside-In) |
| Bouncing with | Acting before a tariff or a conflict forces it |
| Fat tails | A Taiwan crisis is discussed openly; treating it as unthinkable would be the turkey's mistake |
