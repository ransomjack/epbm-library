## Why stakeholder identification matters

Every business has an obvious first job: deliver returns to shareholders. But a firm does not operate in a bubble. It sits inside a web of relationships, and each person or organisation in that web can either help the firm succeed or quietly undermine it. The real question is not whether to engage stakeholders, but whether you do it on purpose or get blindsided when you don't.

<div class="callout">
<p class="callout-title">Definition of a stakeholder</p>

Any person or institution who is (a) **affected** by what a firm does, or (b) whose **approval** the firm may need to act. There is often no legal requirement to engage them, but ignoring them can prove very costly.

</div>

**Why it matters more than ever: the ESG era.** ESG (Environmental, Social, and Governance) has moved stakeholder management from a box-ticking exercise to a boardroom priority. Investors now screen firms on how they treat employees, communities, and the environment. Getting stakeholders right means long-term success.

## The eight stakeholder groups

Prof. Abraham's PPT identifies exactly eight categories. The two that students miss most are Regulatory Agencies (separate from Government) and the "Others" bucket, which includes Competitors.

| Stakeholder | What they care about |
|---|---|
| ① Customers | Revenue, loyalty, product quality, brand trust |
| ② Investors / Partners | Returns, risk, governance quality |
| ③ Employees & Families | Jobs, pay, culture, safety, career growth |
| ④ Suppliers & Distributors | Contracts, payment terms, supply chain stability |
| ⑤ Government | Tax revenue, policy compliance, national interest |
| ⑥ Regulatory Agencies | Compliance, licences, safety standards, permits |
| ⑦ Community | Social licence, environmental impact, local employment |
| ⑧ Others (NGOs, Media, Environmental Groups, Competitors) | Advocacy, public opinion, watchdog, competitive pressure |

<div class="callout trap">
<p class="callout-title">Exam trap</p>

Regulatory Agencies is a **separate** group from Government. And "Others" includes Competitors. Both are frequent MCQ surprises.

</div>

## What do you want from stakeholders?

Identifying stakeholders is only step one. The harder question is what the relationship is actually for. Four questions every manager must answer:

- **Why is this stakeholder important?** Force yourself to name the specific value they enable.
- **What role do they play in the firm's success?** Customers = revenue. Employees = execution. Government = licence to operate.
- **How does our relationship with one affect others?** The interdependency insight. Connects directly to Crane's connectedness theory.
- **Can we address all stakeholder needs at once?** Often no. But knowing the trade-offs is different from ignoring them.

<div class="callout">
<p class="callout-title">Watch out</p>

Addressing one stakeholder's needs can influence how **all** other stakeholders view you, positively or negatively. You are always sending signals, whether you intend to or not.

</div>

## Stakeholder relationships are dynamic

Stakeholder management is not a one-time document. It is a living discipline. Four reasons the map constantly shifts:

- **Needs change over time.** A regulator's priorities in 2019 are not those of 2025.
- **Some become irrelevant; new ones emerge.** Covid-19 example: vacuum cleaner firms made ventilators; alcohol distilleries made hand-sanitisers. The entire stakeholder universe transformed in weeks.
- **Stakeholders can be replaced or substituted.** An NGO partnership can be swapped for a government one.
- **Mapping must be continuous.** The PPT is explicit that stakeholder identification and mapping needs to be a continuous process for the firm.

## Five pillars of good stakeholder relationships

MCQs often present a list and ask which one is **not** on the list. Know all five:

| Pillar | What it means | What it is not |
|---|---|---|
| ① Act in their best interests | Genuine good intent, not just performative | Acting only when legally required |
| ② Visibility of action | Stakeholders must **see** what you are doing | Doing good things in private |
| ③ Explain your motives | Communicate **why** you are acting | Silent action with no explanation |
| ④ Respond proactively | Act **before** regulators or courts force you to | Reactive compliance = managerial failure (Freeman) |
| ⑤ Transparency & integrity | Consistent, honest, and open in all dealings | Selective transparency; hiding problems |

<div class="callout trap">
<p class="callout-title">Most tested pillar</p>

Pillar ④, proactive response. If a question mentions "waiting for regulatory or judicial intervention", that is the **wrong** approach per the PPT.

</div>

## Crane (2020): core argument and trust framework

Crane's central argument: managers have long tried to prioritise stakeholders because resources are limited. But in the information age this is increasingly dangerous, because stakeholders are **interconnected**. What you do to one group is now **visible** to all the others, and sends signals about your trustworthiness across the whole ecosystem.

<div class="callout">
<p class="callout-title">Trust defined</p>

From Pirson & Malhotra (2011): trust is a stakeholder's **willingness to be vulnerable** to a business. When you buy from a firm, work for it, or invest in it, you are taking a risk. Trust is what makes that risk feel acceptable.

</div>

The three core trust dimensions (Mayer et al., 1995):

| Dimension | Meaning | Good example | Bad example |
|---|---|---|---|
| **Ability** | Operational competence to deliver on promises | Mattel: swift global recall of faulty toys | Toyota: slow corrective action despite quick statement |
| **Benevolence** | Genuine care and good intent toward stakeholders | Siemens: voluntary USD 100M anti-corruption programme | Kia/Hyundai: refused voluntary recall despite faulty parts |
| **Integrity** | Fairness, consistency; keeping promises; acting on stated values | McDonald's: transparency videos on food appearance | Toyota: NHTSA called their statement "inaccurate and misleading" |

## Three supplementary trust signals

These three signals are how firms **demonstrate** the three core trust dimensions in practice.

<div class="callout">
<p class="callout-title">① Voluntary action signals benevolence</p>

Acting without being forced by courts, regulation, or public pressure. Freeman (1984): "A solution imposed by a government agency or courts must be seen as a managerial failure."

**Good:** Siemens voluntarily set up a 15-year USD 100M anti-corruption programme. **Bad:** Kia/Hyundai waited for a forced recall, and trust was destroyed.

</div>

<div class="callout">
<p class="callout-title">② Transparency signals integrity</p>

Sharing clear, accurate information proactively. If you have nothing to hide, you share openly, and stakeholders notice.

**Good:** McDonald's Canada ran videos showing why ads look different from real food. **Bad:** Toyota told dealers to inspect floor mats instead of warning customers directly.

</div>

<div class="callout">
<p class="callout-title">③ Responsiveness signals ability and benevolence</p>

Quickly detecting **and** acting on stakeholder needs. Two components: **timing** (act fast, because in the absence of information stakeholders assume the worst) and **ability** (you can actually fix the problem).

**Good:** Mattel recalled 2/3 of affected toys globally and ran a full marketing campaign. **Bad:** Toyota made a fast statement but was slow to act, blamed on its hierarchical structure.

</div>

## The six propositions (Crane 2020)

These six propositions are high-value for MCQs. The most counter-intuitive is P6.

| Prop. | What it says | Why it matters |
|---|---|---|
| P1 | Actions toward **one** stakeholder provide trust cues to **all** others. | Foundation of the whole argument. Nothing happens in isolation. |
| P2 | **Voluntary** actions increase trust of observing stakeholders. | Benevolence inferred from voluntary action transfers to the whole audience. |
| P3 | **Transparent** actions increase trust of observing stakeholders. | Integrity signals generalise: open with one = seen as open with all. |
| P4 | **Responsive** actions increase trust of observing stakeholders. | Ability and benevolence demonstrated in one relationship reassures all others. |
| P5 | **Proximity** amplifies trust effects. Closer stakeholders feel it most. | Flint water crisis: all Americans who drink tap water felt the distrust. |
| P6 ★ | **Prioritisation** of stakeholders **decreases** trust in the firm. | Counter-intuitive. Undermines ability, benevolence, and integrity simultaneously. |

<div class="callout trap">
<p class="callout-title">Why prioritisation destroys trust (P6 deep dive)</p>

- **Ability:** secondary stakeholders wonder "will the firm's resources ever reach me?", which reduces their willingness to be vulnerable.
- **Benevolence:** it creates a zero-sum mentality, helping some at the expense of others (e.g. Boeing's USD 9B tax break demand destroyed perceived benevolence).
- **Integrity:** any unprioritised stakeholder can call it favouritism (e.g. Wells Fargo's shareholder-first culture led to 1.5M fake accounts and a trust collapse).

</div>

## Case study reference map

All company examples from the Crane reading. Know the lesson for each:

| Company | Situation | Key lesson |
|---|---|---|
| Mattel ✔ | Lead-based paint in toys | All 3 dimensions at once: Ability (global recall) + Benevolence (CEO on child safety) + Integrity (consistent campaign). 75% American approval. |
| Toyota ✘ | Acceleration / floor mat crisis | Failed Transparency (misleading statement) and Responsiveness (slow action despite fast words). |
| Volkswagen ✘ | Emissions scandal | Textbook connectedness: one failure cascaded to customers, employees, suppliers, investors, and government simultaneously. |
| Nike ✘ | Asian factory conditions | Workers were suppliers' employees, yet customers, activists and regulators held Nike responsible. Supply chain is not a shield. |
| Siemens ✔ | Bribery scandal | Voluntary action restored partial trust: chairman resigned, voluntary 15-year USD 100M programme. |
| Wells Fargo ✘ | 1.5M fake accounts | Shareholder-first targets led employees to create fake accounts. Integrity destroyed; 5,300 fired. |
| Boeing ✘ | Leaving Washington threat | USD 9B tax break + pension concessions = zero-sum hardball. Benevolence destroyed across all groups. |
| McDonald's ✔ | Food appearance campaign | Voluntary transparency (videos on why ad food ≠ real food) improved reputation and trust. |
| Flint, Michigan EPA ✘ | Lead in water supply | Proximity (P5) example: local EPA failure led to national distrust. Only 47% of Americans believed tap water was safe. |
