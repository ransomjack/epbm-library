## Why do firms invest abroad? Four motivations

Every cross-border investment maps onto one or more of these four categories. MCQs often present a fifth fake option like "tax avoidance" or "competitive retaliation". Neither is on Prof. Abraham's list.

| Motivation | What the firm seeks | Indian example |
|---|---|---|
| ① **Resources** | Energy, commodities, human resources not available or cheaper domestically | ONGC Videsh investing in Russian and Sudanese oil fields |
| ② **Markets** | New markets to sell into; diversification across existing markets | Tata Motors buying JLR to access premium European and US car markets |
| ③ **Efficiency** | Lower costs, better quality, closeness to the market being served | Apple manufacturing in India: cost + market proximity + government incentives |
| ④ **Strategic assets** | Acquire R&D, capabilities, brands; bypass institutional constraints; gain a trading-bloc foothold | Wipro, HCL buying European IT firms to acquire talent and client relationships |

## Horizontal vs. vertical FDI

**Horizontal FDI:** a firm replicates its **entire** value chain in a host country. It operates in one product category but does so in multiple countries. Think McDonald's opening restaurants in India: same product, different country.

**Vertical FDI:** different **stages** of production are placed in different countries to exploit each country's specific advantages. Think Boeing 787: wings from Japan, fuselage from Italy, tail from South Korea, trailing edges from Australia, final assembly in Everett, Washington.

| Type | Scope of risk when disrupted | Why? |
|---|---|---|
| Horizontal FDI | Affects that host-country market only | Each country's operation is self-contained. A problem in India does not stop Germany. |
| Vertical FDI | Can affect third-country and sometimes **global** markets | Each stage feeds the next. Disrupt one node and you break the entire global chain. |

**Three reasons vertical FDI grew substantially:**

1. Dramatic decline in transport and communication costs (sea freight fell to ~20% of its 1930 cost by 2005).
2. Substantial reduction in trade barriers through GATT/WTO rounds and regional blocs.
3. Competitive pressure: once one rival cut costs by going abroad, others had to follow.

## Six country risk categories

Before any cross-border investment, firms must assess country-level risk systematically. Know all six. MCQs often give five correct ones and one impostor.

| Risk type | What it covers | Real-world example |
|---|---|---|
| ① **Political** | Government instability, expropriation, policy reversals, sanctions | Venezuela nationalising oil; Russia sanctions post-2022 |
| ② **Economic** | GDP contraction, high inflation, recession; market attractiveness declining | Eurozone sovereign debt crisis spreading from Greece to Spain, Portugal, Cyprus |
| ③ **Transfer** | Restrictions on repatriating profits, dividends, royalties, or capital | China capital controls limiting profit repatriation by foreign multinationals |
| ④ **Exchange rate** | Currency fluctuation making revenues worth less in home-country terms | Tata subsidiary earning GBP: INR value swings with the GBP/INR rate |
| ⑤ **Location / Neighbourhood** | Risk from geographic proximity to unstable neighbours, even if the host country is stable | Investing in Jordan: affected by instability in Syria and Iraq next door |
| ⑥ **Sovereign** | Risk that government defaults on debt or repudiates its obligations | Argentina's repeated sovereign defaults: every business in-country affected |

## PEST analysis: the core framework

PEST is a framework for identifying four categories of external macro-environmental factors that affect a firm's competitive position. It feeds directly into the Opportunities and Threats part of a SWOT analysis.

<div class="callout">
<p class="callout-title">Golden rule of PEST</p>

Only include factors that (a) have a **direct** bearing on industry attractiveness and (b) are **likely to change** in the foreseeable future. Everything else is noise. Without this discipline, PEST becomes an "endless evaluation" with inconclusive results.

</div>

### ① Political factors

They operate at three levels: supranational (WTO, EU), national (government policy), subnational (state/municipal).

- Trade policies, tariffs, taxation, fiscal policy
- Labour and environmental legislation
- Licensing, approval procedures, lobbying
- Corruption levels, national incentives for enterprise
- Examples: EU tobacco ad ban led Formula One to move to Dubai. Google left China due to censorship. The Arab Spring made firms rethink MENA strategy.

### ② Economic factors

These have the most obvious impact on profitability.

- GDP per capita (at PPP): primary demand indicator for income-elastic industries
- Inflation erodes purchasing power **and** raises input costs
- Exchange rates directly hit margins for international firms
- Unemployment erodes disposable income **but** provides cheaper labour
- Income-**elastic** industries: jewellery, construction, entertainment, tourism, luxury. Demand swings with GDP.
- Income-**inelastic** industries: staple foods, healthcare, basic commodities. Demand stays stable in recession.

### ③ Social factors

- Demographics (age, population growth, ageing populations)
- Psychographics, lifestyle, consumer perception
- Effect of advertising, influencers, racial/ethnic/religious influences
- Examples: Ryanair repositioned air travel as a no-frills commodity. Ageing populations mean rising demand for medicines.

### ④ Technological factors

- New materials, machinery, software; innovations in processes and product design
- New distribution channels (e-commerce) and pricing innovations (dynamic pricing, auctions)
- **Disintermediation:** technology **removes** old middlemen (travel agents replaced by online booking)
- **Re-intermediation:** technology **creates** new middlemen. Amazon, eBay, Expedia are **not** disintermediation; they are re-intermediation.
- Standard wars: two formats fighting to become the industry standard (e.g. VHS vs Betamax)

## PEST variations and the 5-step process

Know all five variations and their distinguishing features. MCQs test the differences precisely.

| Framework | Adds to PEST | Key distinguisher |
|---|---|---|
| PEST | — | Baseline. Political, Economic, Social, Technological. |
| PESTEL / PESTLE | + Environmental + Legal | Adds ecology and legal compliance. Same framework, different letter order. |
| STEEPLE | + Environmental + Legal + Ethics | Takes a CSR perspective. The ethics addition is deliberate. |
| STEEPLED | + Environmental + Legal + Ethics + Demographics | Most comprehensive version. Adds demographic analysis. |
| STEER | + Regulatory factors | Created because of increased environmental awareness. |

**The 5-step PEST process:**

1. **Identify:** current **and** future factors in all four environments.
2. **Analyse:** the possible effect of each factor on competitive position.
3. **Categorise:** each factor as an opportunity or threat (feeds the OT of SWOT).
4. **Prioritise:** by extent of impact and how long it will last.
5. **Act:** develop strategy. Correct threats, build on opportunities.

<p class="callout-title">Quick exam checks</p>

- Which takes a CSR perspective? **STEEPLE.**
- Which adds Demographics? **STEEPLED.**
- Which adds Regulatory? **STEER.**
- PEST E = **Economic**, not Environmental. Environmental appears only in PESTEL/PESTLE.
- In SWOT, PEST gives O and T. Strengths and Weaknesses are internal.
