## A different lens: inside the business

Sessions 1 and 2 read a company from the **outside**, as an investor, banker or regulator would. Session 3 turns the camera round. You are now **inside** the business, planning budgets, setting prices, deciding whether to launch a product, and deciding whether to close a division.

| | Financial accounting (Sessions 1–2) | Management / cost accounting (Session 3) |
|---|---|---|
| Audience | Shareholders, banks, regulators | Managers inside the firm |
| Format | Standardised by ICAI; no discretion | No fixed format; build what you need |
| Safety net | Auditors catch errors | None. A wrong analysis costs the business. |
| Time focus | Records what already happened | Helps you decide before you act |
| Reports | Public, formal annual report | Private, flexible, purpose-built |

> "The mother system is the same. But today we are looking at its other arm, and the audience is you: the internal decision-maker." Prof. Manju Jaiswall

## Classifying cost

### Start with the cost object

Before you classify a single rupee, decide **what you are costing**: a product, a batch, a programme, a project, a customer, a department. That is the **cost object**, and everything else depends on it.

> "Cost does not exist in a vacuum. It always exists in a context. Define the cost object first; everything else follows." Prof. Manju Jaiswall

### Direct vs indirect

| | Direct cost | Indirect cost (overhead) |
|---|---|---|
| Definition | Traced directly and conveniently to the cost object | Can't be traced easily; must be **allocated** |
| Link to output | Clear and high | Indirect; needs a proxy |
| Examples | Plastic in a computer; study material for EPBM-32 | Factory rent, supervisor salary, insurance, machine depreciation |
| How assigned | Measured directly | Through a **cost driver**: units, machine hours, participants |

**Why the driver choice matters more today.** Overhead used to be about 20% of total cost, so a poor driver barely mattered. Automation has cut direct labour, and overhead is now 40–50% of cost in many industries. A poor driver on that base produces large costing errors, which flow straight into wrong prices and wrong strategy.

### Product cost vs period cost

| | Product cost | Period cost |
|---|---|---|
| Timing | Travels **with** the product through inventory | Hits the P&L in the period it is incurred |
| Becomes an expense | Only when the product is **sold** (as COGS) | Immediately, in full |
| Examples | Direct material, direct labour, manufacturing overhead | Advertising, sales commissions, head-office admin |
| Numbers | Spend Rs.5L making 100 computers, sell 80: Rs.4L is COGS, Rs.1L stays as inventory | Rs.50,000 of advertising, 80 of 100 sold: the full Rs.50,000 hits the P&L |

<div class="callout trap">
<p class="callout-title">Product cost ≠ manufacturing cost</p>

Manufacturing cost is what it costs to **make** the product. It becomes an expense (COGS) only when units are **sold**; until then it sits on the balance sheet as **inventory**, an asset. Period costs hit the P&L in full, even if nothing is sold.

</div>

### Mixed costs: splitting fixed from variable

Many real costs are part fixed, part variable. An electricity bill has a fixed standing charge plus a charge per unit used. For CVP every mixed cost must be split. The **high–low (simultaneous equation) method** uses two periods of data:

| Month | Power cost | Chargeable hours | Equation |
|---|---|---|---|
| January | $1,633 | 361 (329 revenue + 32 service) | 1,633 = X + 361Y |
| February | $1,592 | 348 (316 revenue + 32 service) | 1,592 = X + 348Y |
| Subtract | $41 | 13 hours | Y = 41 ÷ 13 ≈ **$3.15 per hour** |
| Substitute | | | X = 1,633 − 3.15 × 361 ≈ **$495 fixed per month** |

<div class="callout trap">
<p class="callout-title">Why the class answer is $4 an hour</p>

The January–February pair alone gives about $3.15 an hour. In class, Prof. Jaiswall averaged **three** pairs (Jan–Feb, Feb–Mar, Jan–Mar), which comes to about **$4 an hour**. Using more pairs reduces the distortion from any one unusual month, and the fixed part then comes out close to zero: almost all of this cost is variable.

</div>

With power at about $4 and operations salaries at $24, Prestige's variable cost is about **$28 per revenue hour**, tiny next to its $800 commercial rate. Each commercial hour contributes **$772** towards fixed costs. Prestige's problem isn't its cost structure. It isn't selling enough hours.

## The contribution income statement

> "I could have just given you the CVP formulas. But once you understand the contribution income statement, the model falls out naturally." Prof. Manju Jaiswall

| | Traditional format | Contribution format |
|---|---|---|
| Start | Sales | Sales |
| First deduction | COGS (a mix of fixed and variable) | **All variable costs** (variable COGS + variable selling) |
| Subtotal | Gross profit | **Contribution margin** |
| Next deduction | Selling and admin (a mix) | **All fixed costs** |
| Bottom line | Net profit | Net profit: **exactly the same number** |
| Separates costs by | Function | Behaviour |
| Can it answer "what if we sell 50 more?" | No | Yes: profit = contribution per unit × units − fixed costs |

The contribution format is the **prerequisite** for CVP: no break-even or target-profit analysis without first splitting fixed from variable. Traditional format is for external reporting; contribution format is for internal decisions.

### Contribution margin, at a chai stall

Contribution margin is the money left from a sale after paying all the variable costs. That leftover goes first towards covering fixed costs, and becomes profit once they are covered. Think of fixed costs as a hole that has to be filled before any profit exists. Every unit you sell throws its contribution into the hole.

| The chai stall | Figure | What it means |
|---|---|---|
| Selling price | Rs.20 a cup | What the customer pays |
| Variable cost | Rs.8 a cup | Milk, tea, sugar, gas: paid per cup |
| Contribution margin | Rs.12 a cup | Goes towards rent, the helper's pay and the loan instalment |
| CS ratio | 60% (12 ÷ 20) | 60 paise of every rupee earned is contribution |
| Monthly fixed costs | Rs.36,000 | The same every month |
| Break-even | 3,000 cups (36,000 ÷ 12) | 100 cups a day in a 30-day month. The hole is exactly full |
| Cup 3,001 onwards | Rs.12 profit each | Fixed costs are already covered |

CVP answers three practical questions before any launch, investment or expansion, plus a fourth for people who think in revenue.

| The question | The formula | At the chai stall |
|---|---|---|
| How many must I sell just to survive? | Fixed costs ÷ contribution per unit | 36,000 ÷ 12 = 3,000 cups |
| How many to hit my profit target? | (Fixed costs + target profit) ÷ contribution per unit | (36,000 + 18,000) ÷ 12 = 4,500 cups |
| How safe am I above break-even? | (Current sales − break-even) ÷ current sales | (4,500 − 3,000) ÷ 4,500 = 33% |
| What revenue do I need to break even? | Fixed costs ÷ CS ratio | 36,000 ÷ 60% = Rs.60,000 of chai |

The structure never changes; only the inputs do. Working in units, divide by contribution per unit. Working in revenue, divide by the CS ratio.

## CVP: building the model from first principles

<Steps items={[
  { title: 'Start', body: 'Revenue − Cost = Profit' },
  { title: 'Expand', body: '(P × Q) − (V × Q) − F = Profit. P price, Q units, V variable cost per unit, F fixed costs.' },
  { title: 'Break-even', body: 'Set profit = 0, so Q = F ÷ (P − V).' },
  { title: 'Target profit', body: 'Q = (F + Target profit) ÷ (P − V)' },
]} />

**Why it works.** P − V is the **contribution per unit**: how fast each unit fills the fixed-cost hole. F is the **size of the hole**. Hole size ÷ fill rate = units needed. Once you see it that way you never need to memorise the formula.

**In sales value:** break-even sales = F ÷ CS ratio, where the **CS (contribution-to-sales) ratio** = contribution per unit ÷ price.

### Worked example: Yule Inc. surfboards

| Item | Value |
|---|---|
| Price (P) | $500 |
| Variable cost (V) | $300 |
| Contribution per board | $200 |
| CS ratio | 200 ÷ 500 = **40%** |
| Fixed costs per month (F) | $80,000 |
| **Break-even** | 80,000 ÷ 200 = **400 boards**, or 80,000 ÷ 40% = **$200,000** of sales |
| Current sales | 500 boards ($250,000) |
| Current profit | 500 × 200 − 80,000 = **$20,000** |
| Boards for $100,000 profit | (80,000 + 100,000) ÷ 200 = **900 boards** |

<Fig caption="Below 400 boards every sale narrows the loss; above it, each board adds $200 of profit.">
  <BreakEven />
</Fig>

<div class="callout">
<p class="callout-title">Manager takeaway: three numbers before any launch</p>

Break-even is the first question before any investment, launch or expansion. You need three numbers: fixed cost, variable cost and price.

- **Product launch:** a health snack with variable cost Rs.1,200, price Rs.2,000. Contribution Rs.800, CS ratio 40%, fixed overhead Rs.4,00,000 a month. Break-even = **500 units** or **Rs.10,00,000** a month. Ask sales: can we commit to 500 units a month by month four? If not, cut fixed overhead or raise the price before launch.
- **New consulting line:** Rs.40,000 variable cost per engagement, Rs.1,00,000 price, Rs.60,000 contribution (CS 60%), Rs.4,80,000 fixed a month. Break-even = **8 engagements** a month. If winning 8 by Q2 is uncertain, start with a smaller fixed team.

Never recommend a launch, expansion or new geography without computing break-even and stress-testing the volume assumption.

</div>

## CVP in action

### Margin of safety

**Margin of safety (MOS) = actual sales − break-even sales.** It is how far sales can fall before you start losing money; a small MOS means you are close to the edge.

Yule: $250,000 − $200,000 = **$50,000** (100 boards).

**Shortcut: Profit = MOS sales × CS ratio.** $50,000 × 40% = $20,000. Past break-even, every dollar of sales adds the CS ratio straight to profit.

**Check yourself: a coaching centre.** It charges Rs.8,000 a student, with a variable cost of Rs.2,000 a student and fixed costs of Rs.1,20,000 a month.

| Question | Working | Answer |
|---|---|---|
| How many students to break even? | Contribution 8,000 − 2,000 = 6,000. 1,20,000 ÷ 6,000 | **20 students** |
| With 30 students enrolled, what is the margin of safety? | (30 − 20) ÷ 30 | **33%**: enrolment can fall by a third before losses begin |

**The same model for a service line.** A managed security service earns about Rs.4,17,000 a month from an average customer (Rs.50 lakh a year) and spends Rs.1,50,000 a month serving each one (analyst hours and customer-specific tools). Contribution is Rs.2,67,000 a customer, a CS ratio of 64%. Fixed costs for the operations centre (rent, platform, certifications, the manager) are Rs.12,00,000 a month. Break-even is 12,00,000 ÷ 2,67,000 = 4.5, so **5 customers**. With 8 customers the monthly profit is 8 × 2,67,000 − 12,00,000 = **Rs.9,36,000**. Every customer above five adds Rs.2,67,000 a month, because the fixed costs are already covered. A chai stall, a software firm and a hospital all follow the same three numbers.

### What-if questions

| Question | Working | Answer |
|---|---|---|
| Spend $10,000 more on advertising to lift sales from 500 to 540 boards? | Extra contribution 40 × $200 = $8,000 vs cost $10,000 | **Reject** (−$2,000). Any spend under $8,000 would add profit. |
| Variable cost rises from $300 to $310. New break-even? | 80,000 ÷ 190 = 421.05 | **422 boards** (from 400). Cost inflation tightens break-even. |
| Next year: 525 boards, $190 contribution, $90,000 fixed. Profit? | 525 × 190 − 90,000 | **$9,750** |

### More than one product

Use a **weighted-average contribution** based on the sales mix: find total break-even units, then split them by the mix.

| Product | Price | Variable cost | Contribution | Units | Mix | Weighted |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| Surfboards | $500 | $300 | $200 | 500 | 62.5% | $125.00 |
| Sailboards | $1,000 | $450 | $550 | 300 | 37.5% | $206.25 |
| **Combined** | | | | 800 | 100% | **$331.25** |

With fixed costs of $170,000: 170,000 ÷ 331.25 = 513.2, so **514 units**: **321 surfboards** and **193 sailboards**.

<div class="callout trap">
<p class="callout-title">Always round break-even up</p>

At 513 units contribution is 513 × $331.25 = $169,931, still $69 short. Break-even means **fully** covered, so round up, never to the nearest whole number.

</div>

## Prestige Telephone: the shutdown decision

Prestige Telephone is a regulated utility in 1999 whose prices the government controls. President **Daniel Rowe** noticed that the mainframe running telephone operations sat idle much of the day, so he set up **Prestige Data Services** to sell those hours: **$800/hour** to outside businesses, **$400/hour** to the parent. Three years on, it has never had a profitable month. It is April 2003. Should Rowe shut it down?

| Hours | January | February | March | What to notice |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| Intracompany | 206 | 181 | 223 | Recovering, strong in March |
| Commercial | 123 | 135 | 138 | Rising slowly |
| Service / maintenance | 32 | 32 | 40 | Unavoidable upkeep |
| **Idle** | **223** | **164** | **143** | **Falling fast: the key positive signal** |

### Q1. Close it? Avoidable vs unavoidable costs

The P&L shows a loss and instinct says close. That answers the wrong question. The right one: **is Rowe better off with the subsidiary open or closed?**

| Cost | Monthly | Goes away if closed? | Why |
|---|---|---|---|
| Computer lease | $95,000 | No | Non-cancellable, four years left |
| Rent + custodial | $9,240 | No | Paid to the parent; the money stays in the group |
| Depreciation | $26,180 | No | Non-cash; the equipment is already paid for |
| Corporate services (allocated parent staff) | ~$15,300 | Largely no | Parent staff stay employed anyway |
| Subsidiary staff salaries | ~$62,000 | **Yes** | Hired for this business; they leave if it closes |
| Maintenance, power, materials | ~$17,000 | **Yes** | Only used when the machine runs |
| Sales and marketing | ~$7,700 | **Yes** | Discretionary; stops on closure |

<div class="callout">
<p class="callout-title">Verdict: do not shut down</p>

Closing saves about **$87,000** a month but gives up about **$200,000** of revenue, while the lease, rent and depreciation (about **$130,000**) keep running either way. Rowe would be significantly **worse off** closing.

</div>

<div class="callout trap">
<p class="callout-title">The P&L loss is the wrong number</p>

March's accounting loss of **$21,438** is real, but it answers "how much did the subsidiary make?" Rowe's question is "am I better off with it or without it?" Only **relevant-cost analysis** answers that, and here the two give **opposite** answers. Never close a unit on a P&L loss alone.

</div>

### Q2. How many commercial hours to break even?

| Step | Amount |
|---|---|
| Total monthly fixed costs (Q1 2003 average) | ~$213,000 |
| Covered by intracompany hours (205 × $372 contribution) | ~$76,260 |
| Gap for commercial hours to fill | ~$136,740 |
| Contribution per commercial hour ($800 − $28) | $772 |
| **Break-even commercial hours** (136,740 ÷ 772) | **~177 a month** |
| Actual commercial hours (Q1 average) | ~132 a month |
| **Gap** | **~45 hours a month** |

This is not a cost problem. It is a **sales** problem with a specific, achievable target.

### Q4. Six fixes to the management report

| # | Change | Why |
|---|---|---|
| 1 | Rebuild as a **contribution** P&L | Shows strong contribution and that the loss comes from unavoidable fixed costs |
| 2 | Label costs **avoidable vs unavoidable** | The $95,000 lease and the $7,700 of marketing look alike on paper; they aren't |
| 3 | Split revenue by **customer type** | $800 commercial and $400 intracompany hours are very different contributors |
| 4 | Put **idle hours** at the top | 223 idle hours in January = $178,400 of unsold capacity at the commercial rate |
| 5 | Flag **internal transfers** | The $9,240 rent goes to the parent; no cash leaves the group |
| 6 | Add a **break-even tracker** | "Break-even commercial hours 177, actual 132, gap 45": a target, not just a loss |

<div class="callout">
<p class="callout-title">Manager takeaway: evaluating a loss-making unit</p>

Close a unit only when the **costs you avoid** exceed the **contribution you lose**. Four words: relevant costs only.

- **Specialty chemicals division:** revenue Rs.60 lakh a year; avoidable costs Rs.50 lakh (dedicated staff 30, materials 15, marketing 5); unavoidable Rs.55 lakh (allocated lease 40, shared R&D 15). Its P&L shows a Rs.45 lakh loss. Close it and you lose Rs.60 lakh of revenue to save Rs.50 lakh: **Rs.10 lakh worse. Keep it open.**
- **IT managed-services practice:** revenue Rs.45 lakh; avoidable costs Rs.48 lakh (team 40, tools 8); unavoidable Rs.35 lakh. The practice doesn't even cover its own avoidable costs. Closing saves Rs.48 lakh and gives up Rs.45 lakh: **Rs.3 lakh better. Close it.** The Rs.35 lakh of shared overhead stays either way and has to be absorbed elsewhere.

The decision is never about the P&L loss; it is about the cash impact of closing.

</div>

<div class="callout">
<p class="callout-title">Manager takeaway: make every report point to an action</p>

- **Plant report:** revenue Rs.3.8 crore, cost Rs.4 crore, loss Rs.20 lakh. As a contribution P&L: contribution Rs.1.2 crore, fixed overhead Rs.1.4 crore. Contribution is healthy; the fixed base is the problem. Put machine utilisation at the top (62% vs a 78% target) and the conversation shifts from "we made a loss" to "lift utilisation by 16 points".
- **SaaS product line:** revenue Rs.1 crore a year, variable costs Rs.15 lakh, fixed engineering Rs.1.15 crore, loss Rs.30 lakh. Contribution is Rs.85 lakh, an 85% margin: excellent unit economics. Break-even revenue = 1.15 crore ÷ 85% ≈ Rs.1.35 crore a year, or about **Rs.11.3 lakh of monthly recurring revenue against Rs.8.3 lakh today**. The team needs about Rs.3 lakh more MRR: an operational target.

**Rule:** every management report should state the gap to break-even as a specific operational action.

</div>
