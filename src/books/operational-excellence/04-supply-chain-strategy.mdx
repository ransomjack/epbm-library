## From cost to resilience

For thirty years supply chains ran on one commandment: make it cheaper. Then the shocks arrived and refused to leave (the pandemic, chip shortages, wars and sanctions, and now the build-out for AI), and boardrooms turned the conversation from cost to resilience. Prof. Mehta's framing: these aren't quarterly stories but decade-long redesigns, and the battery, semiconductor and energy-security supply chains will keep them on the front page for the rest of our careers.

## Two levels of operations decisions

Think of a kirana shop owner. Deciding whether to open a second shop near the bus stand, or to buy a delivery scooter, is **operations strategy**: designing the system. Deciding how much atta to order this week is **operations management**: running the system well. Sessions 2 and 3 were about running it. This session is about designing it.

| Operations strategy (OS) | Operations management (OM) |
|---|---|
| Designs the system: "doing smart things" | Runs the system: "doing things smartly" |
| How much capacity, what kind, when, where | Forecasting, inventory, scheduling, process flow |
| Rare, board-level, very expensive | Continuous, managerial |
| Hard to reverse | Correctable within months |

Live strategy questions right now: how many data centres should a cloud giant build; how much chip capacity should a chip designer commit to; how many million square feet of fulfilment centres, and how many robots, for an online retailer. Capacity means different things in different industries (square feet, machines, people), but the sizing, kind, timing and location of resources is always the core. The map from Session 1 returns: Value = Capabilities × (Assets + Processes). Every box hides a serious problem, and every story in this session is one of those boxes going right or wrong.

One more pillar before the cases: **process innovation beats product innovation for moats.** Dell's build-to-order engine, Zara's speed machine and Toyota's production system created moats rivals couldn't clone. Meanwhile even the mighty get questioned on products: Apple has faced "end of the innovation pipeline" headlines for years, still can't meaningfully leave China, and its closed, product-first ecosystem is one reason its AI effort lagged the open, collaborative world of large language models.

The rule from Session 1 still governs: operations takes its mandate from the business strategy. If the basis of competition is fuzzy, every decision below it wobbles.

## Intel: too little outsourcing

Imagine a shopkeeper who insists on growing his own wheat, milling his own flour and printing his own packets, while the shop next door simply buys the best flour on the market and sells more of it. For two decades Intel was the first shopkeeper. This is the story of how the firm that defined the PC era became a takeover rumour: repeated changes of chief executive in a few years, acquisition talk, and the US government taking a stake of about 10% to steady it.

The chip industry creates value in three stages:

<Flow items={[
  { t: 'Design', s: 'Chip architecture and intellectual property. Nvidia, AMD, Qualcomm, Apple.' },
  { t: 'Fabrication', s: 'Printing chips at a few nanometres. TSMC, using ASML’s machines.', hl: true },
  { t: 'Assembly and test', s: 'Packaging and testing. Cost-driven, largely in Asia.' },

The industry **deliberately split itself up**. No single firm could fund every layer at the pace of innovation, so specialists emerged at each stage and fed one another: TSMC's fabrication turned Nvidia's designs into the hardware that trains modern AI models; ASML's machines (it is the only maker of them) made TSMC possible. Outsourcing here was never a cost trick. It was the industry's engine of innovation.

Intel kept design, fabrication and assembly all in-house: a control-first strategy in an industry where the next source of value had moved outside any one firm's walls. By the time it admitted the miss, it sat on large amounts of the wrong kind of capacity, under-used, while demand stampeded towards AI-grade chips it couldn't supply. That is what explains the churn at the top. Capacity decisions are strategy decisions, and strategy mistakes can't be fixed in a quarter.

**Why the recent bounce isn't redemption.** The weight of the AI world has been shifting from training frontier models, which needs the most advanced chips, to inference, the everyday serving of millions of users, which runs happily on routine chips. That lifts Intel and its peers alike; add government support, and the share price revived. Nothing structural has been solved. The professor was explicit: no clean chit.

**The irony file.** One of Intel's most celebrated chief executives wrote *Only the Paranoid Survive*, the management classic about giants who stop seeing innovation outside their walls. Intel preached the innovator's dilemma and then performed it. A study the class was shown makes it general, "the curse of the top dog": the most valuable company in the index massively outperforms before reaching the top and underperforms after. Contrast Google, which saw its dependence on one chip supplier, refused to accept it and built its own chips. Dell is the softer parallel: a mindset that never diversified beyond PCs and had to go private for years.

<div class="callout trap">
<p class="callout-title">"So vertical integration is bad." No.</p>

Vertical integration buys **control**. Intel's industry needed distributed innovation, not control. Zara's industry needs speed, and owning most of its chain is exactly how Zara delivers speed. Same tool, opposite verdicts. The context decides.

</div>

Keep one question on a loop: **where is the next source of value creation, and is it inside or outside my boundary?** Core stays in; non-core goes out; and what counts as core shifts over time.

## Boeing: too much outsourcing

Some corporate crises are natural disasters. Boeing's, in the professor's words, are completely man-made, which is why the "wisdom in hindsight" mercy extended to Intel was withdrawn here. A century-old engineering icon is now a standing chapter in supply chain courses on how *not* to design one, a chapter it earned years before any crash. The visible wreckage: two crashes of one model within months, rooted in quality, not pilot error; a door panel that blew out mid-air in January 2024 because four bolts were never replaced; three chief executives in three years.

The 787 Dreamliner was, to be fair, a real leap in fuel efficiency. For it Boeing changed its whole supply chain. Prof. Mehta has taught this as a cautionary case since 2013.

| | Earlier aircraft (737) | 787 Dreamliner |
|---|---|---|
| Share outsourced | 35–50% | About 70% |
| Suppliers | Thousands, managed directly | About 50 "strategic partners"; little visibility beyond them |
| Supplier's role | Built parts to Boeing's designs | Designed and built entire sections, and owned the know-how |
| Contracts | Fixed price, with delay penalties | Risk-sharing: paid only when Boeing delivered the aircraft |
| Final assembly | About 30 days | A planned 3-day snap-together of finished sections |

**Why the clever contract backfired.** On paper, "nobody is paid until the aircraft is delivered" should make everyone hurry. In exchange, suppliers kept the know-how and good margins. Then 2008 arrived, some suppliers defaulted, and every survivor did the rational sum: why deliver early and tie up cash waiting for the slowest partner? A contract designed as a race became a slow race. The 787 arrived about five years late, and soon after entering service the whole fleet was grounded over quality problems. The professor's fairness note: this failure deserves some hindsight mercy. The intent was good; the incentives were never stress-tested.

**The deeper problem.** Aircraft manufacturing competes on quality the way chip-making competes on innovation. Yet the Dreamliner chain was approved by a finance-first board on one condition: that it be a tightly cost-controlled model. A cost culture in a quality industry. For two decades Boeing also sliced itself into silos, with separate divisions and even separate headquarters for research, engineering and manufacturing, destroying the closeness between design and making that an aircraft demands. In the words of the class reading, the culture drifted from ingenuity and quality to shareholder returns.

**Where it stands.** The industry term is "travelled work": jobs done out of sequence and unfinished aircraft pushed down the line to meet quotas. An estimated 30 to 40% of the assembly line's effort is rework; the regulator caps monthly output while quality systems are rebuilt; and the new chief executive found whole production lines repairing recently assembled jets. ("It seems like 30 percent of everybody's job is fixing something.") The costliest single mistake, spinning off its own fuselage maker to cut costs, has been reversed by buying it back at enormous expense. Corrections to operations strategy are the most expensive corrections in business.

| | Intel | Boeing |
|---|---|---|
| What the industry competes on | Innovation | Quality |
| The strategy error | Too much vertical integration | Uncontrolled outsourcing |
| What was lost | The outside innovation ecosystem | Control of process and quality |
| The symptom | Idle capacity of the wrong kind | Rework, delays, groundings |
| The scoreboard | Three chief executives in about three years | Three chief executives in about three years |
| The root cause | Misread where value creation lived | Misread what the product demands |

<div class="callout">
<p class="callout-title">Opposite errors, the same root</p>

Outsourcing didn't sink Boeing. Outsourcing the one thing its competitiveness depends on, control of quality, did. **Don't grade the level of outsourcing. Grade the fit between what is outsourced and what the firm competes on.**

</div>

### Four class debates

The session was unusually interactive, and four exchanges each ended in a principle worth keeping.

- **"But outsourcing risks leaking know-how."** True, and sometimes you do it anyway, with open eyes. Tesla built a huge plant in Shanghai knowing it would hand China's ecosystem its playbook, because without China's scale and cost it would never have escaped its overpromise-and-underdeliver phase. The same soil grew a rival that has since overtaken it in electric-vehicle sales. Not an accident: a calculated trade-off. The discipline is calibrated outsourcing: decide what is core, which never leaves the building, and what isn't.
- **Outsourcing as an innovation engine.** The printed circuit board, the heart of every electronic device, was pioneered not by the famous equipment makers but by a contract manufacturer: only a firm pooling everyone's volumes had the scale to fund it. That is the TSMC story two decades later. Even "sleeping with the enemy" becomes rational: Samsung is at once Apple's fiercest rival and one of its most important component suppliers.
- **Being smartly late.** Several carmakers rushed to build electric-vehicle plants and then saw demand soften. Toyota stayed with hybrids, was mocked for years, and has so far been proved right. The professor's nuance: soft demand is demand risk, not dead capacity. The plants haven't vanished, and policy can reignite demand. Waiting can be an operations strategy.
- **"How do I analyse my own company?"** The professor's answer, given twice with feeling: there is no standard script. Take the first principles (the strategy boxes, the capacity questions, the flexibility logic) and do the heavy lifting on your own context. The examples are disposable; the template is what you take away.

> We are not discussing Zara here. Zara is not important; the process is important. You are here at IIM Calcutta to learn to ask questions, not to solve questions.

## Why DMart really wins

Everyone "knows" why DMart wins: it pays suppliers quickly, it is cash-rich, it owns its stores. Prof. Mehta's reply: you're telling me the patient has a fever; I asked what the infection is.

<p class="callout-title">Inventory follows variability, not volume</p>

"High demand needs high inventory" is false when demand is predictable. Safety stock grows with how **noisy** demand is and with the service level you promise. A large, steady seller needs almost no buffer. A small, erratic one can need a lot.

</div>

Run the same chain for any low-cost player. The class sketched a value-fashion chain (limited styles, fast turnover) and IndiGo (one fleet type, so standard maintenance, spares and training). The habit of asking "why" until symptoms give way to the cause is the skill.

## The product–process matrix

One picture organises everything above. Put product variety on one axis and process flexibility on the other. Well-run firms sit on the diagonal: the zone of alignment.

<Fig title="Variety must be matched by flexibility" caption="After Hayes and Wheelwright. Every step towards more variety has to be paid for with a step towards more flexibility.">
  <Quad cols={['Low variety', 'High variety']} rows={['Flexible process', 'Rigid process']} cells={[
    { tag: 'Off the diagonal', t: 'Paying for flexibility you don’t use', b: 'A flexible, costly process serving a narrow range. Pure waste.', tone: 'bad' },
    { tag: 'On the diagonal', t: 'Variety, honestly paid for', b: 'Multi-speciality hospitals; large online marketplaces.', tone: 'best' },
    { tag: 'On the diagonal', t: 'Focused and low-cost', b: 'Ford’s Model T; DMart; IndiGo; a specialist heart hospital.', tone: 'best' },
    { tag: 'Off the diagonal', t: 'Promises the process can’t keep', b: 'High variety on a rigid process: delays, stock-outs, lost sales.', tone: 'bad' },
  ]} />
</Fig>

Variety is complexity: more items mean harder forecasts, more suppliers, more skills and more stock. That complexity has to be absorbed by flexibility, and flexibility costs money. The matrix is really a billing counter: every step towards variety must be paid for with a step towards flexibility. Fall below the diagonal and you are writing cheques your process can't cash. Float above it and you are paying for flexibility you never use.

- **Ford's Model T** moved from craft customisation to one standardised car and invented low-cost scale.

- **A focused heart hospital** applies Henry Ford's idea to surgery: one kind of work, smooth flow, high volume, low cost. As its founder put it, healthcare needs process innovation, not product innovation.
- **A multi-speciality hospital** has unpredictable flows (the X-ray room is swamped today, the scanner tomorrow) and must buy flexibility: cross-trained staff, multi-purpose equipment, spare capacity.
- **Large online marketplaces** absorb extreme variety through depth of stock and the design of their network.
- **Dell** stayed on the diagonal cleverly: it mass-produced the standard 70–80% of every machine and let the customer's choices apply only to the final slice. The class vote on where Dell sits scattered across the matrix until the professor closed it: calling Dell rigid does a disservice to its variety; calling it fully flexible does a disservice to its cost. It never leaves the diagonal.

Variety is a promise of flexibility. Either budget honestly for that flexibility, or curate the variety down.

## Flexibility

Two plants can each make 100 units. Demand for product A turns out to be 110 and for product B, 90. With dedicated plants you sell 100 + 90 = 190: ten customers of A walk away while B's plant has ten units idle. Let each plant make both and you sell all 200. Try both designs.

**Flexibility is the tool for absorbing shocks**: in demand, supply, processing time or lead time. What it looks like depends on the business: well-placed stock in retail, several routes in logistics, multi-product lines in a factory, cross-skilled people in services.

## Risk pooling

A company has a North and a South warehouse. For one product, the noise in each market's demand (its standard deviation) is 5, and company policy is to hold stock at ten times the noise: 50 in each warehouse, 100 in all. What happens if it merges the two into one warehouse? The class's instinct said less than 100, correctly. The reason is the most useful statistics in the module.

A 60-second refresher. Variance is the average of squared deviations from the mean. We square so that overshoots and undershoots don't cancel (both are bad) and to punish large misses more. The square root of variance is the standard deviation, the noise. In finance the same quantity wears a suit and is called volatility.

**Pooled SD = √(SD₁² + SD₂² + 2 × r × SD₁ × SD₂)**

where SD is the standard deviation of demand (the "noise") and r is the correlation between the two markets, from −1 to +1.

<Fig title="Pooling two warehouses" caption="Try r = −1, 0 and +1. Pooled noise can never exceed the two added together, so pooling can only help or break even.">
  <PoolCalc />
</Fig>

| Correlation (noise of 3 and 4; 7 if kept separate) | Pooled noise | Meaning |
|---|---|---|
| r = −1: one rises when the other falls | 1 | A huge saving: the perfect hedge |
| r = 0: independent markets | 5 | A solid saving |
| r = +1: they move together | 7 | No saving, but no worse |

This is the argument for a diversified investment portfolio: when you diversify you are, in the professor's phrase, knocking at the door of negative correlation. And the comfort: even a hopeless fund manager who picks perfectly correlated stocks leaves you no worse off than holding them separately.

<div class="callout trap">
<p class="callout-title">Pooling is not a free lunch</p>

If pooling is so good, why doesn't an online retailer run one giant national warehouse? Because **pooling trades inventory for lead time**: centralise and you hold less stock, but you move away from the customer. Where speed is the promise, that is fatal. Always state the saving and the trade-off together.

</div>

<div class="callout">
<p class="callout-title">Manager takeaway: three questions for any supply chain decision</p>

1. What do we compete on, and does this decision serve it?
2. Is what we are keeping or handing over core to that?
3. What variability are we facing, and where will we absorb it: in stock, in spare capacity, or in the customer's waiting time?

</div>

## The session in one breath

Strategy designs the system; management runs it. Intel gave the outside world too little weight; Boeing gave it too much. Opposite errors, the same root: an operations strategy out of line with the basis of competition. DMart's cause is its curated range, not fast payment. Variety must be paid for with flexibility. Pooling cuts noise and costs lead time.
