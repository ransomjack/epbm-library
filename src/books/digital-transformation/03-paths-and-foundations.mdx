## Two ways back to value

A healthy firm wants its surplus to grow every year: **incremental value creation**. Most transformation programmes are written for that.

Viacom had the opposite: **value depletion**. Revenue down about 10%, profit down about 40%. That needs a different brief.

<Cards min={240} items={[
  { title: 'Value re-creation', body: 'Get back to where you were. Repair what is leaking before trying to grow.', foot: 'For a business whose value is shrinking' },
  { title: 'Value co-creation', body: 'Build new value together with other firms, so that everyone ends up better off.', foot: 'For a business ready to grow' },
]} />

Confuse the two and you fund a growth programme for a company that needs a repair programme.

### What business transformation is not

"We have launched fourteen applications in six years, so we are on a digital transformation journey." No. Fourteen applications mean you can now capture transactions. That is a precondition, not the thing itself.

The test: **going from point A to point B with a positive outcome.** Moving from an in-house ERP to a commercial one fails it: the same transactions, approvals and invoices as before.

## The need tree

Every transformation starts from a need, and needs come from two directions.

<Cards min={260} items={[
  { title: 'Outward-facing: an opportunity', body: 'A new market, a new product, or a competitor showing where the money is.', foot: 'Differentiator · chases the top line' },
  { title: 'Inward-facing: a shortcoming', body: 'Revenue shrinking, margins squeezed, customers leaving, processes wasteful.', foot: 'Cost leader · protects the bottom line' },
]} />

Viacom's need was inward-facing: not an opportunity spotted but numbers that could no longer be ignored. (This is a rule of thumb. Many programmes carry both.)

**Differentiation comes in two kinds.** A carmaker can add safety features until the car earns a five-star rating at the same price: *product innovation*. Or it can leave the car alone and add lifetime roadside assistance and a buy-back guarantee: *customer closeness*. Ten-minute grocery delivery is customer closeness: the biscuits haven't changed, only how they reach you.

<div class="callout">
<p class="callout-title">Ask who, not just what</p>

If the chief executive sensed the need, leadership champions the programme from day one. If it came up from frustrated operations teams, the leader may never be fully committed. Same technology, very different odds.

</div>

## Viacom's long walk to the individual viewer

How did a broadcaster ever know which programmes worked?

<Flow items={[

  { t: 'Streaming apps', s: 'Every tap and pause is recorded. Unit: one person.', hl: true },

Each step improved how well the findings generalised. Only the last made personalisation possible, and that is when the data problem exploded: Viacom was capturing far more than it could use, and decisions stalled.

### Dashboards look backwards

A dashboard always shows what has already happened. Call the present T and the delay K: you are looking at T minus K. K can be squeezed. It can't reach zero.

Viacom's move was to extend its reporting from measures of what happened to predictions of what will. Self-service reporting was re-creation. Prediction opened the door to co-creation.

## Value co-creation

Co-creation isn't teams collaborating inside one firm. It happens when **separate firms, each with its own value chain, all end up better off.**

| Partner | What Viacom shares | Why they value it |
|---|---|---|
| Advertising agencies | What this slot, at this hour, in this region should cost | Viacom predicts better than they can; deals close faster |
| Content creators | What is watched, when and by whom | They commission on evidence rather than instinct |
| Regulators | An early view of which titles are likely to clear review | Fewer surprises on both sides |
| Competitors | Aggregate patterns of what is popular in a region | It powers the "trending" half of everyone's recommendations |

Sharing with rivals has a name: **co-opetition**, co-operating in one defined area where everyone gains while competing everywhere else. Shared payment networks are the standard example. It is not lobbying, where rivals jointly pressure a third party.

## The means: five ingredients

If digital transformation is the finished dish, these are the ingredients. The recipe varies by firm; the list doesn't.

### Organisational capabilities

- **Culture** is the broadest and the stickiest. It changes over decades, not quarters.
- **Structure**: flat and flexible, where you can try an idea and be wrong, or hierarchical and rule-bound, where you can't.
- **Decision making**: how long a decision takes to climb the hierarchy, and what kind of deciding is habitual.

<div class="callout">
<p class="callout-title">A predictor of resistance</p>

If your organisation already decides from numbers, people will accept a model's output: it is the same habit. If decisions have always rested on senior judgement, expect a real fight over letting a model decide.

</div>

**Leadership and culture shape each other.** A leader can raise or flatten a culture, and a culture quietly limits what any leader can do. In a diagnosis, don't try to separate them. Ask which is the binding constraint right now.

### Workforce: nothing in it for the user

A retail group moving from one ERP to another found staff resisting. Run the acceptance test honestly. Is the new system useful? Yes, and so was the old one. Easier? Not for people comfortable with the old one. And for the individual: the same transactions, the same approvals, the same invoices.

The organisation gains scale and a supported platform. The user gains nothing and loses a familiar tool. Perceived usefulness, for the individual, is close to zero. That explains the resistance.

<p class="callout-title">The most expensive mix-up</p>

**Ability gaps are fixed with training. Attitude gaps are fixed with change management and something in it for the user.** Spending training money on an attitude problem is the commonest waste in this field.

</div>

## Grassroots, big bang, or both

Two real companies got their data architecture wrong in opposite directions.

- **The big bang.** A large bank ran one enormous programme to pull all its data into a central lake, without tying it to any business use. Three years on, very little value.
- **The grassroots.** Every team built its own pipeline for its own need. Nothing was reusable, and the tangle became expensive to maintain.

Prof. Kumar's kitchen version: the big bang cooks every dish on the menu before anyone orders. The grassroots kiosk chops onions from scratch for each order. A good restaurant makes **one base gravy** in the morning and turns out many dishes from it.

### Data products

A **data product** is a clean, standardised, well-documented data set built for a function and kept ready for use, to the standard of a good public data set. The layer unlocks four things:

- **Self-service without a business case.** A manager can try an analysis without first justifying the cost of preparing data.
- **Uses nobody has thought of yet.**
- **Cheaper computing**, because heavy processing happens here and not inside the reporting tool.
- **Real collaboration.** What departments share is data products, and a model built on several is stronger than any one department could build.

Is data an asset or a product? A product, because it feeds the value-creating activities directly.

## Data governance

Build that layer and questions follow. What rules apply? How is each field defined? Who checks quality? Who may see what, and who may share it? Answer them and you have **data governance**.

<div class="callout trap">
<p class="callout-title">Governance is not data quality</p>

Ask most firms about governance and they talk about quality. Quality is one part. Governance also covers ownership, definitions, stewardship, classification, access and accountability. India's Digital Personal Data Protection Act is forcing the conversation, as GDPR did in Europe.

</div>

| Term | What it covers |
|---|---|
| **Data governance** | Ownership, definitions, quality, access and sharing |
| **Data security** | Protecting the data itself |
| **Cyber security** | Protecting systems and applications |

Ungoverned data moving between departments leads to unapproved tools appearing across the business. Governance isn't paperwork laid over the architecture. It is what makes one architecture safer than another.
