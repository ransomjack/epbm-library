## Value depletion, and the two ways back

Part 2 closed the question of how you prove a transformation worked. This part closes the two remaining halves of the framework: the ways and the means.

Start with Viacom. Revenue falling year on year, about 10%, and the bottom line falling far faster, roughly 40%. A shrinking top line says market share is going. A bottom line falling four times faster says the money spent to hold customers isn't converting, so every retention effort has become pure operating cost.

Put that against what a healthy firm is normally trying to do. The ordinary ambition is to magnify value every year: more applications, more training, a tighter production line, and the surplus grows. That is **incremental value creation**, and it is what most transformation programmes are written for. Viacom had the opposite: **value depletion**.

Both are "ways". Which one you take is decided by the need, not by the technology available. Confuse the two and you will fund a growth programme for a company that needs a repair programme, which is roughly what happened at General Electric.

## Business transformation, and what it is not

The class had been using one term for three different things.

| Term | What it actually is | The everyday tell |
|---|---|---|
| IT-enabled organisation transformation | You roll out a couple of applications. Onboarding gets easier, some time is saved | The work is the same work, done faster |
| Business transformation | The business drivers themselves change: new markets, new segments, a different way of operating | You are somewhere you weren't before |
| Digital transformation | A business transformation where digital technology is what makes the new position possible | Remove the data and the new model collapses |

The test the professor kept returning to: **going from point A to point B with a positive outcome.** Migrating from an in-house ERP to a commercial one doesn't pass it. The transactions are the same, the approvals are the same, the invoices and audit trails are the same. Useful, expensive, necessary, but not business transformation.

"We have launched fourteen applications in six years, we are on a digital transformation journey." No. Fourteen applications means your ecosystem can now capture transactions. That is a precondition for everything else. It isn't the thing itself.

## The need tree

Every transformation starts from a need, and needs come from exactly two directions.

<Cards min={260} items={[
  { title: 'Outward-facing: you sense an opportunity', body: 'A new market (the same product for a new segment), a new product (a digital stylus reworked for sophisticated digital art), or a competitor signal: you see where a rival is earning and move onto that ground.', foot: 'Differentiator · top line' },
  { title: 'Inward-facing: you identify a shortcoming', body: 'The top line is going, margins are shrinking, the customer base is eroding, processes have gone wasteful.', foot: 'Cost leader · bottom line' },
]} />

Following a competitor makes you a follower, not a leader, but it is a real and common trigger. Viacom sits on the inward branch: not an opportunity spotted, but numbers that had become unmanageable. This is a rule of thumb. Plenty of programmes carry both branches at once.

To describe any need: name the direction, then the value discipline, then the line it targets.

## Two flavours of differentiation

**Product-based innovation.** Take an Indian car maker. A well-known Japanese brand already owns the quality association, so you need a distinction of your own. You add sensors, your safety features go to a gold standard, and your car becomes a five-star vehicle at the same price. The uniqueness is in the product.

**Customer closeness.** Same car, same production line. What changes is the maintenance contract, roadside assistance free for life, perhaps a buy-back agreement. You pay a premium because you won't have to worry about the car for as long as you own it. The uniqueness is in the relationship, not the metal.

A participant asked whether the move from the corner shop to supermarkets to ten-minute delivery counts as customer closeness. It does. The biscuits and the soap haven't changed. What changed is how they reach you: no travel, no waiting, discounts, loyalty.

## Who is driving it changes how it runs

If the need was sensed by the chief executive or the board, leadership champions the transformation from the start, and the way it gets steered and executed is fundamentally different.

If the need arrived from the bottom up, with operations or business development complaining after bad results and proposing a fix, the leader may never have full commitment. Same framework, same technology, a completely different implementation.

## Viacom's long walk to the individual viewer

To understand what Viacom was trying to do, the professor took the class back several decades: to black-and-white television sets, Sunday morning programmes and the mythology serials in the nine-to-ten slot. How did anybody know those slots worked?

Not by gut feel. An agency was hired to understand viewership, and it ran a sample survey.

<Flow items={[
  { t: 'Sample survey', s: 'A hired agency visits a representative set of homes. Unit: a sample.' },
  { t: 'Metering devices', s: 'Boxes in homes record what was watched, and when. Unit: a household.' },
  { t: 'Streaming and apps', s: 'Every tap, pause and re-watch is a data point. Unit: an individual.', hl: true },
]} />

**The first ceiling: generalisability.** An analysis of three thousand viewers can't be confirmed for three hundred thousand. And in the pursuit of generalising, personalisation was given up entirely.

**The second ceiling: the household.** So they invested in metering devices. Better, except that families were larger then. Grandparents, parents and children in front of one set. The finest unit available was still the family.

Then streaming arrived, with individual devices and logins. The unit became one person. And that is exactly when their appetite for data grew, their questions multiplied, and, in the professor's phrase, their decision-making got paralysed. They were capturing far more than they could use.

Notice where the pain moved. The early problem was capture and storage, which is digitisation. Once metering did the capturing, the money moved into building data pipelines. They had transactions and data points. What they were short of was insight: analysis that was not comprehensive, or not accurate, or too late.

## Dashboards look backwards

A dashboard culture is retrospective analysis. Not sometimes. Always.

Open a dashboard at four in the afternoon. The last update might be two o'clock, or yesterday's close. Even with sensors, at 4:18 you are reading data from 4:05. Write it as **T minus K**, where T is now and K is your delay. K can be squeezed. It can't be made zero.

The whole Viacom case was about business intelligence. What they then did was extend it, from measures of what happened into predictions of what is going to happen. Self-service reporting removed the dependency on analysts: a re-creation move. Moving from backward-looking measures to forward-looking insight opened the door to co-creation.

## Five value chains, one value system

What is value co-creation? Several people answered "working together with internal teams towards a common goal". That is collaboration, and a fine thing, but it isn't co-creation, because a value chain is drawn from the point of view of a single firm.

**Co-creation happens when separate firms, each with their own value chain, all end up better off.** Viacom does the heavy lifting on prediction, and four other kinds of firm get value out of it.

| Stakeholder | What Viacom relays | Why they take it |
|---|---|---|
| Advertising agencies | This advertisement, at this hour, in this location, should carry this price | Viacom predicts better than they can, and both sides negotiate faster |
| Content creators | What is being watched, at what time, by whom | Producers start commissioning against insight, not instinct |
| Regulators | An early read on which titles are likely to clear screening | Fewer surprises; Viacom avoids contracts that won't pass |
| Competitors | Aggregate patterns of what is popular across a region | It makes the generic half of a recommendation engine work |

**Why your feed has two personalities.** Log into a streaming service from your tablet and it shows mythology and cartoons. From your phone, food videos. That is the personalised half. But the first item may still be cricket highlights everybody in your region is watching, which you don't follow. That is the generic half, and it runs on shared, aggregate patterns.

Swap the industry for e-commerce and the shape holds: platform, technology vendors, sellers, delivery partners, customers.

## Co-opetition, and what it is not

Sharing pattern data with competitors has a name. **Co-opetition** blends co-operation and competition: rivals collaborating in a defined space where everybody gains, while competing everywhere else. Common payment systems are the standard example. It is a game-theory idea: one player defects and everybody is worse off.

## The means: five ingredients

Ways are done. Now the means: what the transformation runs on. If digital transformation is the finished dish, these are the ingredients, and the question is how much salt, sugar and spice each situation needs. The recipe changes by firm; the ingredient list doesn't.

### Organisational capabilities

**Culture** is the broadest and absorbs most other answers. Innovativeness is a cultural question: are you allowed to be wrong? Culture is extremely sticky. It changes over decades, not quarters.

**Structure.** Flat versus hierarchical, or organic versus mechanistic. In an organic set-up you can walk in with a new idea and run with it, with no justification of returns demanded for a trial. In a mechanistic one you can't.

**Decision making.** First, how long a decision takes: a model produces an output, it escalates to a senior manager, then a vice president, and three days are gone. Second, and more important, what kind of decision-making is ingrained.

<div class="callout">
<p class="callout-title">Objective versus discretionary, and why it predicts resistance</p>

If your organisation habitually decides from numbers, people will accept outputs from models: it is the same habit. If your history is subjective and expert-based, expect real resistance to letting a model decide. This is why existing decision intelligence is a head start: it tells you how fast you can move before you start.

</div>

### Leadership and culture: which shapes which?

The professor put this to the room and admitted he can't answer it either. Most said leadership shapes culture. Both is right, and he made the point with two stories.

**The instruction that worked because of how it was said.** Mid-session he needed the recording paused, and gave the instruction in Bengali. It was obeyed at once. His reading: that had nothing to do with the authority of the chair. What worked was a cultural trigger, a shared language.

**The meeting that lasted two minutes.** He chairs a committee that sits opposite the administration on hostel and sports facilities. Ahead of a sports meet he raised that facilities weren't improving, and a meeting was convened. His single ask wasn't equipment or budget. It was that people reply to his emails, with start and end dates. The meeting took two minutes. The point: this has persisted across directors and administrators, all capable. It is a sixty-year-old legacy in which email simply doesn't get answered; a leader calling people into a room does. Here the culture is shaping how leaders behave.

The relationship is two-way. In a diagnosis don't try to separate them. Ask which is currently the binding constraint on the behaviour you want to change.

### Workforce: nothing in it for the user

A participant from a large retail group described migrating from one ERP to another, and answered "don't ask" when asked how the workforce was finding it.

Run the acceptance logic honestly. Is the new system useful? Yes. Was the old one? Also yes. Is the new one easy to use? Possibly, but their comfort sits with the old system.

Now ask the question almost nobody asks: **what is in it for the individual?** Same transactions, same approvals, same invoices, same audit trails. The organisation gets scale and a supported platform. The user gets nothing measurable and loses what they were comfortable with. Perceived usefulness, at the individual level, is close to zero. That is the whole explanation for the inertia.

With an ERP migration, people usually have the ability; the problem is attitude. With a model it flips: you get an output and have no idea how it was produced, so the issue is capability. And underneath may sit a psychological safety issue, where helping the system get better is helping it replace you.
