## Five building blocks

Part 1 asked what value is. This part asks the harder question: how do you know it is being realised?

A data-driven organisation rests on five blocks. Four come from a well-known Harvard Business Review article; Prof. Kumar adds the fifth.

<Cards min={200} items={[
  { title: 'Leadership', body: 'Someone senior owns the outcome, not just the budget.' },
  { title: 'Collaboration', body: 'Data crosses departmental walls instead of being hoarded.' },
  { title: 'Data empowerment', body: 'People at the front line can get, trust and use data without asking permission.' },
  { title: 'Value realisation', body: 'You can show what actually got better because of the spending.' },
  { title: 'Workforce', body: 'Will people accept what the machine tells them?', foot: 'The professor’s addition', hl: true },
]} />

The blocks aren't separate. Whether departments share data is a question of culture, and culture is set by leadership. A collaboration problem is often a leadership problem in disguise.

**Value only counts if it fits the strategy.** Three shops on one street: the first survives on the lowest price, so value means a rupee saved on each sale. The second stocks what nobody else has, so value means a price nobody argues with. The third knows every regular by name, so value means the customer never goes elsewhere. Ask which shop your company is, then ask whether your dashboard measures that.

## Caselet one: the dashboard nobody trusts

A global logistics firm, a real client, anonymised. A senior sales leader opens the dashboard, sees a spike, and doesn't decide from it. The number looks wrong. So she messages her analysts: "please check". The answer comes back in two days, sometimes a week. One new measure had been requested two and a half months earlier and still wasn't on the dashboard.

The class found three different faults, and all three matter.

<Cards min={220} items={[
  { title: 'Data quality', body: 'Is the number right? Data arrives from many systems with different owners and no single source of truth, so nobody trusts it.' },
  { title: 'Service quality', body: 'How quickly do the people who fix the number respond? The data team is serving an internal customer. Two days when the decision needed two hours is a service failure.', foot: 'The one most people miss' },
  { title: 'System quality', body: 'Does the tool itself work? Fine at 4 pm; two minutes to load on Monday at 10 when everyone opens it.' },
]} />

<div class="callout trap">
<p class="callout-title">Don't collapse the three</p>

Data quality is about the number. Service quality is about the people who fix the number. System quality is about the software that shows the number. Three diagnoses, three different and differently priced fixes.

</div>

## Caselet two: where everything worked

A large Indian card company built a model to help decide who should be given a card. Data quality: fine. Service quality: fine. System quality: fine. Usage: 300 licences issued, 250 people logging in daily. Every measure was green.

And about **two-thirds of final decisions overrode the model**, on the officer's own judgement, with the reason recorded nowhere. The model existed to prevent bad lending, and it wasn't doing so.

### Adoption is not usage

Someone called this an adoption problem. It isn't.

<Cards min={240} items={[
  { title: 'Adoption', body: 'An attitude, formed before anyone uses the thing. It rests on two beliefs: is it easy to use, and will it benefit me?', foot: 'Lives in the head' },
  { title: 'Usage', body: 'A behaviour, after acceptance. Observable and measurable.', foot: 'Shows up in the logs' },
]} />

The two beliefs come from the **Technology Acceptance Model**: *perceived ease of use* (training moves this one) and *perceived usefulness*. Both are "perceived" because nobody has touched the system yet. AI is the clearest example of ease of use shifting: a few years ago using a model meant writing code; now it means typing a sentence.

### Why usage numbers flatter you

| Usage measure | What it proves | What it doesn't prove |
|---|---|---|
| Licences issued | Purchasing happened | That anyone wanted them |
| Active users | The login works | That the tool influenced a decision |
| Frequency per day | It is part of the routine | That the output is believed |
| Time per session | Time was spent | That the time produced anything |

<div class="callout">
<p class="callout-title">The question to ask in a review</p>

"These are adoption numbers. What is the override rate?" Usage measures the tool. The override rate measures whether the tool changed a decision.

</div>

<Decide q="The card company’s officers override the model two times in three. What is the cheapest effective fix?" options={[
  { label: 'Build a more accurate model.', why: 'The model was already accurate. Accuracy wasn’t the broken box.' },
  { label: 'Make the model’s recommendation compulsory.', why: 'That removes human judgement where it may be right, and breeds resentment.' },
  { label: 'Allow overrides, but require a written reason each time.', good: true, why: 'This is what the company did. It makes the reasoning visible, so the model can improve; it removes the effortlessness of overriding; and it turns a hidden behaviour into data.' },
  { label: 'Run more training sessions.', why: 'The officers knew how to use it. This was about willingness, not ability.' },
]} />

A sharper check, raised in class: how often was the model right when it was overridden? If it was right, the problem is behavioural. If it was wrong, the officers were doing their job.

### The fifth block: workforce

The card company fits none of the original four blocks, which is why the fifth exists. Underneath sits **psychological safety**. Why would someone help build the automation that may replace them? Training doesn't fix that. Credible, consistent assurance from leadership does. People share what they know freely only when it can't be used against them.

## Readiness: measure before you spend

A readiness assessment asks, before any model is built, whether the organisation will use one. It scores five areas:

| Area | What it asks |
|---|---|
| Leadership readiness | Are senior people championing and modelling this? |
| Organisational readiness | Do structure and culture let it spread across departments? |
| Data readiness | Is data unified, trusted and accessible? |
| Decision intelligence | What head start exists: dashboards, a reporting habit? |
| Workforce readiness | Are people skilled and comfortable with digital tools? |

Prof. Kumar ran a short version on the class. The chart starts with the class's scores. Move the sliders to see how the diagnosis changes.

<Fig title="A readiness spider chart" caption="The class's own scores, from 49 responses. Further from the centre means more ready. Treat it as a demonstration of the method, not a finding about any company.">
  <Radar />
</Fig>

Reading the class's result from the outside in:

- **Decision intelligence is strongest.** People can open a report and read a number. That head start shouldn't be thrown away.
- **Leadership is comfortable.**
- **Workforce scores above organisational readiness.** Individuals are capable, but work that needs several people together stalls. That is cultural resistance, and it needs change management, not more training.
- **Data readiness is weakest**, which is exactly why the number on the screen is doubted.

### Survey craft

- **How many responses?** For a scored survey, a common rule of thumb is about ten responses per question. For interviews the rule is different: stop when the same problems keep repeating, often after seven or eight conversations.
- **Scores show symptoms.** A rating scale is a temperature reading. To find the cause, ask an open question: "name your top three pain points".
- **Participation is a leadership matter.** A request from the chief executive, repeated in person, gets a very different response from one email from the technology head.

### Where should the data team report?

One bank's technology centre pulled its data people out of the business departments into a central team, then moved that team's reporting line from the technology head to the chief executive. Data went from a support function to a value-creating one. But the newly elevated data engineers then became less willing to do small jobs for the business departments. The fix created its own problem.

<div class="callout">
<p class="callout-title">There is no correct organisation chart</p>

Every structure solves something and breaks something else. A leader's job isn't to find the perfect design. It is to know where the current one leaks, and whether that leak works for or against the strategy.

</div>

Automated decisions are earned. Keep a person in the loop for a few years, watch how often person and model disagree, and remove the person only when the model's errors have become negligible.

## Viacom: the need

Viacom, the media group behind MTV and Comedy Central, is a real and well-documented case that runs through the rest of the module.

Why did it start a transformation? The class said: the data team was swamped, requests took weeks. True, but not enough. A transformation is approved by the chief executive, so the case has to be financial.

<Fig title="Viacom, 2014 to 2016" caption="Revenue fell from $13.78 billion to $12.49 billion. Adjusted earnings fell from about $2.4 billion to about $1.5 billion.">
  <Bars unit="% fall" rows={[
    { label: 'Revenue', value: 9, note: 'customers going elsewhere' },
    { label: 'Earnings', value: 38, note: 'spending no longer converting into business', mark: true },
  ]} />
</Fig>

Revenue falling means customers are leaving. Profit falling four times as fast means the money spent to hold them is being wasted. Marketing campaigns were data-driven, and subscriptions weren't following, so the data was pointing the wrong way. That is a business case, in the two lines a board reads.

What Viacom fixed:

- **Self-service scales; analyst-driven reporting doesn't.** People waited four to eight weeks for insight. In the model's terms, a service quality failure, the same as the logistics firm.
- **The workforce was never the problem.** The team was highly skilled. What was broken was the plumbing and the culture around them.
- **A person stayed in the loop.** Model output was combined with human judgement first. Automation came later, once earned.

## Data as a product

Prof. Kumar's definition of a data culture is specific: **treat data as another product line**, cared for like anything else you make. Data can then earn money in three ways.

<Cards min={210} items={[

  { title: 'Wrap the offering', body: 'Data rides on what you already sell and makes it unique: equipment that ships with failure predictions.', foot: 'Serves the differentiator · top line' },
  { title: 'Sell the information', body: 'The data itself becomes the offering: a carmaker giving suppliers a paid view of its forward demand.', foot: 'New revenue' },
]} />

<div class="callout">
<p class="callout-title">The test for a data product</p>

Take the data away. Does the business still run? Remove data from a ride-hailing app and no cab is booked and no fare is set. When data stops being a helper and becomes part of the core, it is a product.

</div>

## Scope, ways, means and ends

This framework, from Prof. Kumar's own published research, is the frame the individual ideas hang on.

<Steps items={[
  { title: 'Scope', body: 'Why, and how far? The need, and the level: a process, a function, the firm, an industry.' },
  { title: 'Ways', body: 'Which path to value? Re-creating lost value, or co-creating new value with others. See Part 3.' },
  { title: 'Means', body: 'What does it run on? Capabilities, talent, governance, analytics. See Part 3.' },
  { title: 'Ends', body: 'What shows up? Costs, customer perception, offerings, markets.' },
]} />

In the research, the technology itself ranks last in prominence. It behaves like a **hygiene factor**: having it earns no credit; lacking it stops everything.

The framework was tested on two industrial firms:

| | General Electric: what went wrong | Siemens: what went right |
|---|---|---|
| Scope | No assessment of what the platform was worth | Clear goals, including cost and customer loyalty targets |
| Ways | The digital unit and the business units wanted different things | Initiatives built around each division's customers |
| Means | A centralised programme in a decentralised company; bad news suppressed | Ownership pushed down to divisions, overseen by their heads |
| Ends | Short-term focus; customers didn't understand the offer | Costs reduced, reputation strengthened, growth captured |

### Where the quality ideas come from

- **SERVQUAL** is the source of "service quality". Its five criteria spell RATER: reliability, assurance, tangibles, empathy, responsiveness. Most complaints about internal data teams are about responsiveness and assurance, not accuracy.
- **The Balanced Scorecard** (Kaplan and Norton) links four perspectives in a chain: learning and growth → internal processes → customers → financial results. It shows how a non-financial gain reaches the financial line.

<div class="callout">
<p class="callout-title">Manager takeaway: when results are flat, walk the chain</p>

If a digital investment isn't paying back, don't start by buying a better tool. Check each link in order: is the data trusted, is the support fast, does the tool work, do people use it, do they act on it? Fix the first broken link you find.

</div>
