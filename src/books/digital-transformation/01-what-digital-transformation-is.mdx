## Four words that aren't synonyms

Digitisation, digitalisation, digital enablement and digital transformation are used interchangeably in board decks. They are four rungs of a ladder, and each rung stands on the one below.

<Fig title="The ladder" caption="Most firms stop at the second or third rung and then ask why the returns never came.">
<Steps items={[
  { title: 'Digitisation', body: 'Paper becomes an electronic record. We now have the data.' },
  { title: 'Digitalisation', body: 'The data is cleaned, structured and made to flow between systems.' },
  { title: 'Digital enablement', body: 'People and processes can now do things with it. The means.' },
  { title: 'Digital transformation', body: 'The business gets measurably better. The end.' },
]} />
</Fig>

A bank at each rung: the withdrawal slip becomes a database entry (digitisation); the cash machine, net banking and UPI process transactions by themselves (digitalisation); branch staff are trained to act on the risk score the system shows (enablement); cost per account falls and new customers arrive (transformation).

**Digital artefacts** are the tools on the lower rungs: the ERP, the dashboard, the cloud account.

## Noun or verb?

Prof. Kumar opened the module with a question from Aristotle: is an organisation a thing or a process? A noun or a verb?

| What we **have** (nouns) | What we **do** (verbs) |
|---|---|
| Assembly lines, IT systems | Dynamic capabilities, agility |
| Capital spending, the balance sheet | Planning, adoption, scaling |
| ERP, applications, servers | Transacting, onboarding, storing |
| Platforms, tools, dashboards | Consolidating, orchestrating, deciding |

Everything on the left can be bought with a purchase order. Nothing on the right can.

<div class="callout">
<p class="callout-title">The sentence the module rests on</p>

**We don't "have" digital. We "do" digital.** A noun list is a list of purchases. A verb list is a list of things that now happen differently. Only the second is evidence that anything improved.

</div>

## Output versus outcome

A gym membership is an output. Losing eight kilograms is the outcome. Nobody ever got fit by buying equipment, yet the annual review slide almost always shows the equipment.

- An **output** is the thing you now have: a system, a report, a licence. You can point at it.
- An **outcome** is what now happens differently because of it: faster decisions, lower cost, a customer who stays. You have to measure it.

A company replaces monthly reports with a dashboard. The dashboard is the output. The outcome is that a decision which took a week is now taken in a day. One refinement from class: the outcome isn't "access to data". Data is still something you hold. The outcome is **insight** someone can act on.

Two mistakes to avoid: assuming the outcome follows automatically from the output, and assuming every outcome is a good one. An outcome is simply the consequence. Whether it was worth having is settled with numbers.

### The gap nobody puts on the slide

A spike appears on a manager's dashboard. He doesn't act. He wonders whether the spike is even correct, emails the data team, and waits. Two hours later he decides. That hesitation is the gap between output and outcome: a fine dashboard and the same slow decision as before.

Prof. Kumar sees the pattern often in consulting: a mature reporting estate, crores already spent, and working capital, cost leakage and revenue all flat for two years. The tools aren't bad. Evaluation stopped at the output.

## The lower rungs in detail

**Digitisation** is converting records from paper to electronic form: gathering and storing, nothing more. The data always existed. In a paper register it just couldn't scale: answering one question meant walking through ten registers.

**Digitalisation** is what comes next: the stored data is cleaned, quality-checked and made to flow between systems so it can be used.

Two terms worth keeping: **data hygiene** (is the data clean and trustworthy enough to use?) and **data liquidity** (how freely does it move to where it's needed?). A firm with huge volumes of data and no liquidity feels just like a firm with no data.

## Digital enablement

**Digital enablement is equipping people and processes with the right tools, access and skills so they can actually act.** It isn't owning the artefact. It is being able to do something with it.

| The artefact (noun) | The action it enables (verb) |
|---|---|
| ERP | Transact with customers and suppliers |
| Applications | Onboard employees and customers |
| Reports | Inform management |
| Dashboards | See and act on insight |

What enablement is **not**:

- **Not digitisation.** That is what technology has done to industries. Enablement is what managers do with technology.
- **Not IT implementation.** That is one part of it.
- **Not digital transformation.** Transformation is the destination. Enablement is the work of getting there.

<div class="callout">
<p class="callout-title">Means and end</p>

**Enablement is the means; transformation is the end.** You can be fully enabled and still not transformed, and that is where most programmes are stuck.

</div>

### Change versus transformation

Not every change is a transformation. **Change** is any move away from the current way of working: good or bad, small or large, brief or lasting. **Transformation** is change that was intended to be positive, that lasts, and that is judged on outcomes.

### What "value" means

Executives call many things value: profit, revenue, brand, customer satisfaction, productivity. Underneath them is one thing.

**Value = revenue − cost.** It is the surplus. Everything else is either a driver of that surplus or a result of it. Customer satisfaction, for instance, matters because satisfied customers stay, buy more and cost less to serve.

(The **top line** is revenue, the first line of the income statement. The **bottom line** is profit, the last.)

### Two levers, and only two

One fair objection from class: if most of the cost base is fixed, freeing people's time saves nothing unless that capacity is used for growth or actually removed. Before promising a saving, ask whether the cost is fixed or variable, and who will absorb the freed time.

### From value chain to value system

When a firm has surplus it can't usefully reinvest inside, it looks outward: **related diversification** (a phone maker starts making tablets), **unrelated diversification** (it enters hospitality), **forward integration** (it builds the apps that run on its phones) or **backward integration** (it makes its own chips).

A **value chain** is one firm's set of activities. A **value system** is the connected set of chains: your suppliers', your channels', your customers'. This returns in Part 3.

## The four transformations

| | Full name | Where the technology lands | The test |
|---|---|---|---|
| **ITOT** | IT-enabled organisation transformation | The support layer | Is technology helping someone else do the value-creating work? |
| **PT** | Process transformation | One process, rebuilt end to end | Am I improving one flow, not the whole picture? |
| **BT** | Business transformation | The business model | Has the basis of competition changed? |
| **DT** | Digital transformation | Directly on the primary activities | Is technology creating value itself, with no person in between? |

Two worked examples:

| | A car maker | A bank |
|---|---|---|
| **ITOT** | Installs an ERP, factory sensors and cloud. Cars are still built the same way | Rolls out core banking software across branches. Customers still walk in |
| **PT** | Sensors predict a machine failure before it happens. Downtime falls | Loan approval goes from fifteen days of paperwork to a two-day automated flow |
| **BT** | The car becomes a connected product with paid software features | A mobile-first bank: accounts opened in minutes, instant loans |
| **DT** | Every point where technology serves the customer directly | Everything the customer completes without a branch employee |

  { text: 'A ride-hailing app’s algorithm sets the fare and assigns the car.', answer: 'DT', why: 'Technology does the value-creating work directly.' },
  { text: 'An equipment maker starts selling uptime by subscription instead of selling machines.', answer: 'BT', why: 'The revenue model itself has changed.' },
  { text: 'The same sales data is opened up to customers so they can track orders themselves.', answer: 'DT', why: 'The interaction now happens without the firm’s people in the middle.' },
]} />

Two caveats. "Digital transformation" is also used loosely for the whole journey; classify by where the technology lands and you'll be right either way. And if an initiative delivers no intended outcome, it is none of the four. It is just an artefact.

## Where security sits

| Term | What it protects |
|---|---|
| **Cyber security** | The systems: applications, networks, devices |
| **Data security** | The data itself, wherever it sits or moves |
| **Data privacy** | The individual behind the data |

One incident can pass through all three: entry through a neglected old system (cyber security), reaching the data pipelines (data security), reaching payroll records with names attached (privacy).

The more a firm puts technology directly in front of customers, the more of its value-creating activity is exposed. Security then stops being a cost under IT. It is the condition on which realised value stays realised.

## Thirty years of digital evolution

| Period | Stage | What it gave | Its limit |
|---|---|---|---|
| 1990s | ERP systems | Transactions recorded | Ledgers only, no analysis |
| 2000s | Management reporting | Scheduled standard reports | The business waits weeks |
| 2005+ | Business intelligence | Dashboards and warehouses | Every new measure needs IT |
| 2012+ | Self-service analytics | Analysts query data themselves | Governance gaps appear |
| 2015+ | Predictive models | Forecasts and risk scores | Needs specialist teams |
| 2018+ | Sensors and real-time data | Decisions at the edge | Data volume explodes |
| Now | Generative AI | Recommends what to do | Governance becomes critical |

Most organisations skip a stage and pay for it later. The technology changes at every step. The underlying problem, turning data into a decision someone acts on, barely does.
