## Five words we treat as the same thing

Five words get used interchangeably in board decks and business requirement documents: digitisation, digitalisation, digital enablement, digital artefacts and digital transformation. They are not synonyms. They are four rungs of a ladder, plus a bag of tools, and each rung only stands on the one below it.

<Fig title="The ladder" caption="Each rung only works if the one before it is in place. Most firms stop at rungs two and three, then ask why the returns never came.">

  { title: 'Digitalisation', body: 'That data is cleaned, structured and made to flow between systems.' },
  { title: 'Digital enablement', body: 'People and processes can now do things with it. The means, not the end.' },
  { title: 'Digital transformation', body: 'The outcome: the margin actually moves. Positive change, not new tools.' },
]} />
</Fig>

**Digital artefacts** are the tools sitting on the first two rungs: the ERP, the dashboard, the cloud account.

| Word | The one-line test | A bank, at each rung |
|---|---|---|
| Digitisation | Have we replaced paper with an electronic record? | The withdrawal slip and the ledger become a database entry |
| Digitalisation | Is that data flowing, clean and usable by other processes? | The ATM, then net banking, then UPI: transactions now process themselves |
| Digital enablement | Can our people and processes actually do something with it? | Branch staff trained to act on the risk score the system shows them |
| Digital transformation | Did the business get measurably better because of all this? | Cost per account falls, new customers arrive, the model changes |

<div class="callout trap">
<p class="callout-title">The trap that costs money</p>

Because the words blur, firms report rung-one and rung-two achievements and call them transformation. "We have migrated to cloud" is a rung-two sentence wearing a rung-four costume. When the board later asks where the returns went, everybody is surprised. But the programme was never taken up the ladder.

## Noun or verb: the oldest question in the module

The professor opened with a question borrowed from Aristotle, and it sounds far more abstract than it turns out to be. Is an organisation a thing, or is it a process? If it is a thing, it is a noun. If it is a process, it is a verb.

Line up what a company owns and what a company does, and the two columns look completely different.

| The noun view: what we *have* | The verb view: what we *do* |
|---|---|
| Assembly lines | Dynamic capabilities |
| IT systems | Agility |
| Capital expenditure | Planning |
| The balance sheet | Adoption |
| People on the payroll | Scaling |
| ERP, applications, servers | Transitioning, onboarding, storing |
| Platforms, tools, dashboards | Consolidating, orchestrating, deciding |

Every item on the left can be bought with a purchase order. Not one item on the right can. The noun view gives you a value proposition on paper. The verb view is where value actually gets created.

> We do not "have" digital. We "do" digital.

Everything else in this module (digitisation against digitalisation, output against outcome, enablement against transformation, artefact against value) is that same sentence wearing a different costume.

This isn't word-play. It changes what you put on a review slide. A noun list is a list of purchases. A verb list is a list of things that now happen differently. Only one of those is evidence that anything improved.

## Output versus outcome

This is the single most useful idea in the module, and it is deceptively simple.

A gym membership is an output. A treadmill in the spare room is an output. Losing eight kilograms is the outcome. Nobody has ever got fit by buying equipment, and yet the annual review slide almost always shows the equipment.

An **output** is the thing you now have: a system, a report, a licence, a platform. You can point at it and photograph it. An **outcome** is what now happens differently because that thing exists: faster decisions, lower cost, a customer who stays. You can't photograph an outcome. You have to measure it.

The class worked one through live. A company replaces its monthly reports with a dashboard.

- **Output:** the dashboard itself. The thing that got built.
- **Outcome:** the people who use it get to an insight faster and act on it. A decision that used to take a week is taken in a day.

Somebody in class said the outcome was "access to data". The professor pushed back: the outcome is access to *insight*. Data is still an output; it is a thing you hold. Insight is what lets somebody act. The distance between those two words is exactly the distance between a project that pays for itself and one that doesn't.

| Output: what we have | Outcome: what we do differently |
|---|---|
| Rs. 20 crore spent on the ERP migration | Transaction delay down; closing is seamless |
| 200 dashboard licences bought | A decision that took 2 hours now takes 10 minutes |
| A 1,000-seat AI assistant subscription | The working capital cycle actually shortens |
| 200 TB of cloud storage provisioned | Cost leakage falls; the top line moves |
| Dashboard open 3 hours a day | Insight is trusted enough to act on without a re-check |

The left column is nouns: things purchased, easy to count. The right is verbs: behaviour changed, hard to fake. The gap between them is where the return on investment dies.

**Two mistakes people make.** First, assuming the outcome follows automatically from the output. It doesn't: buying the dashboard doesn't buy the decision. Second, assuming every outcome is a good one. A change in behaviour can be a bad change. An outcome is simply the consequence. Whether it was worth having is a separate question you answer with numbers.

## Digitisation: paper becomes a record

Go back twenty or thirty years, to any business at all: a footwear shop, a grocery store, a bank, a shipping line. How were transactions recorded? In a register. The shopkeeper wrote down what was sold and at what price, and at the end of the month somebody added it all up by hand.

Here is the part people miss. **The data always existed.** Businesses have never been short of data. The problem was that it wasn't in electronic form, so it couldn't scale. To answer one question you had to walk through ten registers. Finding a single entry in a bank ledger was a morning's work.

**Digitisation** is converting records from a physical medium into an electronic one. Gathering and storing. Nothing more. You stand in a queue, sign a withdrawal slip, the slip goes into a register, and then those registers become database entries. The work hasn't changed at all: the same steps happen, on a different medium.

Two examples from the professor's own campus. There is a building at IIM Calcutta stuck in renovation limbo because it holds books of records nobody wants to touch: an entire building held hostage by paper. And in the same institute, with leave portals and email and a website, faculty payroll was historically kept in a *lal kitab*, a red register. Modern on the surface, paper underneath. Most large organisations have their own lal kitab somewhere.

<div class="callout trap">
<p class="callout-title">Digitisation is not transformation</p>

Scanning twenty years of registers into PDFs is a real achievement and changes nothing about how the business runs. If your programme charter stops here, you have bought storage. Most Indian enterprises finished this rung ten to fifteen years ago, which is why, when somebody says "we are doing digitisation", the honest follow-up is: still?

</div>

## Digitalisation: making the data flow

Now the same bank puts a cash machine outside the branch. Suddenly there is a system that *processes* the transaction and doesn't merely record it. Then net banking. Then UPI. You are no longer storing a record of what a clerk did. The system is doing the thing.

**Digitalisation** is what happens after digitisation: the stored data is cleaned, structured, quality-checked and made to flow between systems and processes, so that it can be used. The class found good words for it: processing, leveraging, equipping, extending. All point at the same shift, from holding data to moving and using it.

**The glacier, the melt and the glass.** The glacier is your stack of systems: solid, enormous, impressive, frozen. Having the glacier is digitisation. Digitalisation is the melt: water running down the mountain, filtered and carried in pipes. The final glass of clean drinking water, the decision somebody actually takes, is where value gets consumed. Plenty of organisations are extremely proud of their glacier. Far fewer can hand you the glass.

Two words worth taking to work. **Data hygiene** is whether the data is clean and trustworthy enough to be used at all. **Data liquidity** is how freely it moves between the places that need it. A firm can have enormous data volume and almost no liquidity, and it will feel exactly like a firm with no data.

## Digital enablement: the grammar test

This is the sharpest idea in Part 1, and it came from the professor teaching grammar to his daughter. Nouns are names of things. Verbs are actions. Apply that to a company's technology estate and the confusion clears up at once.

**The noun column: what we have.** An ERP, applications, spreadsheets, monthly reports, connectivity, servers and cloud, dashboards. Each has a common form (the category, such as "dashboard tool") and a proper form (the brand you actually bought).

**The verb column: what we do.**

| The artefact (noun) | The action it enables (verb) | Who is on the other end |
|---|---|---|
| ERP | Transact | Customers and suppliers, connected to the firm |
| Applications | Onboard | Employees, customers, suppliers, buyers |
| Spreadsheets | Record, store, analyse | Whoever needs the working number |
| Monthly reports | Report | Management |
| Dashboards | Visualise and consume insight | Decision-makers |

<div class="callout">
<p class="callout-title">Digital enablement, defined</p>

Digital enablement is equipping people and processes, with the right tools, the right access and the right skills, so that the verbs can actually be performed. It isn't owning the artefact. It is being able to act with it.

</div>

Two clean examples from class. Rolling out AI-assistant training across the workforce is enablement, not procurement. Making sure a UPI QR code is physically present at every counter, including the vegetable vendor's, is enablement: the technology existed long before the enablement did.

**What enablement is not:**

- **Not digitisation.** Digitisation describes what technology has done to industries. Enablement describes what managers do with technology.
- **Not IT implementation.** That is a subset, the data and process conditions, not the whole.
- **Not digital transformation.** Transformation describes the destination. Enablement describes the work of getting there.
- **Not AI adoption.** Adoption is one outcome on the people side, not the full picture.

Digital enablement is the means; digital transformation is the end. Enablement puts people in a position to act differently. Transformation is the positive outcome that shows up in the numbers when they do. You can be fully enabled and still not transformed, and that is precisely where most programmes are stuck.

Put another way: outcomes are aspired to by business leaders; outputs are realised by technology leaders to support the business teams. Enablement is what managers do so that technology produces impact and not just activity.

## How companies wrongly measure success

Now watch what happens when a company evaluates its own digital journey. Here is the list on the review slide, and every line is commonplace industry language.

- We have invested Rs. 20 crore migrating to a new ERP.
- We have bought 200 dashboard licences.
- We have an enterprise-wide, 1,000-seat AI assistant subscription.
- We have provisioned 200 TB of cloud storage.
- Our dashboard utilisation rate is three hours per user per day.

Every single one is an output. They describe what was bought and how much it was used. Not one describes anything that got better.

**The gap nobody puts on the slide.** Push for the outcome and some of it is real: an in-memory ERP does improve transaction response time. But then comes the line everybody says: "and our decision-making will be more effective". Ideally, yes. Practically, watch what happens.

> A spike appears on my dashboard. I do not act on it. I wonder whether the spike is even correct. I email the back-end team. We check back and forth. Two hours later, I take the decision.

That inertia is the gap. It is a short arrow on a whiteboard and an enormous distance in practice. If the reconciliation happens fast, the decision really is insight-driven. If it doesn't, you have a beautiful dashboard and a two-hour decision cycle, which is what you had before, with better colours.

The pattern the professor keeps seeing in consulting: firms with a fully mature reporting ecosystem, twelve crore already spent, and working capital ratios unchanged, cost leakage unchanged, the top line broadly flat for eight quarters. The diagnosis isn't that the tools are bad. Evaluation stopped at the output. People have adopted the system, but it isn't integrated into how the work actually gets decided.

## Change versus transformation

A smaller question the class argued about. Is every change a transformation?

No. **Change** is any movement away from the current way of working. It can be positive or negative, small or drastic, temporary or lasting. **Transformation** is change that was intended to be positive, and that lasts. The word carries the intent inside it.

The class tested it with a live example. Generative AI has changed how a whole generation works, and arguably it is also taking away some of their original thinking. Is that a transformation?

<div class="callout">
<p class="callout-title">Intent defines it; measurement corrects it</p>

When we plan, we plan only for positive change. Later we may discover side effects, and we plug those as we go. A programme is called a transformation because of the positive outcomes it intends and pursues, not because every consequence turned out well. Technology isn't automatically transformative.

</div>

And a transformation is judged on outcomes, not outputs: positive changes, sustained, measured on what actually got better.
