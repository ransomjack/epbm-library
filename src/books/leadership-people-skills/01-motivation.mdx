## The question this session answers

A student who can't read a textbook for twenty minutes will finish a long novel in one sitting. Same brain, same afternoon, very different effort. Motivation is about that gap: not how capable someone is, but **what makes them try, in which direction, and for how long**.

Prof. Jha opened with two old, opposing answers to why people behave as they do:

- **Nurture.** Mowgli, the child raised by wolves, behaves like a wolf. Change the surroundings and you change the behaviour. The same employee behaves differently in six different jobs.
- **Nature.** Phrenology, the discredited colonial "science" of reading skull shape to predict criminality, assumed you are born what you are and cannot change.

The truth sits between them, and Organisational Behaviour (OB) studies both.

## Motivation, defined: three dials

**Motivation** is the set of processes that account for an individual's **intensity**, **direction** and **persistence** of effort toward a goal.

<Cards min={180} items={[
  { k: '1', title: 'Intensity', body: 'How hard someone tries. What most people mean by "motivated", and not enough on its own.' },
  { k: '2', title: 'Direction', body: 'Where the effort points. High intensity aimed the wrong way is wasted.' },
  { k: '3', title: 'Persistence', body: 'How long the effort lasts. The whole month, not just week one.' },
]} />

A delivery driver who drives hard, on the right route, for the whole month hits the target. Drop any one dial and the target slips.

## The early theories: needs

These are the theories everyone has heard of. They are easy to explain and useful for starting a conversation, but the research behind most of them is thin.

### Maslow's hierarchy, and Alderfer's ERG upgrade

Maslow arranged human needs in five levels. You chase the next level only once the one below is reasonably satisfied. Lower needs are met from outside (pay, security); higher ones from inside (self-respect, growth). The practical instruction: find where a person is on the ladder and motivate at or just above it.

<Fig title="Maslow's five levels, and how ERG regroups them" caption="ERG (right) folds Maslow's five levels into three and drops the strict order: you can pursue all three at once.">
  <Pyramid />
</Fig>

Alderfer's **ERG theory** collapses the five into **Existence**, **Relatedness** and **Growth**, and makes two changes that matter:

1. **No strict hierarchy.** You can care about growth before your existence needs are fully met.
2. **Frustration–regression.** When a higher need is blocked, people compensate by demanding more of a lower one.

Frustration–regression explains a pay pattern you see everywhere. A worker on an offshore rig can't satisfy relatedness for weeks at a time, so the demand regresses to "then pay me more", and offshore jobs carry a premium. Long-haul truck drivers are cut off from family in the same way but usually get no such premium. Their frustrated need simply goes unanswered.

<div class="callout">
<p class="callout-title">Maslow's "fast-forward advantage"</p>

Prof. Jha's phrase for why managers love Maslow: it is famous and takes thirty seconds to explain. But research barely supports it, because needs don't queue in a neat order. Use it to open a conversation, not as a literal ladder.

</div>

### Herzberg's two-factor theory

Herzberg asked people to describe times they felt exceptionally good or bad about their jobs. The two lists were made of different things. His conclusion: **satisfaction and dissatisfaction are not opposite ends of one scale**. They are two separate scales.

<Fig title="Two separate scales">
  <TwoScales />
</Fig>

A clean canteen and market pay get you to "no complaints". The chance to own a project end to end is what creates real drive.

<div class="callout trap">
<p class="callout-title">Common mix-up</p>

The opposite of *satisfaction* is **no satisfaction**. The opposite of *dissatisfaction* is **no dissatisfaction**. Removing a hygiene problem never creates satisfaction; it only removes dissatisfaction.

</div>

The method draws fair criticism: people credit themselves for the good times and blame their surroundings for the bad. Yet the theory is used widely, including across Asia in settings such as Japan and India (few managers anywhere are unfamiliar with it), and its core recommendation, enrich the work itself, has held up.

### McClelland's theory of needs

Three needs, acquired through life and held in different mixes by different people.

<Cards min={200} items={[
  { title: 'Need for achievement (nAch)', body: 'The drive to excel against a standard. High achievers prefer roughly 50–50 odds: easy wins give no satisfaction, impossible ones no challenge.' },
  { title: 'Need for power (nPow)', body: 'The need to influence and have impact on others.' },
  { title: 'Need for affiliation (nAff)', body: 'The desire for friendly, close relationships.' },
]} />

**The best managers tend to be high in nPow and lower in nAff.** A manager who needs to be liked struggles with hard calls. In the classic beanbag-toss test, a high achiever picks the middle target, challenging but winnable, rather than the near-impossible jackpot. The catch: these needs are hard to measure, so the theory helps you understand people more than it helps you sort them.

## The contemporary theories: better evidence

These are the theories to lean on when you actually have to decide something.

### Self-determination theory

People want to feel their actions are their own. Three psychological needs drive us: **autonomy** (control over what we do), **competence** (being good at it) and **relatedness** (connection with others).

Its most useful sub-idea is **cognitive evaluation theory**: adding an external reward to a task someone already enjoys can *reduce* their interest in it. Once you are paid to do it, it starts to feel like something you have to do. In the textbook example, a woman who happily volunteered fifteen hours a week at an animal shelter found the joy draining away once the shelter hired her to do the same work for pay.

A related idea, **self-concordance**, is how well your reasons for pursuing a goal fit your own values. People who pursue goals for intrinsic reasons are happier and enjoy the striving whether or not they win.

<div class="callout beyond">
<p class="callout-title">Beyond the notes: when rewards help and when they hurt</p>

The crowding-out effect is strongest when the reward is **expected and tied to simply doing the task**, and when it feels controlling. Rewards that are unexpected, or that recognise genuine competence ("this was excellent work"), tend not to damage intrinsic interest. For interesting work: pay people fairly and well, so money is off the table, then let recognition and autonomy do the motivating.

</div>

### Goal-setting theory and MBO

"Just do your best" is one of the least useful instructions a manager can give. Locke's goal-setting theory shows that **specific, difficult goals, with feedback, produce higher performance** than vague or easy ones.

<Cards min={170} items={[
  { title: 'Specific', body: 'beats vague. "12 round trips a week" beats "do better".' },
  { title: 'Difficult', body: 'beats easy, once the goal is accepted.' },
  { title: 'Feedback', body: 'beats silence. Self-generated feedback is the most powerful kind.' },
]} />

Three things decide whether goals work: **commitment** to the goal, the **task** (simple, independent tasks respond best) and **national culture**.

**Management by Objectives (MBO)** puts goal-setting into practice: organisation-wide goals cascade into specific, verifiable, time-bound goals at every level, set together with the people who must deliver them.

<div class="callout trap">
<p class="callout-title">Goals have a dark side</p>

Wells Fargo's aggressive cross-selling targets (eight products per customer) led staff to open around two million accounts customers never asked for. Specific, difficult goals work, but unrealistic goals tied to rewards invite cheating. As one group of researchers (Ordóñez, Schweitzer and colleagues) put it, goals work like a prescription drug: powerful, with side effects, so dose them carefully.

</div>

### Self-efficacy theory

**Self-efficacy** is your belief that you can do a *specific* task. People with high self-efficacy try harder after negative feedback; people with low self-efficacy give up. Bandura identified four ways to build it, in order of strength:

<Steps items={[
  { title: 'Enactive mastery', body: 'Actually doing the task successfully. The strongest source by far.' },
  { title: 'Vicarious modelling', body: 'Watching someone similar to you succeed. A peer, not a champion.' },
  { title: 'Verbal persuasion', body: 'A credible person convincing you that you can.' },
  { title: 'Arousal', body: 'Getting psyched up. Helps effort tasks; hurts tasks needing calm precision.' },
]} />

Watching a world champion won't lift your golf confidence. Watching a friend with your handicap sink the putt will.

**The Pygmalion effect** is the same idea seen from the manager's side: one person's expectation makes itself come true. Teachers told that certain (randomly chosen) students were gifted gave them more attention and harder work, and those students really did achieve more. Goal-setting and self-efficacy reinforce each other here: setting someone a difficult goal tells them you believe in them.

### Reinforcement and social learning

Where goal-setting is about intentions, **reinforcement theory** ignores the inner state altogether: behaviour is a function of its consequences. People repeat rewarded behaviour and drop unrewarded behaviour, and rewards work best when they come immediately (Skinner's operant conditioning). Strictly, it is not a theory of motivation, but it is a powerful way to analyse what controls behaviour.

**Social-learning theory** extends it: we also learn by watching others. A model's influence depends on four processes.

<Flow items={[
  { t: 'Attention', s: 'We notice the model' },
  { t: 'Retention', s: 'We remember what they did' },
  { t: 'Motor reproduction', s: 'We can actually do it' },
  { t: 'Reinforcement', s: 'We are rewarded for doing it' },
]} />

A new hire copies how a respected senior handles an angry client: noticing, remembering, being able to imitate, and being praised when they do.

### Expectancy theory: the spine of the session

Vroom's expectancy theory says the strength of effort depends on three linked beliefs. If the answer to any one is "no", motivation collapses.

<Fig title="Expectancy theory" caption="Each link is scored from 0 to 1 and the three multiply, so a zero anywhere zeroes the whole thing.">
  <Flow items={[
    { t: 'Effort', s: '"If I try hard…"' },
    { t: 'Performance', s: '"…will it show up as good performance?"' },
    { t: 'Reward', s: '"…will I really be rewarded?"' },
    { t: 'Personal goal', s: '"…and is it a reward I want?"', hl: true },
  ]} links={['Expectancy', 'Instrumentality', 'Valence']} note="Motivation = Expectancy × Instrumentality × Valence" />
</Fig>

This is why so many capable people do only the minimum: one link has quietly broken for them.

| Broken link | What the employee is thinking | The fix |
|---|---|---|
| **Expectancy** | "However hard I try, I can't hit that number" or "the appraisal won't see it" | Training, resources, realistic targets, fair appraisal |
| **Instrumentality** | "Last year's top performers got the same increment as everyone else" | Visibly tie rewards to performance, and keep promises |
| **Valence** | "The reward is a photo with the CEO. I'd rather have the money" | Let people choose their reward |

The valence example is real: a quality programme rewarded the best team with a photo with the CEO, and the workers asked if they could skip the photo and have the money. The standard fix is **cafeteria-style rewards**: "we'll spend this amount on you, you choose how". A young employee takes course fees; an older one takes medical cover.

### Equity theory and organisational justice

We don't judge rewards on their own; we compare. We take the ratio of our **outcomes** (pay, recognition) to our **inputs** (effort, experience) and compare it with a **referent**, usually someone similar.

<Fig title="Fairness is a ratio, not an amount">
  <Flow items={[
    { t: 'My outcomes ÷ my inputs', s: 'What I get for what I put in' },
    { t: 'Referent’s outcomes ÷ inputs', s: 'What a comparable person gets', hl: true },
  ]} links={['compared with']} note="Equal ratios feel fair. Unequal ratios create tension that people act to remove." />
</Fig>

When the ratios don't match, people act in one of six ways: change their inputs (work less), change their outcomes (push for more), distort how they see themselves, distort how they see the other person, pick a different referent, or leave.

In the class example, Biprajeet earns ₹40,000 a month and is *less* satisfied than Aneesha on ₹20,000, because he compares himself with managers at other firms who earn more. Higher pay, lower satisfaction: his referent changed.

Equity broadens into **organisational justice**, which has four parts:

<Cards min={190} items={[
  { title: 'Distributive', body: 'Are the outcomes fair?' },
  { title: 'Procedural', body: 'Is the process used to decide them fair?' },
  { title: 'Informational', body: 'Are we given truthful explanations?' },
  { title: 'Interpersonal', body: 'Are we treated with dignity and respect?' },
]} />

<div class="callout">
<p class="callout-title">Manager takeaway: fair process protects you when the outcome is bad</p>

You can't always give people the raise or promotion they want. You can always explain the decision honestly, use a process they can see, and treat them with respect. People accept disappointing outcomes far more readily when procedural, informational and interpersonal justice are intact.

</div>

### Job engagement

**Engagement** is the investment of a person's physical, cognitive and emotional energy in their work, deeper than simply liking the job. It rises when the work feels meaningful, when there are enough resources to do it well, and when the person's values match the organisation's. Two cautions: it overlaps heavily with job satisfaction, and over-engagement has a dark side. A nurse completely absorbed in patient care is more effective, but the same total involvement, unchecked, becomes burnout.

## Putting it together: the integrated model

None of these theories competes with the others. Expectancy theory provides the spine, and every other theory explains one of its links.

| Link | What it needs | Theories that explain it |
|---|---|---|
| Effort → Performance | Ability, opportunity, a fair appraisal, and goals that direct effort | Goal-setting, self-efficacy |
| Performance → Reward | Rewards genuinely tied to performance, judged fair | Reinforcement, equity and justice |
| Reward → Personal goal | Rewards that match what the person values | Maslow, ERG, McClelland, self-determination |

One exception: a person high in need for achievement jumps straight from effort to personal goals. They are driven by the work itself, not by the organisation's rewards.

**The manager's diagnostic question** is therefore not "which incentive should I use?" but "which of the three links is broken for this person, and is the reward at the end one they actually value?"

## From theory to practice: the manager's toolkit

### Designing the job: the Job Characteristics Model

Any job can be described on five core dimensions, which create three psychological states that drive internal motivation.

| Core dimension | Meaning | Low vs high | Creates |
|---|---|---|---|
| **Skill variety** | How many different skills the job uses | Turning one part all day vs running a small workshop | Meaningfulness |
| **Task identity** | Completing a whole, identifiable piece of work | Making table legs vs building the whole cabinet | Meaningfulness |
| **Task significance** | How much the job affects other people's lives | Sweeping a floor vs ICU nursing | Meaningfulness |
| **Autonomy** | Freedom to schedule and decide how to do the work | Following a script vs planning your own day | Responsibility |
| **Feedback** | How directly the work shows how well you're doing | Waiting for an annual review vs seeing results daily | Knowledge of results |

The effect is strongest for people with high **growth-need strength**: those who want to learn and stretch at work.

The five combine into a **Motivating Potential Score**. Try it: move autonomy or feedback to 1 and watch the score collapse.

</Fig>

### Redesigning jobs

| Approach | What it does | Watch out for |
|---|---|---|
| **Job rotation** (cross-training) | Moves people between tasks of similar skill level. Fights boredom, builds a flexible workforce and lets people see how they fit the whole. | Training time and lost productivity at each switch |
| **Job enrichment** | Adds higher-level responsibility: planning, deciding, checking your own work. Herzberg's motivators in practice. About twice as effective at cutting turnover as simply giving candidates a realistic preview of the job. | Only works if people want more responsibility |
| **Relational job design** | Lets employees see the difference they make to others. | Needs real contact, not a poster |

The textbook's case for redesign is a worker who quit a well-paid car assembly-line job for a lower-paid one at a print shop, because fitting the same panel eight hours a day was, in his words, turning his brain to jelly. Redesign exists to prevent exactly that. The case for relational design: university fundraisers who spent a few minutes with a student who would receive the scholarship persisted 42% longer on calls and raised nearly twice as much money.

### Giving people a voice

**Employee involvement and participation (EIP)** uses employees' input to raise their commitment. **Participative management** means real joint decision-making with the boss. **Representative participation** works through a small group, such as works councils (consulted on decisions) or employee board members, as at Volkswagen. Its motivational effect is weaker than direct participation. Done well, involvement raises intrinsic motivation and the sense that procedures are fair. Done carelessly, it becomes lip service. The failure mode is "we talk, but they don't listen": asking for input and then ignoring it is worse than not asking.

### Pay and rewards

Pay involves two separate decisions.

**1. What to pay: the pay structure.** Balance **internal equity** (what the job is worth inside the firm, found through job evaluation) against **external equity** (what the market pays, found through pay surveys). Firms choose to lead, match or lag the market. Costco pays above its warehouse-club rivals and gets higher productivity and lower turnover in return: leading the market can pay for itself.

**2. How to pay individuals: variable pay.**

| Plan | How it works | Strength | Risk |
|---|---|---|---|
| **Piece rate** | A fixed amount per unit produced | High productivity | Quantity over quality |
| **Merit pay** | Tied to appraisal ratings | Rewards individual performance | Only as fair as the appraisal |
| **Bonus** | One-off reward for recent performance | Doesn't inflate base pay | Feels like an entitlement if routine |
| **Profit sharing** | Paid from a profitability formula | Builds commitment, especially in small firms | Weak link to individual effort |
| **Gainsharing** | Team shares the gains from improved performance | Ownership and cooperation | The formula must be seen as fair, and stress can rise because everyone now has a stake in everyone else |
| **ESOP** | Employees own company shares | Long-term ownership | Works only if people feel like owners and have a say |

**Flexible benefits** let each employee build their own benefits package: expectancy theory's valence point made concrete, because a young single employee and an older parent value completely different things. **Recognition programmes** are the cheapest tool of all, because praise is free yet powerful. The risk is favouritism, so the criteria must be visibly fair.

<div class="callout">
<p class="callout-title">Manager takeaway: recognition does what money can't</p>

The textbook tells of an employee on $8.50 an hour, in a job that isn't challenging, who speaks about it with real enthusiasm. Her supervisor praises her in front of others and named her Employee of the Month. If your budget is tight, recognition is the lever you always have: specific, prompt, public where appropriate, and fair.

</div>

## The case, briefly: Shivani Carriers

The case isn't there to be memorised. It earns its place by making every theory above concrete at the real bottom of the pyramid, with blue-collar truck drivers instead of office staff. The test is whether the principles hold across that gap.

The class case was a family-run Indian trucking firm, founded in the 1970s, carrying full truck loads (one client's goods fill one truck to one destination) for large consumer-goods clients, with a fleet of about ninety trucks and drivers drawn mostly from low-income, rural backgrounds. The drivers' working life is punishing: very long days, weeks away from family, little respect. Margins are thin. Fuel is more than half of operating cost, and a truck's fixed costs are the same whether it runs 5,500 or 8,000 km a month, so **the driver is the biggest lever on profit**: good driving habits and lower turnover cut fuel and maintenance by 20 to 25%, while about a quarter of drivers leave each year.

The owner's intervention reads like a checklist of this session: a clear monthly target of 7,000 km with a per-kilometre incentive above it and a higher rate beyond 8,000 km, results displayed weekly, a "stars of the month" board, a deliberate shift to treating drivers with dignity, and supporting systems: vehicle tracking, closed containers so drivers could sleep safely, pre-paid cards, and routes grouped so they could spend time with their families. It worked, until the scheme was changed after the fact with a new baseline and a cap, and drivers felt the reward had been clawed back.

| Concept | Where it shows up |
|---|---|
| Maslow / ERG: existence | Cash to support the family is the dominant motive |
| ERG: frustration–regression | Relatedness is blocked by weeks away, so the demand becomes "just pay me more" |
| Herzberg | Pay and conditions are hygiene; respect, and being told they are a pillar of the industry, are the real motivators |
| Goal-setting | A specific, accepted monthly target with visible feedback |
| Expectancy | Effort to performance: the daily distance needed was shown to be doable. Performance to reward: broken when the scheme was changed after drivers delivered. Valence: cash over a ceremonial gift |
| Equity and justice | Drivers compare themselves with managers; cuts based on suspicion violate interpersonal justice |
| Self-efficacy / Pygmalion | Encouraging literacy, and telling drivers they carry responsibility for valuable cargo, raises their belief in themselves |
| Variable pay | Capping an incentive and adding conditions after the fact is exactly the demotivator the theory warns about |

The manager's real question isn't "which incentive?" but "which of the three expectancy links is broken for this person, and is the reward at the end one they actually value?"

The lesson travels well beyond trucking: wherever a job is easily substituted and a higher need is frustrated, the same mechanics apply, and **a reward changed after people have earned it does more damage than no reward at all**.
