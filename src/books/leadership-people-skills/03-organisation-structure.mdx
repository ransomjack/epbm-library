## Why structure matters

Greg James's team in Session 2 didn't fail for lack of effort or goodwill. It failed for lack of an architecture: who owns what, who reports to whom, where decisions sit. That architecture is not an org chart drawn after the fact. It is designed, and the design decides what the people inside can do.

An **organisational structure** defines how job tasks are formally **divided, grouped and coordinated**.

### Structure serves effectiveness, but whose?

Prof. Jha opened with a bigger question. Structure exists to make an organisation effective, but effectiveness isn't one number. Employees, customers, shareholders and regulators each judge it differently, and their measures pull in different directions: higher salaries please employees and cut short-term returns. You make effectiveness judgements all day: as a customer, as a parent choosing a school, as an employee. What a business school counts (enrolment, word of mouth, dropouts) isn't what your family counts. A manager could track hundreds of measures; the skill is choosing which ones go on the dashboard. Because these competing measures rarely move together, structure is partly a decision about whose effectiveness wins.

The **strategic constituencies model** says an organisation organises itself around whoever is most critical to its survival. When the US airline industry was heavily regulated, winning route rights was the survival issue, so lawyers rose to run airlines. A consumer-goods firm elevates sales; a cash-starved firm elevates finance; a public-sector unit orients itself to its ministry.

## The seven dials

Every structure ever designed is a setting of the same seven elements.

| Element | What it means | The question it answers |
|---|---|---|
| **Work specialisation** | How far work is divided into separate jobs | How narrow is each job? |
| **Departmentalisation** | The basis for grouping jobs | How are we organised into units? |
| **Chain of command** | The unbroken line of authority from top to bottom | Who do I take orders from? |
| **Span of control** | How many people one manager directs | How many people per manager? |
| **Centralisation and decentralisation** | Where decisions are made | How much authority sits lower down? |
| **Formalisation** | How far jobs are standardised by written rules | How rule-bound is the work? |
| **Boundary spanning** | Contact across group and organisational boundaries | Who reads the outside world? |

Think of seven dials on a mixing board. A mechanistic firm turns specialisation, centralisation and formalisation up; an early-stage startup has them all near zero; Indian Railways sits near the maximum. Neither extreme is right everywhere.

<div class="callout">
<p class="callout-title">Prof. Jha's three dimensions</p>

The seven elements can be grouped into three dimensions:

1. **Complexity**, made up of *horizontal differentiation* (division of labour across jobs), *vertical differentiation* (the number of layers) and *spatial differentiation* (spread across locations). More of any one makes the organisation more complex.
2. **Standardisation**, used interchangeably with formalisation: how far rules and procedures fix the work.
3. **Centralisation versus decentralisation**: where decisions are made.

</div>

### Work specialisation: gains, then a ceiling

Specialisation raises productivity, up to a point. Ford's moving assembly line cut the time to assemble a Model T chassis from about 12.5 hours to about 1.5 hours. But the gains flatten and then reverse as human diseconomies set in: boredom, errors, absenteeism and turnover, until the cost of coordinating exceeds the gain.

The professor drew the case for specialisation as a learning curve: heavy effort for small early gains, then performance rising fast, then tapering. (His own joke: he isn't better at his 5,000th class than at his 4,000th.) Two limits follow. Efficiency gains aren't endless, and doing the same task for ever demotivates. He tied it to the class's own lives: "you signed up for this programme because you felt stuck in a repetitive role."

  <SpecCurve />
</Fig>

McDonald's runs extreme specialisation: consistent and fast, with high staff turnover. Toyota builds in rotation and quality participation and gets lower turnover and higher productivity per worker.

The work-redesign movement grew from this ceiling. Rotation had limits in practice (you can't make the cook the security guard). **Job enlargement** added more tasks at the same level, which doesn't help if they are all dull. **Job enrichment** gave people a slice of the boss's job, which does. An IIM Calcutta colleague's experiment at a post office replaced single-task counters with single-window service: queues shortened and staff satisfaction rose. It is the old ideal of task variety: "fisherman in the morning, factory worker in the afternoon, philosopher in the evening."

### Departmentalisation: five ways to group jobs

| Basis | How it works | Example |
|---|---|---|
| **Functional** | Group similar specialists together | Acme; cooks with cooks, waiters with waiters |
| **Product or service** | Each line has its own resources | Tata Group: Steel, Motors, TCS |
| **Geographic** | Group by region | Regional structures in consumer goods |
| **Process** | Group around stages of the workflow | Kitchen → runner → pantry → floor |
| **Customer** | Group by type of customer | A bank's retail, corporate and private-wealth arms |

Every department builds its own identity and its own "us versus them" ("we bring in the money", "we sell it"). That is **differentiation**, and an organisation needs as much of it as its environment demands. But the specialists must then be pulled back together, which is **integration**: cross-functional teams, regular all-hands, or a superordinate goal ("at the end of the day we all serve the same organisation"). Good structure does both.

### Chain of command and span of control

**Unity of command** means each person reports to one boss; two bosses with conflicting instructions produce paralysis. **Span of control** sets the shape of the whole organisation.

<Fig title="4,096 front-line workers, two spans of control" caption="Doubling the span from 4 to 8 removes two levels of management and 780 managers. Wide spans are cheaper and faster but only work when the work is routine and staff are skilled.">
  <SpanTree />
</Fig>

With a span of 4, those 4,096 workers need 1,365 managers across more levels; with a span of 8, only 585.

Span depends on how critical the task is (holding a nuclear button might be one-to-one), how skilled the staff are and how complex the work is. There is a human trade-off too: customers prefer flat organisations (a complaint is fixed faster with fewer layers), while many employees prefer tall ones, because layers mean more promotions, titles and, in India especially, status symbols such as a bigger car. The professor's aside from his own career: institutions like the IIMs are quietly becoming more hierarchical over time, and the flat, democratic structure he joined for is eroding.

<div class="callout">
<p class="callout-title">What is a supervisor actually for?</p>

Every layer of hierarchy costs money. A chicken chain with three layers of managers carries overheads that a single-owner shop selling similar food doesn't, so the lean shop can undercut it. Prof. Jha split the supervisor's job in two:

- **Control** (making sure people don't slack) is increasingly hard to justify in knowledge work.
- **Coordination** (making sure hand-offs between people and processes work) remains essential.

Quality shows the same shift: end-of-line inspection is giving way to in-process quality, where each step checks the one before it. Every layer must earn its keep.

His sharpest provocation: authority based on experience weakens in a fast-changing world. "Human beings are not pickles; older isn't automatically better" when the world has changed under you.

</div>

### Centralisation, decentralisation and formalisation

Centralised organisations decide at the top: consistent and controlled, but slow, and the boss becomes a single point of failure, a "one-man band". Decentralised ones push authority down: faster, more engaging, and they develop future leaders, at the risk of inconsistency. Amul is decentralised by design: farmers and village cooperatives decide locally while the federation handles processing and distribution. Most firms mix the two, centralising brand, strategy and capital, and decentralising front-line service.

<div class="callout">
<p class="callout-title">The fly in the soup</p>

Prof. Jha's running example. Under centralisation the waiter says "I'm not authorised, I'll escalate", and days later a letter offers a free soup: slow and useless, and the story distorts as it travels up. Under decentralisation the waiter replaces the soup at once, because that's where the information is. But pure decentralisation invites abuse. The elegant answer is to **control the premises of the decision, not the decision itself**: every waiter may give away five soups a month, no questions asked, and anything beyond that is reviewed. Decentralised and controlled at once.

</div>

**Formalisation** ranges from near-total (the control room of an ISRO moon mission) to none at all (developing a film concept).

**Boundary spanning** is mostly about the edge between the organisation and its environment: HR reads the hiring market, sales reads demand, top management reads everything. Citing Karl Weick's idea of *enactment*, Prof. Jha stressed that there is no single objective environment. People see what their training and interests let them see: a finance person reads finance signals, a marketer reads market signals, and the same monsoon forecast produces opposite strategies in two firms. "The environment lies in the eyes of the beholder."

## Eight common structures

| Structure | Strengths | Weaknesses | Example |
|---|---|---|---|
| **Simple** | Fast, flexible, clear accountability | Can't scale; everything depends on one person | A kirana store |
| **Bureaucracy** | Efficient for high-volume routine work; consistent | Rigid; rule-following crowds out problem-solving | Indian Railways |
| **Functional** | Deep expertise; clear career paths | Silos; poor coordination across functions | Traditional manufacturing |
| **Divisional** | Each division focuses on its market; clear accountability | Duplicated resources; divisions compete | Tata, Reliance |
| **Matrix** | Shares scarce specialists; cross-functional expertise | Two bosses: breaks unity of command and creates role conflict | Consulting, aerospace |
| **Virtual** | Small core, minimal overhead; buys best-in-class partners | Loss of control; culture is hard to build | A brand that outsources manufacturing |
| **Team** | Breaks silos; high ownership | High coordination cost without shared direction | Mission and product teams |
| **Circular** | Signals equality and openness | Unclear authority; confusing to hierarchical thinkers | Some NGOs |

<div class="callout trap">
<p class="callout-title">The matrix's built-in conflict</p>

The matrix deliberately gives people two bosses: a functional manager and a project manager. When they want different things from the same person, that is the role conflict from Session 2, now built into the organisation chart. A matrix works only with strong psychological safety and clear rules for resolving conflicts.

</div>

<div class="callout beyond">
<p class="callout-title">How you employ people is a structural choice</p>

Prof. Jha took virtual structures past the textbook. **Casualisation**, where firms want "switch on, switch off" labour rather than permanent staff, is the structural root of the gig economy. Its human cost is **precarity**: insecure work leads people to postpone families and life decisions. His reading suggestion was Michael Sandel's *What Money Can't Buy*.

</div>

## Mechanistic versus organic

The eight structures sit along a spectrum between two ideal types.

| | Mechanistic | Organic |
|---|---|---|
| Specialisation | High; narrow roles | Low; overlapping, cross-functional |
| Authority | Rigid hierarchy | Follows expertise, not position |
| Span of control | Narrow; close supervision | Wide; self-direction expected |
| Formalisation | High; explicit rules | Low; method left to judgement |
| Decisions | Centralised | Decentralised, participative |
| Information | Top-down, filtered | Flows in every direction |
| Adapting to change | Slow | Fast |
| Best for | Stable environments, cost control, routine work | Dynamic environments, innovation, novel work |

A traditional joint family, where elders decide and roles are fixed, is mechanistic. A nuclear family with two working partners who debate every decision is organic. Neither is better; one is built for stability, the other for change.

### Structure shapes behaviour

| Feature | Effect on people |
|---|---|
| High specialisation | Short-term efficiency; long-term boredom and absence once variety drops too low, whatever the pay |
| Narrow span | Can feel suffocating to skilled staff, but helps learning on complex, high-stakes work |
| High formalisation | Fewer errors on routine work; less initiative when something new happens |
| Decentralisation | Higher satisfaction and commitment, when people feel able to handle the responsibility |
| Individual fit | People with a high need for clarity (need-for-clarity) thrive in mechanistic structures; people who need autonomy in organic ones |

**Downsizing**, the permanent, deliberate reduction of the workforce, belongs here too. Done as a redesign of the work, it brings lasting speed. Done as a payroll cut without changing the work, it produces **survivor syndrome**: the remaining staff carry the load, morale falls and knowledge walks out of the door. The test is simple: did we redesign the work, or just remove the people?

## The cases

### The Restaurant: structure evolving in real time

A diner grows through four stages, and its owner works through the seven dials without knowing their names.

<Timeline items={[
  { title: 'Stage 1', when: 'Simple structure', body: 'Three people do everything. Task variety keeps everyone engaged.' },
  { title: 'Stage 2', when: 'Specialisation', body: 'Cooks, dishwashers and waiters appear, and with them the first coordination problem. Everything still depends on the owner being there.' },
  { title: 'Stage 3', when: 'First management layer', body: 'The owner’s span is exceeded. Supervisors are added, and he becomes a manager of managers.' },
  { title: 'Stage 4', when: 'Second layer', body: 'A service pantry adds complexity, and the dilemma: how to gain the benefits of standardisation without losing the warmth that made the place special?' },
]} />

The sensible design is a functional structure with:

- clear roles, and spans of no more than about 8–10;
- unity of command;
- an operations manager as the coordination bridge;
- written procedures where precision matters (portions, cost), and discretion where warmth matters (guests);
- a training coordinator who turns the owner's personal standards into something teachable.

It brings its own risks: bureaucracy creep, silos and regulars missing the owner.

<div class="callout">
<p class="callout-title">Three routes to consistency</p>

The owner's real choice is how to make people behave consistently. Prof. Jha offered three routes:

1. **Formalisation:** written rules. Powerful but brittle: even a thousand-page manual can't foresee every exception.
2. **Socialisation:** teach the philosophy, then trust judgement. Founders meeting every recruit (as at MindTree), a new joiner receiving the founder's book (as at Four Seasons), or the Udupi restaurant tradition, consistent through shared culture rather than manuals.
3. **Professional socialisation:** the profession shapes behaviour. A chartered accountant checks everything because that is what accountants are trained to do.

For a restaurant built on warmth, socialisation preserves more than rules.

</div>

Rules also accumulate. **Structural inertia** means a structure resists change: the world shifts, rules are written to catch up, the world shifts again, and the old rules stay, because adding a rule is easier than removing one. Hold that thought for Session 4.

<div class="callout beyond">
<p class="callout-title">Why trusted work needs fewer supervisors</p>

The self-managed team movement began in 1960s Scandinavia, where a more democratic society asked: citizens make huge decisions at home, so why treat them as incapable at work? Three ideas explain why some organisations manage with far less supervision:

- **McGregor's Theory X and Theory Y.** Theory X assumes people dislike work and must be controlled. Theory Y assumes people will work hard if the work is fulfilling, so poor effort signals a badly designed job, not a lazy worker.
- **Toyota's jidoka.** Any operator can pull the andon cord and stop the whole line on spotting a defect: authority pushed to the front line.
- **Ouchi's Theory Z.** Long-term employment, collective decisions and strong shared values, as in post-war Japanese firms, with the value order the professor described (country first, company second, family third, individual last), let people work without being watched.

The common thread: where people are trusted and the work is meaningful, you need less hierarchy.

</div>

**"Weaponised incompetence."** A lighter but real point: people protect their turf by pretending they can't do a task, so that nobody asks them to. (In Bombay Hindi: *yeda banke peda khana*.) The serious counter-point: skills aren't inborn. With training and exposure most people can pick up most things, which is why the Stage 1 restaurant, where everyone did everything, worked.

### Acme and Omega: structure is a choice

Two electronics firms with the same parent, product, market and city, and opposite structures.

| | Acme | Omega |
|---|---|---|
| Type | Mechanistic | Organic |
| Specialisation | High; narrow roles | Low; fluid roles |
| Formalisation | High; memos and documented procedures | Very low; no org charts, no memos |
| Decisions | The general manager decides | A management team decides together |
| Profit today | Higher; wins more contracts | Lower, but more adaptable |

Then a new technology threatened both firms' old business, and a photocopier maker that needed 100 prototype units fast deliberately split the trial order between the two firms: two suppliers spread the risk and compete on price. Both hit the same problems.

| | Acme | Omega |
|---|---|---|
| How problems were handled | Every issue went to the general manager by memo, bounced between departments, and set off a turf war resolved only by escalation | Department heads met in one room and decided together; engineering suggested a Japanese substitute part (at Acme that would have been "not your department"); the team caught a design error because everyone owned the whole product |
| Delivery | Late: 18–21 days against a 14-day target | On time: 11 days |
| Quality | 10 defective units. People knew a part would fail but stayed in their lanes | Zero defects |

Same problems, opposite handling. The structure decided the outcome.

<div class="callout trap">
<p class="callout-title">Don't crown Acme for being more profitable</p>

Acme's mechanistic design is a perfect fit for a stable, high-volume market. Change the context (a new technology, a supply shock, a custom order) and Omega's flexibility becomes the decisive advantage. Always ask: profitable in what environment?

</div>

**Lonely at the top.** Centralisation also makes the boss the only person who sees the whole product, so everyone expects him to know everything. Prof. Jha's image: in the oldest surviving hierarchy, the Roman Catholic Church, the qualities attributed to God (all-knowing, all-powerful, everywhere) quietly transfer to the hierarchical superior. His parable of a graduate in a tiger skin made the same point: everyone is pretending to be a tiger. No real person is like that, which is why "competent authority" is often a polite fiction and over-centralised structures are fragile. He warned too against the Great Man view of management: the general manager personally testing one unit and declaring the whole batch fine. He also drew a useful line between **delegation** (prepare people, give resources, praise and correct) and **relegation** (just offloading work onto someone else).

## Exploration, exploitation and ambidexterity

This is the idea the whole session built towards.

<Fig title="Two capabilities, two structures">
  <Quad cols={['Mechanistic structure', 'Organic structure']} rows={['Exploitation', 'Exploration']} cells={[

    { t: 'Poor fit', b: 'Flexibility costs money you don’t need to spend on routine work.' },
    { t: 'Poor fit', b: 'A machine that executes but can’t think: new ideas die in the hierarchy.' },
    { tag: 'Omega', t: 'Good fit', b: 'Invent and do new things. Suits differentiation and innovation.', tone: 'best' },
  ]} />
</Fig>

Historically, firms chose one. Then Toyota's cuts to changeover time and its just-in-time system made **mass customisation** possible: variety *and* low cost. Today firms are expected to be **ambidextrous**: to exploit and explore at the same time.

Acme is a well-oiled machine that executes but can't think; Omega is a living organism that thinks but costs more.

Knowing you need both doesn't make it happen. Where do you put your best people? Logic says exploration, the future. But career paths, rewards and current revenue all sit in exploitation, so the best people and the money flow there and exploration starves. Nor can you simply merge an Acme with an Omega: Acme people feel rudderless under Omega's style, and Omega people feel suffocated under Acme's. Many mergers fail on exactly this kind of cultural mismatch, which is why the combined company's value so often falls despite complementary skills on paper.

<div class="callout beyond">
<p class="callout-title">Beyond the notes: how firms make ambidexterity work</p>

Research describes two common approaches. **Structural ambidexterity** (O'Reilly and Tushman) separates exploratory units from the core business, with their own processes and culture, linked only at the top by a leadership team that protects their budgets. **Contextual ambidexterity** (Gibson and Birkinshaw) builds a culture in which individuals divide their own time between improving today's work and exploring new ideas. Either way, exploration needs ring-fenced money and senior protection, or the core business will absorb it.

</div>

<div class="callout">
<p class="callout-title">The investor-versus-employee paradox</p>

Asked where they'd rather work, almost everyone in class picked Omega. Asked where they'd invest a prize of a lakh, most picked Acme. The professor used a journalist's thought experiment about a discount retailer (would you pay a dollar more so its employees are treated better? most won't) and the story of a famously employee-friendly Indian technology firm, with self-approved leave and unlimited sick leave, whose generosity eroded over time. When our preferences as employees and as investors point in opposite directions, the investor usually wins, so the world drifts towards Acme, and work-life balance erodes. A structural choice with a social consequence.

</div>

Structure is strategy made visible. Every reporting line, every rule, every decision about who talks to whom is a leadership choice. It decides what the organisation can do, how fast it can move, and what kind of people will want to work for you.

<div class="callout">
<p class="callout-title">Where this leads</p>

Acme's tidy structure was perfect for a stable market. But the things that make a structure efficient (fixed roles, settled rules, a comfortable way of working) are exactly what make it resist change when the world shifts. The structure built in this session becomes the thing that fights you in the next.

</div>
