## Why change is so hard

The first three sessions described the person, the group and the structure as if they stood still. They don't. Markets move, technology shifts, people leave, and yesterday's well-fitting structure becomes today's cage. Every earlier idea returns here: motivation (why people cling to the old way), groups (why teams hold each other in place) and structure (why the organisation's own machinery resists).

Organisations rarely fail because nobody saw the change coming. Seeing it and acting on it are two different problems: the first is analysis, the second is people.

A shop owner's son installs a billing app: faster bills, automatic stock alerts, clean GST records. For months the owner keeps the notebook going on the side, calls the app "too slow" and bills regulars the old way when his son isn't looking. His intelligence isn't the issue. What's under threat is his habit, his sense of mastery and his identity as the person who knows the shop best.

<div class="callout">
<p class="callout-title">The core idea</p>

Change management is not about convincing people the new thing is better. It is about **managing the loss of the old thing**: habit, status, competence, relationships. The shop owner doesn't doubt the app; he mourns the notebook.

</div>

**Planned change** is intentional and goal-oriented. It has two aims: to improve the organisation's ability to adapt, and to change how employees actually behave. An organisation has only changed when its people do things differently on Monday morning; a new strategy document changes nothing.

**Change agents** are the people who act as catalysts and take responsibility for a change, whether managers or not, insiders or outsiders. Boards often hire outsiders for big transformations precisely because outsiders have no old loyalties to protect.

## Why people resist, and why that's useful

Some resistance is healthy. It brings stability, sparks real debate and often exposes genuine flaws in the plan. It becomes dangerous when it goes underground.

<Cards min={240} items={[
  { title: 'Loud resistance', body: 'Complaints and pushback in the meeting. Visible, so you can engage with it.', pill: 'Manageable', pillOk: true },
  { title: 'Silent resistance', body: 'Loyalty drops, effort fades, errors and absences creep up. Months later one small change becomes the last straw.', pill: 'The killer' },
]} />

A new process is announced at a townhall. No hard questions, plenty of nodding. Three months later adoption is near zero, workarounds are everywhere, and two good people are interviewing elsewhere. Nobody objected, because the objection went underground on day one. **The nodding was the resistance.**

### Ten sources of resistance

| Inside people | | Inside the system | |
|---|---|---|---|
| **Habit** | Changing a routine takes conscious effort, so the mind votes for the old way | **Structural inertia** | The checks and procedures that create stability defend the old way like antibodies |
| **Security** | "Will I still matter here?" | **Limited focus of change** | Parts of an organisation are connected like a baby's mobile; change one and the rest pull it back |
| **Economic fear** | "I'm good at the old way and my pay depends on it" | **Group inertia** | Even someone who privately agrees is held in place by the group's norms |
| **Fear of the unknown** | People prefer a familiar problem to an unfamiliar promise | **Threat to expertise** | A new skill devalues masters of the old one, so your strongest people may resist hardest |
| **Selective hearing** | We filter out information that threatens the world we've built | **Threat to power** | Whoever controls a decision today resists any change that moves it. The fight is about who decides |

<div class="callout">
<p class="callout-title">Manager takeaway: write two lists before you announce</p>

Before announcing any change, list **who loses a habit** and **who loses power or status**. The first group needs training and time. The second needs a conversation *before* the announcement, not after. And treat silence in the room as your most important data point.

</div>

## Winning people over: eight tactics

The top five build change *with* people; the bottom three do change *to* people. The lower you go, the faster it works, and the more it poisons every future change.

A few catches worth knowing:

- **Communication** only fixes resistance that comes from misunderstanding. If people understand and still lose something, talking more won't help.
- **Participation** is slower, and you must genuinely be willing to let the plan change. Fake participation is worse than none.
- **Trust is a bank account** filled in normal times. A change is a large withdrawal, so the account must be full before announcement day.
- **Fairness** is fragile: one visible exception undoes it.

<div class="callout">
<p class="callout-title">Honest cooptation exists</p>

Giving your loudest credible critic a real design role, with actual power to change the plan, is participation. Giving them a token seat is manipulation. The difference is whether you are willing to be changed by them.

</div>

### Expert or process consultation?

Edgar Schein distinguished two ways to help an organisation. In **expert mode**, the consultant arrives with the answer, which invites everyone to prove them wrong ("you don't understand our clients"). In **process consultation**, the consultant helps the organisation ask itself the right questions, on the basis that the people inside know their work best. Expert mode fits only when the outsider genuinely holds knowledge the insiders lack, such as entering a brand-new market.

## Lewin: unfreeze, move, refreeze

Kurt Lewin, a German-Jewish social psychologist who fled the Nazis, spent his career on how to change attitudes. His model says change succeeds in three steps.

<Flow items={[
  { t: 'Unfreeze', s: 'Create the felt need for change. Kotter calls it a “burning platform”.' },
  { t: 'Move', s: 'Shift to the new way of working.' },
  { t: 'Refreeze', s: 'Lock it in with systems, rewards and habits so it sticks.', hl: true },
]} />

You can't reshape an ice cube by pushing it: melt it, pour it into the new mould, freeze it, or it slides back into the puddle of old habits. A New Year resolution shows the pattern. The doctor's report unfreezes, joining the gym is the move, and refreezing (a fixed slot, a gym partner) is the step most people skip, which is why it's over by March.

### The force field: release the brake

Lewin's second idea: any status quo is held in place by opposing forces, like a car with the accelerator and brake pressed at the same time.

<Fig title="Lewin's force field" caption="Most managers press the accelerator harder (more targets, more emails). The cheaper, smarter move is usually to release the brake.">
  <ForceField driving={['New technology', 'Competitor pressure', 'Customer demands', 'Leadership targets']} restraining={['Past success: “why fix what isn’t broken?”', 'Fear of losing status or competence', 'Not enough capacity or skills', 'Unclear benefits']} />
</Fig>

The strongest restraining force is often **past success**: "why fix what isn't broken?" is loudest in winning companies.

Satya Nadella's turnaround of Microsoft is a textbook walk-through: honest urgency, a rebuilt leadership coalition, a clear "cloud first, mobile first" vision, scrapping the stack-ranking appraisal system that punished collaboration, publicising early cloud wins, and a decade of anchoring a "growth mindset" culture.

### Action research: let the data choose the solution

Planned change run like the scientific method.

## Change-ready organisations

The best organisations treat change as a capacity, not an event.

- **Managing paradox.** Leaders hold tensions that never fully resolve: build on the past while letting go of it; empower people while keeping order. Like riding a bicycle, balance only exists with forward movement.
- **Stimulating innovation** needs organic structures, rewards for innovation, slack resources to absorb failure, plenty of communication across boundaries, and **idea champions** who push an idea through resistance.
- **The learning organisation** keeps adapting: a shared vision, willingness to drop old routines, systems thinking, open communication and personal agendas set aside for the common goal.

A change-ready organisation keeps pockets of Omega alive inside the Acme machine: a loose, exploratory team running alongside the disciplined, process-driven one.

## Stress: change's shadow

Change is one of the biggest sources of stress at work, which is why the textbook pairs them. Japan even has a word, *karoshi*, for death from overwork.

**Stress** is what you feel when you face a demand or opportunity whose outcome is both **uncertain** and **important**. It lives in the gap between **demands** (deadlines, reviews) and **resources** (skills, support, control). Stressors add up, which is why a trivial request can trigger an outsized reaction.

<Cards min={240} items={[
  { title: 'Challenge stressors', body: 'Workload and time pressure on meaningful work. They push you towards goals and can energise.', pill: 'Keep', pillOk: true },
  { title: 'Hindrance stressors', body: 'Red tape, office politics, unclear responsibilities. They block you from goals and only corrode.', pill: 'Remove' },
]} />

Two teams face the same release date. Team A is stretched but energised: the goal matters, ownership is clear, blockers are cleared within hours. Team B is exhausted: nobody is sure who owns what, three approvals are pending, and a turf war eats every stand-up. Same deadline; different leadership.

| Source | What's in it |
|---|---|
| **Environmental** | Economic, political and technological uncertainty |
| **Organisational** | Task demands (design, deadlines, no autonomy), role demands (conflict, overload, ambiguity) and interpersonal demands (no support, an abusive boss). The part a leader can fix |
| **Personal** | Family and money worries that walk in every morning |

Stress shows up in the body (headaches, blood pressure), the mind (anxiety, irritability) and behaviour (falling output, rising absence and attrition). **The behavioural signs are a manager's dashboard**: you can't see blood pressure, but you can see absences creeping up. The popular inverted-U idea, that moderate stress improves performance, is only weakly supported, so use it with care. **Social support** is the best-documented buffer in the stress research.

**Managing stress** happens at two levels. Individuals can manage time, exercise, practise relaxation and lean on social support. Organisations can select and train for high-stress roles, set clear goals (clarity itself reduces stress), redesign jobs for more autonomy, involve employees, communicate regularly, offer sabbaticals, and run wellness programmes, which work only when leaders visibly take part.

<div class="callout">
<p class="callout-title">Manager takeaway: audit stress like a P&L</p>

List your team's top five stressors and mark each C (challenge) or H (hindrance). Protect the Cs and remove the Hs. Most hindrances are within your own signature: unclear ownership, duplicate reporting, approval loops. Role ambiguity is the cheapest stressor to fix and the one managers create most often.

</div>

## The case: Jess Westerly at Kauflauf

Jess Westerly, a young American outsider, was hired by Kauflauf, a German software firm with a flat, relationship-driven culture, to push growth. Its salaried field consultants ranked money last among their motivators. Her analysis was excellent: consultants spent only about a third of their time on the customers who produced most of the revenue, and shifting some of that time to large clients would lift revenue sharply. Three months in, she emailed the whole field force a directive memo. It failed completely, and every reason maps to this session.

| Concept | How it appears in the case |
|---|---|
| Change agent as outsider | New and with nothing invested: the perfect profile to start a change, and no political capital when resistance came |
| No unfreezing | She went straight to "move". A memo changes information, not behaviour |
| Skipped Kotter 1–4 | No urgency (the firm was winning), no coalition (the sales head learnt of it from a cc), no shared vision, one-way communication |
| Threat to power | Call patterns had been the consultants' own decision; an outsider simply took it |
| Threat to identity | Many consultants had joined precisely to avoid big-account selling |
| Limited focus of change | Development capacity couldn't support more large-client work, so the system snapped back |
| Silent resistance | Her boss was more worried by the consultants who never replied than by those who complained |
| Expert mode | She arrived with the answer and invited everyone to prove her wrong |
| Ignoring context | She assumed a redesign that worked at her last company would work here |
| Wrong messenger | The directive went out under her own name, so she absorbed all the blame |

<div class="callout">
<p class="callout-title">Two paradoxes of timing</p>

**Outsider versus insider.** An outsider sees the need for change more readily; an insider knows better what will actually work. Pair outside diagnosis with insider knowledge.

**Winning versus losing.** When a firm is doing well, its *ability* to change is high but the *felt need* is low. When it is doing badly, the need is obvious but the ability collapses, and each failed fix feeds a downward spiral of blame. The sweet spot is to change while still strong, which is exactly why unfreezing a winning firm is so hard.

</div>

**What she should do next:** unfreeze by presenting her data as a shared problem at a company meeting; build a coalition (her boss as co-sponsor, regional heads as co-owners, respected big-account consultants as champions); rebuild her standing by spending time in the field; fix the system the consultants themselves flagged; flex targets by territory; pilot with volunteers to get early wins; and refreeze through territory plans, reviews and onboarding, recognising success with pride and stories rather than cash. The one thing she must not do is send another mass directive.
