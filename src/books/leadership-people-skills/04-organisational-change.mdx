## Why change is so hard

The first three sessions described the person, the group and the structure as if they stood still. They don't. Markets move, technology shifts, people leave, and yesterday's well-fitting structure becomes today's cage. Every earlier idea returns here: motivation (why people cling to the old way), groups (why teams hold each other in place) and structure (why the organisation's own machinery resists).

Organisations rarely fail because nobody saw the change coming. Seeing it and acting on it are two different problems: the first is analysis, the second is people.

A kirana shop owner's son installs a billing app: faster bills, automatic stock alerts, clean GST records. For months the owner keeps the notebook going on the side, calls the app "too slow" and bills regulars the old way when his son isn't looking. His intelligence isn't the issue. What's under threat is his habit, his sense of mastery and his identity as the person who knows the shop best.

<div class="callout">
<p class="callout-title">The core idea</p>

Change management is not about convincing people the new thing is better. It is about **managing the loss of the old thing**: habit, status, competence, relationships. The shop owner doesn't doubt the app; he mourns the notebook.

</div>

**Planned change** is intentional and goal-oriented. It has two aims: to improve the organisation's ability to adapt, and to change how employees actually behave. An organisation has only changed when its people do things differently on Monday morning; a new strategy document changes nothing.

**Change agents** are the people who act as catalysts and take responsibility for a change, whether managers or not, insiders or outsiders. Boards often hire outsiders for big transformations precisely because outsiders have no old loyalties to protect.

Run your unit through the six forces once a quarter. Leaders who name the rising water early get to do planned change. Those who don't end up doing damage control, with the same effort, six months later.

## Why people resist, and why that's useful

Some resistance is healthy. It brings stability, sparks real debate and often exposes genuine flaws in the plan. It becomes dangerous when it goes underground.

<Cards min={240} items={[
  { title: 'Loud resistance', body: 'Complaints and pushback in the meeting. Visible, so you can engage with it.', pill: 'Manageable', pillOk: true },
  { title: 'Silent resistance', body: 'Loyalty drops, effort fades, errors and absences creep up. Months later one small change becomes the last straw.', pill: 'The killer' },
]} />

A new process is announced at a townhall. No hard questions, plenty of nodding. Three months later adoption is near zero, workarounds are everywhere, and two good people are interviewing elsewhere. Nobody objected, because the objection went underground on day one. **The nodding was the resistance.**

### Ten sources of resistance

| Inside people | | Inside the system | |
|---|---|---|---|
| **Habit** | Changing a routine takes conscious effort, so the mind votes for the old way | **Structural inertia** | The checks and procedures that create stability defend the old way like antibodies |
| **Security** | "Will I still matter here?" | **Limited focus of change** | Parts of an organisation are connected like a baby's mobile; change one and the rest pull it back |
| **Economic fear** | "I'm good at the old way and my pay depends on it" | **Group inertia** | Even someone who privately agrees is held in place by the group's norms |
| **Fear of the unknown** | People prefer a familiar problem to an unfamiliar promise | **Threat to expertise** | A new skill devalues masters of the old one, so your strongest people may resist hardest |
| **Selective hearing** | We filter out information that threatens the world we've built | **Threat to power** | Whoever controls a decision today resists any change that moves it. The fight is about who decides |

Watch any housing society's annual general meeting and you see the politics of change in miniature: the loudest defenders of "how we've always done it" are the long-serving committee members whose influence flows from the existing arrangements. The more mechanistic the structure, the heavier its inertia. And your senior experts in the old technology may resist the new one hardest.

<div class="callout">
<p class="callout-title">Manager takeaway: write two lists before you announce</p>

Before announcing any change, list **who loses a habit** and **who loses power or status**. The first group needs training and time. The second needs a conversation *before* the announcement, not after. And treat silence in the room as your most important data point.

</div>

## Winning people over: eight tactics

The top five build change *with* people; the bottom three do change *to* people. The lower you go, the faster it works, and the more it poisons every future change.

<Fig title="Eight tactics for overcoming resistance">
  <Ladder />
</Fig>

A few catches worth knowing:

- **Communication** only fixes resistance that comes from misunderstanding. If people understand and still lose something, talking more won't help.
- **Participation** works because it is hard to fight a decision you helped make (Toyota's kaizen is the model). It is slower, and you must genuinely be willing to let the plan change. Fake participation is worse than none.
- **Support and commitment:** counselling and training turn "I can't" into "I can". Run the training before go-live.
- **Trust is a bank account** filled in normal times. A change is a large withdrawal, so the account must be full before announcement day.
- **Implementing fairly** means the same rules for all, reasons explained, and leaders taking the first cuts. Fairness is fragile: one visible exception undoes it.
- **Manipulation and cooptation** blow up the moment people realise they were played; credibility drops to zero for good.
- **Selecting people who accept change** does nothing for the rollout you are struggling with this quarter.
- **Coercion** ("accept this or face a transfer") is the fastest tactic with the highest interest later: compliance on the surface, disengagement underneath, and people remember.

<div class="callout">
<p class="callout-title">Honest cooptation exists</p>

Giving your loudest credible critic a real design role, with actual power to change the plan, is participation. Giving them a token seat is manipulation. The difference is whether you are willing to be changed by them.

</div>

### Expert or process consultation?

Edgar Schein distinguished two ways to help an organisation. In **expert mode**, the consultant arrives with the answer, which invites everyone to prove them wrong ("you don't understand our clients"). In **process consultation**, the consultant helps the organisation ask itself the right questions, on the basis that the people inside know their work best. Expert mode fits only when the outsider genuinely holds knowledge the insiders lack, such as entering a brand-new market.

In the case, had the newcomer framed it as "we're doing well; how do we reach the next level?" and let respected consultants who had served large clients make the argument, it would have stopped being an us-against-them fight.

## Lewin: unfreeze, move, refreeze

Kurt Lewin, a German-Jewish social psychologist who fled the Nazis, spent his career on how to change attitudes towards race and prejudice, a question as urgent now as then. His model says change succeeds in three steps.

<Flow items={[
  { t: 'Unfreeze', s: 'Create the felt need for change. Kotter calls it a “burning platform”.' },
  { t: 'Move', s: 'Shift to the new way of working.' },
  { t: 'Refreeze', s: 'Lock it in with systems, rewards and habits so it sticks.', hl: true },
]} />

You can't reshape an ice cube by pushing it: melt it, pour it into the new mould, freeze it, or it slides back into the puddle of old habits. A New Year resolution shows the pattern. The doctor's report unfreezes, joining the gym is the move, and refreezing (a fixed slot, a gym partner) is the step most people skip, which is why it's over by March.

No change moves until someone makes standing still feel unsafe. Kotter's readable little book on this is *Our Iceberg Is Melting*. A trap for technical experts: they assume what is obvious to them is obvious to all. They think "2 + 2 is always 4", but in organisations 2 + 2 is "how much do you want it to be?"

### The force field: release the brake

Lewin's second idea, the force-field: any status quo is held in place by opposing forces, like a car with the accelerator and brake pressed at the same time.

<Fig title="Lewin's force field" caption="Most managers press the accelerator harder (more targets, more emails). The cheaper, smarter move is usually to release the brake.">
  <ForceField driving={['New technology', 'Competitor pressure', 'Customer demands', 'Leadership targets']} restraining={['Past success: “why fix what isn’t broken?”', 'Fear of losing status or competence', 'Not enough capacity or skills', 'Unclear benefits']} />

The strongest restraining force is often **past success**: "why fix what isn't broken?" is loudest in winning companies.

### Kotter: Lewin with a checklist

There are as many change models as there are consultants, Prof. Jha said, everyone invents a branded model to charge for, and nearly all are reworkings of Lewin: steps 1 to 4 unfreeze, 5 to 7 move, 8 refreezes. Learn Lewin well and you can decode any model you meet. Kotter's eight steps are the best known.

<Fig title="Kotter's eight steps, mapped to Lewin" caption="More than half the work happens before anything visibly changes. Most failed change efforts skipped steps 1–4, not 5–8.">

Satya Nadella's turnaround of Microsoft is a textbook walk-through: honest urgency, a rebuilt leadership coalition, a clear "cloud first, mobile first" vision, scrapping the stack-ranking appraisal system that punished collaboration, publicising early cloud wins, and a decade of anchoring a "growth mindset" culture.

## Change-ready organisations

The best organisations treat change as a capacity, not an event.

- **Managing paradox.** Leaders hold tensions that never fully resolve: build on the past while letting go of it; empower people while keeping order. Like riding a bicycle, balance only exists with forward movement.
- **Stimulating innovation** needs organic structures, rewards for innovation, slack resources to absorb failure, plenty of communication across boundaries, and **idea champions** who push an idea through resistance.
- **The learning organisation** keeps adapting: a shared vision, willingness to drop old routines, systems thinking, open communication and personal agendas set aside for the common goal.

A change-ready organisation keeps pockets of Omega alive inside the Acme machine: a loose, exploratory team running alongside the disciplined, process-driven one.

## Stress: change's shadow

Change is one of the biggest sources of stress at work, which is why the textbook pairs them. Japan even has a word, *karoshi*, for death from overwork: the textbook tells of a young telecom employee who worked until the last train, slept at his desk when he missed it, once worked 37 hours straight, and was dead within a few years. Stress isn't a poster topic for HR. It is a leadership responsibility. (This part of the chapter wasn't taught in class; it is here for completeness.)

**Stress** is what you feel when you face a demand or opportunity whose outcome is both **uncertain** and **important**. It lives in the gap between **demands** (deadlines, reviews) and **resources** (skills, support, control). High demands with high resources feel like a challenge; high demands with low resources feel like drowning. Stressors are additive: they add up, which is why a trivial request can trigger an outsized reaction.

<Cards min={240} items={[
  { title: 'Challenge stressors', body: 'Workload and time pressure on meaningful work. They push you towards goals and can energise.', pill: 'Keep', pillOk: true },
  { title: 'Hindrance stressors', body: 'Red tape, office politics, unclear responsibilities. They block you from goals and only corrode.', pill: 'Remove' },
]} />

Two teams face the same release date. Team A is stretched but energised: the goal matters, ownership is clear, blockers are cleared within hours. Team B is exhausted: nobody is sure who owns what, three approvals are pending, and a turf war eats every stand-up. Same deadline; different leadership.

| Source | What's in it |
|---|---|
| **Environmental** | Economic, political and technological uncertainty |
| **Organisational** | Task demands (design, deadlines, no autonomy), role demands (conflict, overload, ambiguity) and interpersonal demands (no support, an abusive boss). The part a leader can fix. Role ambiguity is the most underrated stressor. One study found that simply being treated unfairly raised people's cortisol: unfair treatment is a physiological event, not just bad manners |
| **Personal** | Family and money worries that walk in every morning. Even high earners report money trouble |

Stress shows up in the body (headaches, blood pressure), the mind (anxiety, irritability) and behaviour (falling output, rising absence and attrition). **The behavioural signs are a manager's dashboard**: you can't see blood pressure, but you can see absences creeping up. The popular inverted-U idea, that moderate stress improves performance, is only weakly supported, so use it with care. A related idea is *allostasis*: we stay stable by continually adjusting, and the cumulative wear is the allostatic load.

Individual differences change everything: perception (the same layoff terrifies one person and frees another), experience, personality, and **social support**, the best-documented buffer in the stress research. Culture shapes it too. One study found US employees stressed by lack of control while Chinese employees were stressed by job evaluations and lack of training. In collectivist cultures extra hours can read as a sacrifice for the family; in individualist ones, as time taken from it.

**Managing stress** happens at two levels. Individuals can manage time, take non-competitive exercise, practise relaxation (even 20 minutes makes a measurable difference) and lean on social support. Organisations can select and train for high-stress roles, set clear goals (clarity itself reduces stress), redesign jobs for more autonomy, involve employees, communicate regularly, offer sabbaticals (several large Indian IT firms offer extended voluntary leave, which helps most when the person fully switches off), and run wellness programmes. At one technology centre in Bangalore, employees credit daily yoga; a review of 36 wellness programmes found real reductions in stress. They work only when leaders visibly take part, not when they just approve the budget.

<div class="callout">
<p class="callout-title">Manager takeaway: audit stress like a P&L</p>

List your team's top five stressors and mark each C (challenge) or H (hindrance). Protect the Cs and remove the Hs. Most hindrances are within your own signature: unclear ownership, duplicate reporting, approval loops. Role ambiguity is the cheapest stressor to fix and the one managers create most often.

</div>

## The case: Jess Westerly at Kauflauf

Jess Westerly, a young American outsider, was hired by Kauflauf, a German software firm with a flat, relationship-driven culture, to push growth. Its salaried field consultants ranked money last among their motivators. Her analysis was excellent: consultants spent only 35% of their time on the customers who produced 85% of the revenue, and shifting a fifth of their time from small to large clients would lift revenue by at least 30%. On 14 June 2011, three months in, she emailed the whole field force a directive memo. It failed completely, and every reason maps to this session.

| Concept | How it appears in the case |
|---|---|
| Change agent as outsider | New and with nothing invested: the perfect profile to start a change, and no political capital when resistance came |
| No unfreezing | She went straight to "move". A memo changes information, not behaviour |
| Skipped Kotter 1–4 | No urgency (the firm was winning), no coalition (the sales head learnt of it from a cc), no shared vision, one-way communication |
| Threat to power | Call patterns had been the consultants' own decision; an outsider simply took it |
| Threat to identity | Many consultants had joined precisely to avoid big-account selling |
| Limited focus of change | Development capacity couldn't support more large-client work, so the system snapped back |
| Silent resistance | Her boss was more worried by the consultants who never replied than by those who complained |
| Expert mode | She arrived with the answer and invited everyone to prove her wrong |
| Ignoring context | She assumed a redesign that worked at her last company would work here |
| Wrong messenger | The directive went out under her own name, so she absorbed all the blame |
| Action research skipped | Her diagnosis was superb, but she never shared the findings and built the plan with the field |
| Some resistance is free consulting | At least two replies were real warnings about capacity and the mix of territories, not wounded pride |
| Double minority | Not German, and a young woman in a field force that had been all male: an outsider on several counts, which raised the bar for credibility |
| Self-set and assigned goals | Imposing a target removed the consultants' ownership; goals people set themselves win far more commitment |

**Who even wanted this change?** The firm was winning in its niche, so it didn't feel it needed to change. The push came not from the market but from the "grown-ups": professional managers brought in to bring order to a founder-led culture where money wasn't the motivator. The professor likened it to outside money and managers entering a well-known Indian start-up. To the field consultants the directive read as "who is this newcomer imposing her last company's playbook on us?" Change imposed without a felt need reads as an outsider's vanity project. People reach for the tool they can use, not the one the situation needs.

<div class="callout">
<p class="callout-title">Two paradoxes of timing</p>

**Outsider versus insider.** An outsider sees the need for change more readily; an insider knows better what will actually work. Pair outside diagnosis with insider knowledge.

**Winning versus losing.** When a firm is doing well, its *ability* to change is high but the *felt need* is low. When it is doing badly, the need is obvious but the ability collapses, and each failed fix feeds a downward spiral of blame that can end in dissolution, as with the camera-film maker that survives mainly as a licensed brand name. The sweet spot is to change while still strong, which is exactly why unfreezing a winning firm is so hard.

</div>

**What she should do next:** unfreeze by presenting her data as a shared problem at a company meeting; build a coalition (her boss as co-sponsor, regional heads as co-owners, respected big-account consultants as champions); rebuild her standing by spending time in the field; fix the system the consultants themselves flagged (protected development capacity, fast qualification of leads, a playbook for big accounts); flex targets by territory; pilot with volunteers to get early wins; and refreeze through territory plans, reviews and onboarding, recognising success with pride and stories rather than cash. The one thing she must not do is send another mass directive, or reach for pay as the first lever.
