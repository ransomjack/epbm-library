## The homework: charge a fee, or pay a rebate?

Four months had passed since the campus sessions. The road ahead: this session on decision analysis, then two on statistics.

The assignment is the Pilgrim Bank data. Put yourself in a US bank in the early 2000s. There is almost no internet, laptops are rare and smartphones don't exist. Online banking is brand new, and the bank must choose between two deliberately extreme options: **charge customers a fee** for using it, or **pay them a rebate**.

Why would a bank ever pay you to use a service? Because some new services cut the bank's own cost. Many of us haven't walked into a branch for years, and the bank loves that: the branch, the tellers and the paper cost far more than a server. If online banking cuts cost, promote it, even pay for migration. But if customers keep visiting the branch anyway, online banking is an extra facility on top of the old cost, and the bank should recover it from those who use it. Two opposite logics. The data decides.

What the professor asked for: analyse the data and make up your mind either way. Using an AI assistant is fine; you would at work too. What matters is that you can support your recommendation, defend it, and show why it beats the other side. "Not just because my bot said so."

## What makes a decision good?

The professor asked a simple question and then spent an hour dismantling the class's first answer.

The board filled quickly: a decision is good if it produces a desirable outcome; if it accounts for the available courses of action; if it passes an ethical test; if it is effective; if it serves the bigger goal. Four of these are fine. The first is a trap.

**"How does it feel to be wrong?"** The chat filled with the obvious: disappointing, frustrating, bad. Then the twist.

> Being wrong feels exactly like being right. Because you do not know you are wrong.

Every feeling the class listed is the feeling of *finding out*. Until then, being wrong feels confident and settled. The moment you feel most sure isn't evidence that you are right.

### The outcome trap

When you decide, the outcome hasn't happened yet. You launch a product; the market reacts months later. Grade the decision by the outcome and you are grading it with information that wasn't available at the time. That is hindsight, and hindsight isn't analysis.

It is worse than unfair. **It promotes mediocrity.** When something unusual lands on your desk, the first instinct is to look for a precedent, because it is a safety net: if it fails, "we did what worked last time". We behave this way because organisations reward good outcomes and punish bad ones. So everyone plays safe, nobody reaches for the option that would have realised their potential, and the organisation settles in the middle. It doesn't fail, and it never wins big.

<div class="callout">
<p class="callout-title">The definition to carry with you</p>

A good decision is based on logic, considers all available data and possible alternatives, and is obtained through rational analysis of these data and alternatives.

</div>

| Test | Question to ask |
|---|---|
| All courses of action | Were all the alternatives considered, including the ones the decision-maker didn't like? |
| All possible outcomes | Were all the states of nature listed, not just the comfortable ones? |
| Rational use of data | Was all the available information used, or was inconvenient data quietly ignored? |

If a decision passes these three tests, it is a good decision whatever the outcome. A good decision can meet bad luck and lose money. A careless one can get lucky and look brilliant. It is still a bad decision. It just happened to work. Under certainty the link between decision and outcome is one-to-one, so judging by outcome is harmless. Under risk and uncertainty, the outcome tells you about luck.

### Alcoa: what rewarding the process looks like

Aluminium was once more precious than gold. Napoleon III served his most honoured guests on aluminium cutlery and the rest on gold and silver, because separating the metal from its ore was so difficult. The company that changed that was Alcoa.

In 1987 Alcoa hired a new chief executive, Paul O'Neill, who came from government, not industry. In those days a new chief would address the analysts, who would then run to the nearest phone booth to tell their firms to buy or sell. O'Neill's entire speech was about worker safety. The analysts ran to the phones and told everyone to sell. The stock fell before he had done anything at all.

He stayed until 2000. In the class's telling, annual profit went from about 200 million dollars to about 3 billion. What did safety have to do with it? American unions then resisted almost everything: sharing data, changing how work was done. But no union can refuse when management says "we are changing this process because the old one is hazardous to your members". Safety became the door through which every process was re-examined.

The mechanism was a rule: every accident had to reach him within a fixed number of hours, with a plan to ensure it never happened again. One occurrence is a diagnosis; a repeat is unacceptable. He fired a high-performing executive, not because an accident happened at his plant but because he tried to cover it up. The cover-up is the outcome mindset in action. Once it takes hold, people fudge the process, and then luck, not skill, decides your results.

<div class="callout">
<p class="callout-title">Audit the process</p>

Organisations that do well over the long run don't reward or punish the outcome of decisions made under risk, because chance played a part. They ask: did you look at all the data, all the options, all the outcomes? A robust process doesn't win every time. It wins more often than it loses.

</div>

### "But we do live by outcomes"

A batchmate pushed back: a cricket captain choosing the next bowler gets feedback in six balls, while someone who quits a job to start a business may learn only ten years later.

**Short feedback loops are noisy.** Six balls tell you very little. M.S. Dhoni failed in his first matches; Sourav Ganguly kept backing him because his judgement said this player had potential, and gave him enough chances for that judgement to be tested properly.

**Long feedback loops are mostly regret.** Ten years on, what you "know" about the road not taken is speculation. There is no parallel universe. What you can control is whether you decided well with what you knew.

### The power of writing

Everyone knows the endless argument in the head, with the boss, a subordinate or the household help, where you rehearse what they will say and win every round. In reality you rarely win, and you come out more frustrated. The professor's habit, at home as much as at work: **write it down.** Not pages. Two minutes, a few bullets.

It works for a reason. Because of a self-serving bias, you won't write something that makes you look bad even in your own eyes, so the pettiness falls away. What is left is the real issue. Then: write a problem statement (not a solution); empathise (why would this person behave this way?); and list the options you actually have. Sometimes you discover you have no other option, and that acceptance alone removes the stress.

And you see why you were postponing. Nobody postpones the big match at 1 a.m. on a work night. Everybody postpones the assignment due the next morning. Once you can see that gap on paper, you either do it or consciously decide not to. Either way it stops living in your head.

In your next appraisal or project review, ask "was the process sound with the information available at the time?" before "did it work?". Teams graded on outcomes learn to hide accidents. Teams graded on process learn to report them.

## Fall in love with the problem

What kind of people do we like at work? The ones who talk about solutions. The professor agreed, and then pointed at the danger inside that instinct.

Once we have built a prototype and spent weeks on it, we are emotionally invested. When early signs appear that the solution doesn't fit the problem, we don't drop the solution. **We quietly reshape the problem so that the solution fits.** On the slide the solution looks perfect. On the ground the problem is exactly what it always was.

For a routine problem this doesn't arise: a gear breaks, replace the gear. The trouble is the problem that keeps coming back, usually because you have been treating a symptom. For that, she borrowed the first pillar of **design thinking**: in the first steps you aren't allowed to talk about solutions at all. You look at the problem from every stakeholder's point of view, without judgement and without the hurry to fix anything.

### The firefighting test

What share of your day goes on routine, urgent, operational tasks, and what share on strategic work that will pay off later? If the split is 90 to 10, your vision keeps receding. If it is 50-50, you are in decent shape.

Firefighting isn't supposed to be daily. If fires come every day, one of four things is true: your processes are broken; they are right but badly implemented; your team isn't aligned; or you keep solving a symptom while the root cause sends the next fire.

<div class="callout">
<p class="callout-title">Cause or symptom?</p>

After you solve a problem, do you get a real period of calm, time you can spend on strategy? If yes, you probably treated the cause. If the next fire follows without a pause, you treated a symptom. The calm is the test.

</div>

### The problem that was not "blindness"

Daniel Kish was born with a cancer of the eye. His right eye was removed at seven months and his left at thirteen. His parents put his freedom first, so he moved out at eighteen, pays taxes and rides a mountain bike. Today he teaches blind children to ride bikes and play basketball.

How? **Echolocation**, the trick bats and dolphins use. He clicks his tongue and reads the echoes: a wall, a tree and a moving car each return the sound differently.

Before your team builds anything, make them write a problem statement with no solution hiding inside it. "We need a dashboard" is a solution. "Regional heads discover a stock-out three days after it happens" is a problem, and it may have five solutions, of which a dashboard is the most expensive.

## The six steps of decision making

<Steps items={[
  { title: 'Define the problem', body: 'Clearly, in writing. Spend the most time here. Root cause or symptom? “Solving the right problem roughly beats solving the wrong problem accurately.”' },
  { title: 'List all the alternatives', body: 'Every course of action under your control, including the ones you dislike. Let the process eliminate weak options, not your bias.' },
  { title: 'Identify the states of nature', body: 'What could follow, beyond your control: customer reaction very bad, mildly bad, neutral, favourable, very favourable.' },
  { title: 'Estimate the payoffs', body: 'For each alternative and state, the result on your chosen measure: profit, revenue, cost, market share. It need not be money.' },
  { title: 'Select the model', body: 'It depends on the environment: certainty, risk or uncertainty.' },
  { title: 'Apply it and decide', body: 'Mechanical. If the first four steps are honest, this takes minutes.' },
]} />

The first four are judgement, and that is where the time goes. The last two are mechanics.

**Why "all" alternatives: the rival's product.** Suppose the colleague you like least has proposed a new product, and you are asked to test-market it. You could test nationally, regionally or in one small town. Because it is your rival's idea, the national launch will probably never appear on your list. Now flip it: it is your own idea. The small-town test quietly disappears. Either way an alternative has been killed by bias, not analysis.

**The same skeleton on a shop counter.** A kirana owner has noticed footfall dropping since a quick-commerce store opened two streets away. *Problem:* regulars are shifting part of their basket to app delivery. *Alternatives:* start home delivery; list on a delivery platform; do nothing. *States of nature:* the neighbourhood takes to delivery heavily, moderately or hardly at all. *Payoff:* monthly profit under each combination. The whole structure fits on the back of a bill.

<div class="callout trap">
<p class="callout-title">Alternatives don't have probabilities</p>

"What is the probability of choosing the large plant?" is the wrong question. An alternative is your choice: you take it or you don't. Only states of nature carry probabilities.

</div>

## Environments, probability and gut feeling

| Environment | What you know | Tools | Example |
|---|---|---|---|
| Certainty | One outcome per action | Linear and integer programming | A one-year fixed deposit: compare rates |
| Uncertainty | Several outcomes, no probabilities: "shooting in the dark" | Maximax, maximin, regret | The first e-reader; a new pharma molecule that may not work, may have side effects, may not get approval |
| Risk | Several outcomes, with probability estimates | Expected value, decision trees | Thompson Lumber: a 50-50 market |

Where do the probabilities come from? Market research, experts, historical data, and experience and gut feeling.

**Most decisions start in uncertainty, and we convert them to risk** because risk is more comfortable to handle. How? With proxies. Even the most advanced technology firm tests: first within the team that built the product, then family and friends, then all employees, then a beta release. Each stage turns "we have no idea" into "roughly 60-40".

### Is gut feeling allowed?

The class was split. The professor's answer began with a comparison. Statistics uses a sample to draw conclusions about a population. Gut feeling does the same: the situations you have lived through are a sample of everything that could happen. The gut of someone on their first day and the gut of someone with twenty years in the trade aren't the same instrument, because the samples aren't the same size.

So gut feeling isn't bad. What is bad is stubbornness: "my gut says so, and I am the boss".

| Situation | What to do |
|---|---|
| Gut and evidence agree | The happiest case. Proceed |
| They disagree: check 1 | Is my gut resting on an assumption that isn't true here? Dig until you can put it on the table |
| They disagree: check 2 | Am I looking at the right data? The same data can be cut to tell almost any story |

### Challenger: the right gut, the wrong chart

The Space Shuttle Challenger mission carried a civilian schoolteacher and had been badly delayed. Under that pressure, the launch was set for 28 January 1986, a morning with a freezing forecast.

The booster engineers had argued for months that the seals in the boosters, the O-rings, failed in the cold. Management asked for evidence. The engineers' chart plotted the launches that had suffered O-ring damage against temperature. Damage appeared at low temperatures and at high ones. "This proves nothing." The night before, the engineers again said don't fly. A line that entered management folklore was spoken to the contractor's vice-president: "Take off your engineering hat and put on your management hat." Seventy-three seconds after lift-off the shuttle broke apart, killing all seven on board.

The engineers' gut was right. But they failed the second check: they never found the data that would support it. The chart showed only the failures. Put the successes on it and the story is stark: every clean launch sat at the warm end; below the mid-60s Fahrenheit there were no clean launches at all; and the forecast was about thirty degrees colder than anything attempted.

<div class="callout">
<p class="callout-title">Always ask where the successes were</p>

A chart of only the failures can support any story. Experienced people trust their gut and ask the right question: while everyone else looks at the failures, they ask to see the successes too. When gut and evidence disagree, find the faulty assumption or find the right data. Never just pull rank.

</div>
