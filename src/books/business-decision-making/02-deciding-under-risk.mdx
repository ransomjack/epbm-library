## What makes a decision good?

Asked what makes a decision good, the class first said "it produces a good outcome". Prof. Sharma spent the next hour taking that answer apart.

She began with a second question: how does it feel to be wrong? Disappointing, frustrating, everyone said. Her reply, an idea made famous by the writer Kathryn Schulz: **being wrong feels exactly like being right, because you don't yet know you're wrong.** The feelings the class listed are the feelings of *finding out*.

When you decide, the outcome hasn't happened. Grading the decision later by its outcome is grading it with an answer key you never had.

<div class="callout">
<p class="callout-title">The definition</p>

**A good decision is based on logic, considers all available data and possible alternatives, and is reached through rational analysis of them.** Three tests: were all the alternatives considered, including the ones you didn't like? Were all the possible outcomes listed, not just the comfortable ones? Was all the available information used?

</div>

A decision that passes those tests is good whatever happens next. A careless decision that works out is still a bad decision that got lucky.

### Why judging by outcome breeds mediocrity

Organisations reward good outcomes and punish bad ones. So when something unusual lands on a desk, people look for precedent: if it fails, "we did what worked last time." Everyone plays safe, and the organisation settles in the middle. It doesn't fail, but it never wins big.

**Alcoa** shows the alternative. When Paul O'Neill became chief executive in 1987, his first speech to investors was entirely about worker safety. Analysts told clients to sell. By the time he left in 2000, Alcoa's profits and market value had multiplied. Safety was the door through which every process could be re-examined, because no union could object to removing a hazard. His rule: every accident had to reach him quickly, with a plan to make sure it never happened again. He dismissed a high-performing executive, not for an accident on his site but for concealing one.

<div class="callout">
<p class="callout-title">Audit the process, not the outcome</p>

Teams graded on outcomes learn to hide accidents. Teams graded on process learn to report them, which is the only way the same accident stops recurring. A sound process doesn't win every time, but it wins more often than it loses.

</div>

### Write it down

For decisions that keep circling in your head, Prof. Sharma's habit is two minutes of writing: a few bullet points, not pages. Then turn them into a problem statement (not a solution), ask why the other person might be behaving this way, and list the options you actually have. Sometimes you find there is no other option, and accepting that removes the stress. Sometimes you find better ones.

## Fall in love with the problem

We like people who talk about solutions. The danger: once we have built a solution, we are invested in it. When signs appear that it doesn't fit the problem, we quietly reshape the *problem* until the solution fits. On the slide the problem is solved. On the ground, nothing has changed.

Design thinking's first rule is the cure: in the early steps you aren't allowed to talk about solutions at all. Look at the problem from each stakeholder's side. Why does it happen? What does it do to the people who live with it?

<div class="callout">
<p class="callout-title">The calm test</p>

After you solve a problem, do you get a real period of calm? If yes, you probably fixed the cause. If the next fire follows straight after, you treated a symptom. Daily firefighting means broken processes, broken implementation, a team that isn't aligned, or a root cause left untouched.

</div>

One rule keeps the table honest: **you choose alternatives; only states of nature carry probabilities.**

Gut feeling has a place. It is inference from a lifetime's sample of experience. What isn't acceptable is stubbornness when the evidence disagrees. When your gut and the data conflict, check two things: is an assumption behind your gut feeling false in this case, and are you looking at the right data?

## The Thompson Lumber decision

John Thompson is deciding whether to build a plant to make garden sheds. The market may turn out favourable or unfavourable, and he puts the chances at 50–50.

| Alternative | Favourable market (50%) | Unfavourable market (50%) | EMV |
|---|---|---|---|
| Build a large plant | 200,000 | −180,000 | 10,000 |
| Build a small plant | 100,000 | −20,000 | **40,000** |
| Do nothing | 0 | 0 | 0 |

**EMV = the sum of each payoff × its probability.** The small plant is best.

"But the small plant never actually earns 40,000." True. EMV is the long-run average if you faced this decision many times. Used consistently across many decisions, it wins.

### Expected opportunity loss

The same answer from the other side. **Regret** is the best payoff in a state minus the payoff you got.

| Alternative | Regret if favourable | Regret if unfavourable | Expected regret (EOL) |
|---|---|---|---|
| Large plant | 0 | 180,000 | 90,000 |
| Small plant | 100,000 | 20,000 | **60,000** |
| Do nothing | 200,000 | 0 | 100,000 |

Minimising expected regret always picks the same alternative as maximising EMV.

### Sensitivity analysis: how sure do you need to be?

The 50–50 was a judgement. How wrong can it be before the answer changes? Move the slider.

## What is information worth?

A consultant offers to tell Thompson for certain which market he'll get, for a fee of 65,000. Should he pay?

**With perfect information** he would build the large plant when the market is favourable (200,000) and do nothing when it isn't (0). Before the consultant speaks, each is 50% likely, so the expected value with perfect information is 0.5 × 200,000 + 0.5 × 0 = 100,000.

**EVPI = expected value with perfect information − best EMV without it** = 100,000 − 40,000 = **60,000**.

That is the most any information could be worth. The consultant wants 65,000, so decline. EVPI always equals the expected regret of the best alternative (60,000 above).

<div class="callout">
<p class="callout-title">Information is worth only what it changes</p>

If you would make the same choice whatever the answer, the information is worth nothing to you, however interesting it is.

</div>

## Decision trees

A tree is the same arithmetic drawn as a picture, and it comes into its own when decisions happen in sequence.

- **Squares** are your decisions. **Circles** are chance: nature's branches, each with a probability.
- **Draw it left to right**, in the order things happen in real life.
- **Solve it right to left**: at each circle take the expected value; at each square pick the best branch.

### Is a market survey worth 10,000?

Thompson can commission a survey for 10,000 before deciding. It will come back "favourable" 45% of the time. If it does, the chance the market really is favourable rises to 78%. If it comes back "unfavourable", that chance falls to 27%. Every payoff on the survey side is 10,000 lower, to cover its cost.

With the survey: 0.45 × 106,400 + 0.55 × 2,400 = **49,200**, against 40,000 without it.

A tree doesn't give one answer. It prescribes a **strategy**: commission the survey; if it says favourable, build the large plant; if unfavourable, build the small one.

**EVSI**, the expected value of sample information, is what the survey would be worth if it were free: (49,200 + 10,000) − 40,000 = **19,200**. It costs 10,000, so the net gain is 9,200. The survey delivers 19,200 ÷ 60,000 = 32% of what perfect information would. EVSI can never exceed EVPI.

## Bayes' theorem: where the tree's probabilities come from

A research firm tells you its track record: when markets were favourable, its survey said so 70% of the time; when they were unfavourable, it wrongly said "favourable" 20% of the time. You can't put those numbers straight into the tree. The tree needs the reverse: given what the survey says, how likely is each market?

**Bayes' theorem** does the conversion in three steps: multiply your prior belief by how often that evidence appears in each state; add the results to get how often the evidence appears overall; divide.

| | Favourable market | Unfavourable market | Total |
|---|---|---|---|
| Prior belief | 0.50 | 0.50 | |
| Survey says "favourable" in this state | 0.70 | 0.20 | |
| Joint (prior × evidence) | 0.35 | 0.10 | **0.45** |
| Updated belief (joint ÷ total) | **0.78** | 0.22 | |

Those are the 45% and 78% in the tree.

<div class="callout">
<p class="callout-title">Manager takeaway: turn uncertainty into risk, cheaply</p>

Most business decisions begin without probabilities. Pilots, test markets and staged roll-outs produce estimates you can put on a tree. Before paying for any of them, ask what you would do differently for each possible result. If the answer is "nothing", save the money.
