## Two policymakers, one economy

The government and the central bank can each push demand up or down. They are separate institutions, and nothing guarantees they push the same way.

When the government borrows heavily it draws on the pool of saving, and the interest rate starts to rise. If the central bank then buys bonds with new money, the rate comes back down. Monetary policy is said to **accommodate** fiscal policy.

## Aggregate supply: both sides were right

Economists fought for decades over the shape of the supply curve. They were describing different stretches of time.

<Cards min={220} items={[
  { title: 'Classical: the long run', body: 'Output is fixed by capital, labour and technology. Only prices move.', foot: 'Supply is vertical' },
  { title: 'Keynesian: the very short run', body: 'Prices are stuck. Output moves with whatever is demanded.', foot: 'Supply is flat' },
  { title: 'The real world: the short run', body: 'Higher prices draw out more output.', foot: 'Supply slopes upward', hl: true },
]} />

Why upward? Two reasons. **Wages lag prices**: when prices rise and wages haven't yet, producing more is profitable. And **producers misread the signal**: a firm seeing its own price rise can mistake general inflation for extra demand for its product, and makes more.

Both work only until people catch on. The supply curve is drawn for a given level of **expected prices**. When expectations rise, the whole curve shifts up.

## The Phillips curve

When output grows, firms hire and unemployment falls. Replace "higher output" with "lower unemployment" and "higher prices" with "higher inflation" and the supply curve becomes the **Phillips curve**: low unemployment goes with high inflation.

| | Point A: weak demand | Point B: strong demand |
|---|---|---|
| Output | 7,500 | 8,000 |
| Unemployment | 7% | 4% |
| Inflation | 2% | 6% |

Even at potential, unemployment isn't zero. What remains is the **natural rate**.

In words: **inflation = expected inflation − something proportional to (unemployment − the natural rate) + any supply shock.** Three things move inflation: expectations, how tight the labour market is, and shocks to costs.

### Expectations and the wage-price spiral

High inflation makes people expect high inflation. Expecting it, workers ask for bigger raises. Bigger raises increase costs. Firms raise prices. This is why central banks guard expectations above all, and why the US Federal Reserve raised its rate on 16 September 2026, with inflation still above its target.

### The central banker's dilemma

A jump in oil prices, a broken supply chain, a failed monsoon and higher inflation expectations all raise costs. Each is a **negative supply shock**. A demand shock moves inflation and output together. A supply shock moves them in opposite directions.

### Disinflation: a shorter pain for a longer gain

**Disinflation** is a deliberate reduction in the rate of inflation. In 1980 US inflation was 13.5%. Paul Volcker pushed interest rates close to 20%; unemployment passed 10%; by 1986 inflation was 1.9%.

The cost has a name: the **sacrifice ratio**, the output or employment lost for each point taken off inflation. One historical rule of thumb, quoted in a 2023 US Senate hearing, was 3.6 points of unemployment for every 2 points off inflation. In the event inflation fell to about 3% with unemployment still under 4%. Disinflation hurts less when the central bank is believed and when the budget helps.

## Opening the economy: S − I = X − M

Start from Y = C + I + G + (X − M). Move C and G across: income not consumed by households or the government is the nation's **saving**, S. So:

<div class="callout">
<p class="callout-title">The identity</p>

**S − I = X − M.** A country that exports more than it imports must be saving more than it invests. A country that imports more than it exports is saving less than it invests. There is no third possibility.

</div>

<Cards min={260} items={[
  { title: 'Net lenders', body: 'Save more than they invest, export more than they import, and lend the spare saving abroad.', foot: 'China, Germany, Japan' },
  { title: 'Net borrowers', body: 'Save less than they invest, import more than they export, and borrow other countries’ saving.', foot: 'India, United States' },

If India earns 100 dollars from exports in a month and spends 150 on imports, the gap of 50 is filled by someone else's saving, arriving as shares and bonds or as factories. That is why a tariff does not close a trade deficit: it changes what is imported, not the gap between saving and investment.

## The exchange rate, nominal and real

The **exchange rate** is the price of a currency, set by the demand for a country's goods and assets. If the rupee is weakening, more people want dollars, fewer dollars are on offer, fewer people want rupees, or more rupees are on offer. A bigger number of rupees per dollar is a weaker rupee.

Trade follows a different number. An Indian plant makes a car for Rs. 71 lakh. A US plant makes a similar car for 105,900 dollars. At Rs. 82 to the dollar the Indian car costs about 86,600 dollars, so one American car is worth 1.22 Indian cars.

**Real exchange rate = nominal rate × price abroad ÷ price at home.** When it rises, Indian goods have become cheaper relative to foreign goods: a real depreciation, and exports gain.

Three lessons. A depreciation raises exports only if it is real. Inflation at home is the enemy of the exporter. And the real rate can move while the nominal rate stands still, if a rival country learns to produce more cheaply.

Track your own real exchange rate: the exchange rate, your price, and the price of your nearest foreign rival. An exporter should watch its cost inflation against competitors abroad as closely as it watches the rupee. In services the work moves even faster than in goods, because there is no factory to relocate.
