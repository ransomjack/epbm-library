## Two layers of money: M = m × B

Money is made in two places, so who is in charge of how much there is?

<Cards min={260} items={[
  { title: 'Layer 1: base money (B)', body: 'Currency notes plus the reserves banks keep with the RBI. Created by the RBI, and directly in its control.', foot: 'In the classroom example: Rs. 100' },
  { title: 'Layer 2: total money (M)', body: 'Currency plus all bank deposits: the money people actually spend. Created by banks each time they lend. Nobody controls it directly.', foot: 'Rs. 1,000 at a 10% reserve ratio' },
]} />

**M = m × B.** Total money equals the multiplier times the base. Think of the RBI as a dairy and banks as tea stalls. The dairy decides how many litres of milk go out each morning. The stalls decide how many cups each litre becomes. The town drinks tea, not milk.

| What moves | Works on | Who decides |
|---|---|---|
| Bond purchases and sales, QE and QT | B, the base | The RBI |
| The cash reserve ratio | The ceiling on m | The RBI |
| The repo rate | The price of reserves, and so the appetite to lend | The RBI |
| Willingness to lend | The actual m | Banks |
| Willingness to borrow | The actual m | Firms and families |
| Cash kept in wallets | The actual m | The public |

When the RBI cut the CRR from 4% to 3% during 2025 it created not one new rupee. Banks simply had to lock up less of each deposit, so the chain ran longer. A CRR cut works on m, not on B.

### When the multiplier stalls: India in 2020

The formula assumes that when the RBI adds to the base, banks multiply it. In the first lockdown the RBI bought bonds and filled banks with fresh reserves. The banks did not lend. They were carrying old bad loans, few borrowers looked safe, and a banker who doesn't lend is never questioned later about a loan that failed. The cash was parked straight back at the RBI, which paid interest on its own money.

### New kinds of money

Three groups are trying to put currencies on new digital rails: central banks (a **central bank digital currency**), existing banks (**tokenised deposits**) and private firms (**stablecoins**). A stablecoin issuer holds full reserves and does not lend, so it resembles a money market fund more than a bank. Only the bank creates money by lending.

## Business cycles and the output gap

On a long highway drive your average may be 60 km an hour, yet you are almost never doing exactly 60. An economy is the same: over decades it grows along a trend, and in any year it runs a little faster or slower. **Business cycles** are these swings, in which many activities expand and contract together, recurring but not on a timetable.

**Potential GDP** is what the economy can produce with its capital, labour and technology. The **output gap** is actual output minus potential.

Policy manages the gap, not the trend. The trend comes from three things: more capital, more labour, better technology. Capital grows only by **net investment**, which is investment minus depreciation: invest 100 while 30 wears out and the stock grows by 70.

A new technology raises average productivity but lowers the value of the old tasks it takes over. Jobs return when new tasks appear that people do better than the machine.

## More money: output or prices?

It depends on one thing: whether prices are free to move.

| | Short run | Long run |
|---|---|---|
| Prices are | Sticky: stuck at a level set earlier | Flexible |
| Output is decided by | Demand at those prices | Capacity: capital, labour, technology |
| More money leads to | More output | Higher prices |

Prices are sticky because changing them costs effort (**menu costs**), because frequent changes annoy customers, and because no rival wants to move first.

"Too much money chasing too few goods" is World 1. With output fixed, **M = P × Y** says money can move only prices.

## Why falling prices are worse

Cheaper everything sounds good. It isn't.

- **Buyers wait.** If a car will be cheaper next month, why buy now? Demand falls further.
- **Margins and wages shrink.** Price cuts reach salaries and jobs.
- **Investment dries up.** There is little reason to build a factory whose product keeps getting cheaper.
- **Debts don't shrink.** An EMI is fixed in rupees. If salaries fall, its real burden rises.

Rising prices are a nightmare; falling prices are a night terror. **Involution** is the name for a price war nobody wins: firms cut prices for market share, rivals match, and everyone's margin and wages suffer. China's oversupplied solar, battery and electric-car industries are the current example.

## Why markets don't correct themselves

In a recession, the advice "everyone go and buy a car" would fix it. No sensible household follows it: when jobs are being lost, you hold on to your money. That **precautionary saving** is prudent for one family and damaging when every family does it, because your spending is someone else's income. This is the **paradox of thrift**.

Keynes's conclusion was that someone who isn't frightened has to spend, and that someone is the government. The same failure runs the other way: once inflation rises, expectations keep it high. So every economy has two institutions able to intervene: the central bank, through money and interest rates, and the government, through spending and taxes.

## Fiscal policy and the multiplier

**Budget balance = taxes − spending.** With taxes of 90 and spending of 110 the deficit is 20, which the government borrows.

A rupee the government spends raises income by more than a rupee. It buys chips worth Rs. 100. The seller spends Rs. 80 of that on pots. The potter spends Rs. 64. And so on. The share of each extra rupee that people spend is the **marginal propensity to consume** (MPC).

It has the same shape as the money multiplier. There, the leak at each round is reserves; here it is saving. Both are ceilings that depend on behaviour.

Real multipliers are smaller than the textbook's, because taxes and imports drain each round too. And it matters who gets the money: a family on a tight budget spends almost all of an extra rupee.

## Aggregate demand

**Aggregate demand** is the total demand for the economy's output at each price level. The curve slopes down, and the reason is not that incomes change. With Rs. 100 and a price of Rs. 5 you can buy 20 items; at Rs. 4 the same Rs. 100 buys 25. A lower price level raises the real value of money.
