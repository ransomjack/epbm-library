## Why a manager studies this

Your company's strategy is something you choose. Your customer's budget, the EMI on your loan, the cost of your imported components and the speed at which your salary loses value are chosen for you, by the economy.

Think of a ship's captain. He doesn't control the weather, but he would be a fool to sail without reading it. Your firm is the ship. Macroeconomics is the weather: growth, inflation, interest rates and the rupee. The aim is not to turn managers into economists. It is to make the weather report stop sounding like a foreign language.

| The reason | In plain words | On a manager's desk |
|---|---|---|
| **Analyse the boundary conditions** | The economy sets the limits every business plays within: how fast demand can grow, what money costs, what a rupee buys | A plan to grow 25% a year in an economy growing 6 to 7% means taking business from somebody. Budget for a fight, not a rising tide |
| **Analyse the current condition** | Read where we are now, not where we were when the last plan was written | When to hire, when to lock in a loan, when to hold a price |
| **Read policymakers' signals** | Central bankers and finance ministers speak in a code, and markets move on a single sentence | A rate hike announced today is a costlier loan and a more cautious customer a few months from now |

### Decoding the signals

Each of these is one sentence, and each moved money somewhere in the world.

| The signal | What it says | What a manager does with it |
|---|---|---|
| "The chances of a soft landing are likely to diminish." US Fed chair, September 2022 | A soft landing is bringing inflation down without a recession. Rates would keep rising even at the cost of one | Dollars get costlier and American customers cautious. An Indian IT firm should expect slower deal closures |
| "Stagflation concerns rise." A newspaper headline | Stagnation plus inflation: prices rise while growth and jobs weaken. The cure for one worsens the other | Demand falls while costs rise. Protect margins and stress-test the budget |
| "...reserve losses and imported inflation." RBI Governor, 2022 | When the US raises rates, money leaves emerging markets, the rupee weakens and imports get costlier | An importer hedges. An exporter enjoys a tailwind |
| "No targeted band, the market will decide." RBI Governor, December 2025 | The RBI won't defend any particular level of the rupee. It will only smooth violent moves | Don't build a budget on the belief that the RBI will hold a number for you |

Two institutions stand behind all these sentences. The government decides taxes and public spending: **fiscal policy**. The central bank (the RBI in India, the Federal Reserve in the US) decides how much money there is and what it costs: **monetary policy**. The professor's label for the pair was "two benevolent dictators": each has enormous power and each believes it acts for the public good. When a president and a central bank chair quarrel in public, the quarrel is over the central bank's independence, and rates and currencies swing more.

The first two and a half sessions stay inside the domestic economy. The rest open the borders. By the end, each sentence above should read like plain English.

## GDP: sizing an economy

Imagine an orchard that grows apples and oranges and runs a juice stall at the gate. How big was the business this year? You can't add 500 apples to 300 oranges to 2,000 glasses of juice. The only common unit is money: value everything at the price it sold for and add it up. Do the same for every farm, factory, shop, clinic and office in the country, and the total is **gross domestic product**: the market value of the final goods and services produced in a country during a given period. Change any one phrase and you are measuring something else.

| Phrase | What it means | Example |
|---|---|---|
| **Market value** | Everything is converted into rupees at the price it sold for. Whatever is never sold is left out | A cook paid Rs. 8,000 a month is in GDP. The same meals cooked by a family member are not |
| **Final** | Only what reaches its end user. Intermediate goods are skipped: their value is already inside the final price | Tyres sold to a car maker: intermediate. A tyre you buy to replace a worn one: final |
| **Goods and services** | Things you can touch and things you can't | A phone, and the mobile plan that runs on it. Services are about half of what India produces |
| **Produced within a country** | Geography, not nationality | Samsung's phone plant in Noida counts in India's GDP. An Infosys office in Texas counts in America's |
| **In a given period** | Only what was produced in that quarter or year | A flat built in 2015 and resold today adds nothing, except the broker's fee |

### More awkward cases from class

| The case | In India's GDP? | Why |
|---|---|---|
| The case that comes with your new spectacles | Not separately | The optician built its cost into your bill |
| India imports a phone and pays in rupees | No | GDP asks where a thing was produced, not which currency paid for it |
| A firm imports parts worth Rs. 100, assembles them here and exports the product for Rs. 120 | Rs. 20 | Only the value added on Indian soil |
| Tata Motors owns Jaguar Land Rover, which builds its cars in the UK | No | The cars are British GDP. The dividends sent home are part of India's national income, a different measure |
| A cash transfer of Rs. 10,000 into each beneficiary's account | No | A transfer payment. It counts only when the recipient spends it on something newly produced |
| A cab ride booked on an app | Yes, the whole fare | A final service: the driver's share and the platform's commission together |
| A company that is making losses | Yes | GDP measures production, not profit. Sales of Rs. 100 crore with Rs. 60 crore of bought-in inputs add Rs. 40 crore of value |
| Aliens from Mars open a factory in Pune | Yes | Who owns the factory doesn't matter. Where the production happens does |

Three fine points. A machine bought by a factory is a final good: it is a capital good, counted in full as investment. **Gross national income** is GDP plus what Indians earn abroad minus what foreigners earn here. And a few unsold things are estimated and included, such as the food a farmer grows and eats, and the rent an owner would have paid on the house he lives in.

<div class="callout trap">
<p class="callout-title">GDP is not welfare, wealth or profit</p>

Two economists out walking each pay the other Rs. 100 to do something thoroughly unpleasant. Neither is a rupee richer, and GDP has risen by Rs. 200. GDP counts priced production during a period. It is a flow, like a salary, not a stock like a bank balance. It counts whatever is paid for, useful or not: a traffic jam that burns fuel raises GDP; a parent's unpaid care adds nothing.

</div>

## Who buys the output: Y = C + I + G + NX

Everything produced is bought by someone. Stand at the nation's cash counter and there are only four kinds of buyer.

  { k: 'I', title: 'Investment', body: 'Spending today that helps produce tomorrow: machines, software, new homes, and stock not yet sold.' },
  { k: 'G', title: 'Government purchases', body: 'What governments buy or produce: teachers’ salaries, defence equipment. Not pensions or subsidies.' },
  { k: 'NX', title: 'Net exports', body: 'Exports minus imports: IT services and medicines sold abroad, less oil and electronics bought.' },
]} />

The equation is an **identity**: true by definition, the way assets equal liabilities plus equity.

**Why are imports subtracted?** Not because imports are bad. An imported phone is already inside C. It wasn't made in India, so it is taken out again.

<div class="callout trap">
<p class="callout-title">"Investment" here is not what your broker means</p>

Buying shares, bonds or a resale flat is financial investment: one asset swapped for another. In macroeconomics, investment means creating new productive capacity. Your SIP becomes investment only when a company uses the money to build something.

</div>

| Part of investment | What it is | Example |
|---|---|---|
| Business fixed capital | Plant, machinery, buildings, software and R&D used for years | A new assembly line; a data centre |
| Residential investment | Newly built homes | A new apartment tower. A resale flat is not new production |
| Change in inventories | Goods produced this year but not yet sold | Diwali stock built in September. When it sells, inventories fall and consumption rises by the same amount |

G is only about 12% because it covers day-to-day purchases. The roads and railways the government builds are counted inside I, and its pensions and subsidies are transfers.

India is consumption-driven: private consumption has stayed at 55% to 60% of GDP for two decades. China is the mirror image: consumption under 40%, investment over 40%. A country that produces far more than its households consume has to sell the rest abroad, which is why China runs a trade surplus and India usually a deficit.

Two worries came up in class. India's investment share has slipped from its peak of about 40% around 2007 to 2011, with private companies slow to build new capacity. And household saving, the pool that funds investment, has been sliding as families borrow more. An economy can't keep investing more than it saves without borrowing from abroad.

## The value-added road to the same number

Instead of standing at the final counter, walk down the supply chain and count only what each stop added. The samosa seller outside your office buys potatoes, flour, oil and gas worth Rs. 6 and sells the samosa for Rs. 15. What he added is Rs. 9, which pays for his labour, his rent and his profit. **Value added** is a firm's sales minus what it bought from other firms. Wages are not subtracted: the work of a firm's own people is exactly the value it adds.

| Stage | Sells for | Bought-in inputs | Value added |
|---|---|---|---|
| Forestry company: cuts the logs | 1,000 | 0 | 1,000 |
| Furniture maker | 2,500 | 1,000 | 1,500 |
| Retailer, sells to the consumer | 3,000 | 2,500 | 500 |
| **Total** | **6,500** | **3,500** | **3,000** |

Rs. 6,500 changed hands. Only Rs. 3,000 of new value was created, and Rs. 3,000 is exactly what the final customer paid. Turnover is not GDP.

**Now you try.** A farmer sells wheat to a miller for 1 dollar. The miller sells flour to a baker for 3. The baker sells a loaf for 6. Value added is 1, then 2, then 3: GDP is 6, the price of the loaf.

Adding value added sector by sector gives **gross value added** (GVA). Roughly, services produce over half of India's output, industry about a quarter and agriculture about 17%. The uncomfortable fact: agriculture produces about a sixth of the output but employs roughly 45% of the workers, so value added per worker on the farm is a fraction of what it is elsewhere. The normal route out is for workers to move into factories, which is why industry's stalled share is the real worry.

GDP = GVA + taxes on products − subsidies on products. GVA is what producers received; GDP is what buyers paid. In April to June 2026 real GVA grew 8.2% and real GDP 7.8%.

For a product company that assembles imported kits, value added may be 15 paise in the rupee, which is why "assembled in India" and "made in India" are different claims. Outsourcing doesn't create value; it moves it to someone else's books.

There is a third road too. Every rupee of value added lands in someone's pocket as wages, rent, interest or profit, so adding up all incomes gives the same number. A biscuit factory that adds Rs. 100 of value might pay Rs. 60 in wages, Rs. 10 in rent, Rs. 10 in interest and keep Rs. 20 as profit. That is why GDP per person is used as a rough measure of the average income. India's total GDP is among the largest in the world and its GDP per person is far down the list. One answers how big the market is; the other, how rich the customer is.

## Nominal versus real

GDP is prices multiplied by quantities, so it can rise for two reasons: more was produced, or everything became costlier.

An economy grows five apples. Last year an apple cost Rs. 20: GDP of Rs. 100. This year it grows five apples at Rs. 40: GDP of Rs. 200. **Nominal GDP** doubled. **Real GDP**, which values this year's output at base-year prices, hasn't moved. Not one extra apple was grown.

<div class="callout">
<p class="callout-title">The rule of thumb</p>

Nominal growth = real growth + inflation, approximately. In April to June 2026 India's nominal GDP grew 10.3% and real GDP 7.8%. The gap of about 2.5 points is prices. When a headline says "the economy grew 7.8%", it means real GDP.

</div>

| The question on your desk | Use | Why |
|---|---|---|
| Next year's revenue budget in rupees | Nominal | Invoices are in current rupees |
| Is my market growing, or only its prices? | Real | Strip out prices to see volumes |
| Was my salary hike a raise? | Compare with inflation | 6% with prices up 5% is a 1% raise |
| Are we better off than ten years ago? | Real, per person | Only quantities per head measure living standards |

**The base year.** Real GDP needs a reference year whose prices are frozen. The statistics ministry chooses it and replaces it about once a decade: in February 2026 India moved to 2022-23 from 2011-12. Can a government pick a convenient year? Far less than people imagine. It has to be a normal year, with no drought or pandemic, for which large fresh surveys exist, and the method follows an international rulebook.

**Decoding "India will grow at 10 per cent".** Targets such as a 5 trillion dollar economy, and the growth assumed in every Union Budget, are nominal: typically 6% to 6.5% real growth plus about 4% of price rise. Real growth of 6% to 7% is India's normal cruising speed; a quarter at 7.8% is above trend, not the new floor.

**Convergence.** Why does the US celebrate 2% growth while India is disappointed with 6%? A poorer economy can copy technology that already exists; a rich one must invent the next thing. But watch the base: 10% growth on an income of Rs. 60,000 adds Rs. 6,000, while 6% on Rs. 2,80,000 adds Rs. 16,800. The poorer state grows faster and the gap in rupees still widens for years.

Divide nominal GDP by real GDP and multiply by 100 and you get the **GDP deflator**: the average price of everything the country produces. In the apple economy it is 200.

## Measuring inflation

Inflation is a sustained rise in the general level of prices. India publishes three measures.

| Measure | What it tracks | Used for |
|---|---|---|
| **Consumer Price Index (CPI)** | A fixed basket bought by a typical household, imports included | The cost of living. The RBI is asked to keep it near 4%, within 2% to 6% |
| **Wholesale Price Index (WPI)** | What producers and wholesalers receive. Goods only | An early warning: retail prices tend to follow |
| **GDP deflator** | Everything produced at home, with no fixed basket | Turning nominal GDP into real GDP |

The CPI is built in three steps: survey households to fix the basket, price that basket every month, and compare its cost with the base period. The classroom basket is 20 pizzas and 10 CDs.

A **weight** is an item's share of total spending. Pizza takes Rs. 200 of the Rs. 350 basket, so a 10% rise in pizza lifts the index by 5.7%, while a 10% rise in CDs lifts it by only 4.3%. In India's CPI food carries the largest weight, which is why a bad tomato season moves the national number.

<div class="callout trap">
<p class="callout-title">Falling inflation is not falling prices</p>

If inflation drops from 8.1% to 2.5%, prices are still rising, only more slowly. That is **disinflation**. Prices actually falling is **deflation**.

</div>

| | CPI | GDP deflator |
|---|---|---|
| Machines and other capital goods | Left out: households don't buy them | Included: they are produced here |
| Imported consumer goods | Included | Left out: not produced in India |
| The basket | Fixed until the base year is revised | Changes every year with what the country produces |

Your customers feel CPI. Your purchase department feels something closer to WPI: steel, chemicals, fuel, packaging. When WPI runs ahead of CPI, a product company's costs are rising faster than the prices consumers will accept. Writing a published index into long contracts protects you without a negotiation every year.

## What India buys: the new basket

A CPI basket is a photograph of how a country spends. In early 2026 India replaced its 2012 photograph with a 2024 one (base year 2024), using the household consumption survey of 2023-24 and the United Nations classification, which makes India's CPI comparable with others.

- **Food's share has fallen sharply**: from 45.9 to 40.1 overall, and from 54.2 to 44.8 in rural India. Families aren't eating less. Incomes grew, and the extra rupees went elsewhere.
- **The extra went to services**: health, education, transport, communication and personal care.
- **Rural housing appears for the first time.** The old series compiled no rural housing index. Many places that are rural on paper are urban in every way that matters to a household budget.
- **Transport is the biggest riser** in the finer breakdown (6.4 to 8.8). Rural India has caught up with urban India on mobility.

### Engel's law

Give a child Rs. 10 and all of it goes on toffees. Give the same child Rs. 100 and most of the extra goes on other things. Stomachs have limits; wants do not. As income rises, the share spent on food falls.

| Developing economies | Food share | Advanced economies | Food share |
|---|---|---|---|
| Indonesia | 53.0% | Japan | 14.7% |
| India | 48.8% | Germany | 11.5% |
| China | 36.7% | United Kingdom | 8.8% |
| Malaysia | 28.0% | USA | 5.7% |

A shrinking share is not a shrinking market. Food spending in rupees still rises; it just grows more slowly than the customer's income. The new basket is a growth map: two-wheelers and fuel, personal care, appliances and phones are where the rural household's additional rupee is going.

## Headline and core inflation

**Headline inflation** is the price change of the whole basket. **Core inflation** leaves out food and fuel, which jump around for reasons unrelated to the state of the economy.

| Why food prices swing | Why fuel prices swing |
|---|---|
| The monsoon: a weak one cuts the crop | Imported crude: the price is set by world markets and wars |
| The cobweb pattern: costly tomatoes this year mean everyone plants them, then a glut, then a spike again | The rupee: oil is paid for in dollars |
| Vegetables can't be stored | |

Between mid-2023 and mid-2024 food inflation ran near 8% while core inflation slid from about 5% to almost 3%. Anyone looking only at the headline thought prices were under pressure everywhere. The pressure was in the kitchen.

<div class="callout">
<p class="callout-title">Manager takeaway</p>

GDP is the size of the pie your industry is cut from: a firm can outgrow it for years only by taking share or entering new categories, and its board should know which the plan assumes. Split every growth number into price and volume before praising anyone. A contract renewed at the same rupee price is a price cut in real terms.
